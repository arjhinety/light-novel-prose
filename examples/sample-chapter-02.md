Chapter 2: Do You Like the Go-Home Club? 2

"Hey, Kuze. You and Rin, you know each other?"

First period hadn't started yet, and Haruto Amamiya was standing beside Kuze's desk.

It was the first time he had ever come over on his own. Kuze noted the date, the way you'd note an earthquake.

"Not really."

"Not really? She was sitting on your desk."

"She was."

"So you know each other."

"We talked yesterday. For about two minutes."

"Huh."

Amamiya scratched the back of his head. He was smiling, the same easy smile he wore for everyone, but his eyes kept drifting across the room to where Rin sat with her chin on her fist, glaring at the blackboard as if it had insulted her family.

"She's kind of a handful, you know? Rin. She says stuff she doesn't mean. So if she said something rude, don't take it personally, okay? That's just how she is."

"...Okay."

"Cool, cool. Just wanted to say."

He knocked twice on Kuze's desk, a friendly little rap with his knuckles, and wandered back to his seat, where Hina immediately grabbed his sleeve and started telling him something with her hands.

Kuze looked at the spot on the desk where the knuckles had landed.

*She's kind of a handful. That's just how she is.*

It was a nice thing to say. It was the kind of thing a good friend says, stepping in to smooth things over before there's even anything to smooth. If you'd heard it on its own, you'd have thought: *What a considerate guy.*

But Kuze kept snagging on one small word.

*She.*

Amamiya hadn't asked what she wanted, or whether everything was all right. He'd come to explain Rin to Kuze, as though she were a dog with a habit of barking at strangers, and he were the owner apologizing over the fence.

*Don't take it personally. That's just how she is.*

Was it kindness or ownership? Was there a difference, from the outside?

Kuze didn't know. What he did know was that nobody explains a person they don't consider, on some level, theirs.

The sediment settled a little thicker.

Then the bell rang, and the math teacher came in, and Kuze spent fifty minutes not learning anything about quadratic functions.

1

"Mina! Mina, Mina, Minaaa!"

At the ten-minute break after second period, a large, damp-haired boy filled the doorway of 2-3 and waved both arms as if he were landing a plane.

Kuze sighed and stood up.

Daichi Enomoto had been his friend since they were five, when they'd been placed next to each other at the kindergarten's pool day because their names both had a *K* in them somewhere and the teacher was tired. Daichi had cried because the water was cold. Kuze had not cried, because he'd refused to get in the water. They'd been inseparable ever since.

Now Daichi was a head taller than him and the swim club's best freestyler. He smelled permanently of chlorine, and he had spent twelve years getting in the water for both of them.

"You don't have to say it four times."

"I have to say it four times. You never come out on the first one."

"That's not true."

"Mina. It's scientifically true. I have data."

Daichi slung an arm around Kuze's neck and dragged him into the hallway, which was loud with the usual break-time traffic: first-years sprinting to the vending machines, third-years walking to them slowly, and someone's club poster peeling off the wall.

"Okay. Okay. So. I have heard," Daichi said, in the hushed voice of a man delivering state secrets, "a rumor."

"I'm sure you have."

"A rumor that a certain *Rin Tachibana* of the kendo club, famous for breaking a first-year's bamboo sword with a single hit, sat on a certain *someone's* desk this morning."

"It's been two hours."

"The swim club group chat is very efficient."

"You're in a different class."

"The swim club group chat," Daichi said solemnly, "has members in every class. We're everywhere. We're like water."

"That's the worst thing you've ever said."

"Three points?"

"Two."

"*Two?!* That was a good one! That was a water joke from the *swim club!*"

"That's why it's two."

Daichi clutched his chest as if he'd been shot. A passing first-year girl gave him a wide berth.

Then he dropped the act, just like that, the way he always did. His voice came down half a step.

"So what's the deal? She's not gonna hurt you, right?"

"She wants to talk. At lunch. On the roof."

"The *roof?*"

Daichi's eyebrows went up and stayed there.

"Mina. The roof is where people confess. Or fight. Those are the two things that happen on a roof. It's in every manga."

"Neither of those is going to happen."

"How do you know?"

"Because she's in love with Amamiya."

He'd said it without thinking. It came out flat and simple, like the forecast, and he only heard it afterwards: the matter-of-factness, and the small, sour note underneath.

Daichi heard it too. He always did.

"...Huh," he said.

"What?"

"Nothing." Daichi looked at him a second longer than necessary. Then he grinned, and the moment passed, or pretended to. "Well, if it's a duel, I'll carry your body home. I'm very strong. Swimming shoulders."

"I'm touched."

"If it's a confession, I'm going to need *all* the details."

"It's not a confession."

"Details, Mina. Every one."

The warning bell rang. Daichi ruffled Kuze's hair as though he were still five, dodged the swat that followed, and jogged off toward 2-5, leaving a faint smell of chlorine behind him.

Kuze fixed his hair and went back inside.

*The roof is where people confess. Or fight.*

He had a feeling it would be neither, and a much stronger feeling that it would somehow be worse.

2

Seiryou's roof was technically off-limits.

This meant that the door at the top of the east stairwell had a sign on it that said *STUDENTS MAY NOT ENTER*, and a lock that had been broken since before Kuze was born, and that every student in the school knew about both.

He pushed it open. The hinges shrieked.

Creeeak.

Wind hit him in the face. It was a clean, cool April wind with the smell of the cherry trees below in it, the last few petals of the season blowing over the chain-link fence like confetti somebody had forgotten to stop throwing. Beyond the fence the town spread out gray and green, the river glittering, the train line drawing a silver thread along the bottom of the hills.

Rin was sitting against the water tank with her back straight and her knees together, and there were two lunch boxes in her lap.

Two.

"You're late."

"It's been four minutes since the bell."

"Three minutes too many."

"The stairs are long."

"Then walk faster."

She patted the concrete beside her once, hard, like a judge with a gavel.

Kuze sat. He left a precise meter between them. Rin looked at the meter, then at him, and seemed to approve.

For a while, neither of them said anything. The wind filled the silence. Somewhere down below, a volleyball smacked against a gym wall, and someone shrieked with laughter.

"..."

"..."

"So," Kuze said. "What's--"

"Eat this."

She shoved one of the lunch boxes at his chest.

It was the navy-blue cloth. The same careful bow, now slightly crumpled, as though it had been held too tightly on the way up the stairs.

Kuze looked at it. Then he looked at her.

"This is Amamiya's."

"It's *not* Haruto's! It's just... it's a *test* one!"

"A test one."

"A prototype!"

Her face had gone red again, starting at the ears and spreading inward like ink in water.

"I... I've never made one before. Okay? And I'm not going to give Haruto something that tastes like garbage. So somebody has to taste it first. And you already know. So it's efficient."

"So I'm a taste tester."

"You're a *sacrifice.*"

"That's much worse."

"Just eat it!"

He untied the cloth.

The lunch box was a two-tier lacquered one, black on the outside, red inside. It looked old and good, the kind that got handed down. Inside, arranged with obvious, painstaking effort, were rice with a pickled plum pressed into the center, three pieces of karaage, a few blanched green beans, a small mound of potato salad, and a rolled omelette cut into four slices.

The omelette was the color of a traffic cone at dusk. The outside was very, very brown.

"...Itadakimasu."

He picked up a slice of the omelette. Rin watched him the way a cat watches a bird it hasn't decided whether to kill.

He put it in his mouth.

It was sweet. It was aggressively sweet. It was sweet like a threat, as though someone had heard that tamagoyaki could be sweet and taken it as a personal challenge. Beneath the sweetness was a thin, bitter crust where the pan had been too hot, and beneath *that*, somewhere, was the ghost of egg.

He chewed.

"..."

"Well?"

"..."

"*Well?!*"

He swallowed.

"How much sugar did you put in?"

"...The recipe said one spoon."

"How big was the spoon?"

"It was a *spoon!* A normal spoon!"

"A teaspoon or a tablespoon?"

"..."

Rin looked at the green beans.

"There's a difference?"

Kuze put the chopsticks down and took a breath of the clean April air, which tasted, at the moment, faintly of burnt sugar.

"Tachibana. A tablespoon is three teaspoons."

"*Three?!*"

"And your heat was too high. The outside burned before the inside set, so you rolled it thicker to hide it, which made the outside cook even longer."

"H-How do you know all that?!"

"My mother works late. I make my own lunch."

"...You do?"

"Since middle school."

She stared at him as if he'd just revealed he could fly.

"The karaage," he added, because he was being thorough, "is good."

"...Really?"

"The coating's a little thick, but the marinade's right. Ginger and garlic, soy sauce. You didn't rush it."

"I let it sit overnight."

"It shows."

Something happened to Rin's face then. It wasn't a smile. She seemed to be holding a smile underneath her face and pressing down on it with both hands, and it was pushing back.

"...Tch. Of course it's good. I'm not an idiot."

"You're not. You just can't tell spoons apart."

"*Shut up!*"

She hit him on the arm. It was not a hard hit. For someone who could reportedly break bamboo, it was almost gentle.

Kuze ate the rest of the karaage. He ate the green beans and the rice and the potato salad, which had too much mayonnaise but was otherwise sound. He ate the other three slices of the terrible omelette too, one after another, and he didn't know why, only that leaving them felt like breaking something.

Rin watched him the whole time with her arms crossed, her own lunch box untouched in her lap.

"You don't have to eat all of it," she said finally. Her voice was quieter.

"I'm hungry."

"You have your own lunch. I can see it in your bag."

"I'll eat it later."

"..."

"..."

She looked away, toward the fence, where the last of the petals were blowing out over the town.

"...Thanks," she said. "For eating it."

It was so quiet the wind nearly took it.

3

"So." Kuze wrapped the empty lunch box back in its cloth and tied the bow, better than it had been. "Why me? Really."

"I told you. Because you already knew."

"You could have practiced on your mother. Or a friend."

"..."

Rin's fingers tightened on her own lunch box.

"My mom's in Osaka for work. Until summer."

"And friends?"

"..."

She didn't answer, and the not-answering was an answer.

Kuze had suspected it. He'd seen her in the hallways. The crowd didn't only part for her. It closed again behind her, fast, the way water closes behind a stone. Girls who talked to Hina for twenty minutes at a time went quiet when Rin passed. He'd assumed she liked it that way.

He was starting to suspect that he'd assumed a lot of things.

"The girls in my class think I'm scary," Rin said, to the fence. "And the girls in the kendo club think I'm stuck up, because I'm better than the third-years and I don't pretend I'm not. And the guys..." She made a face. "The guys look at me like I'm a *poster*."

"A poster."

"Like I'm something on a wall. Something to *look at*." She clicked her tongue. "It's gross."

"Amamiya doesn't?"

It came out before he could stop it. Rin's head turned sharply toward him.

"Haruto's different."

"How?"

He shouldn't have asked. He knew it as soon as he did. It was the question he'd been carrying for nine days, and it had slipped out in the wrong place, to the wrong person, in a voice that was a little too sharp.

But Rin didn't hit him.

She looked down at her lunch box, the untouched one, and for a long moment she didn't say anything at all.

"In middle school," she said slowly, "I was in the kendo club too. There was this senpai. A third-year. He was the captain. He kept... he kept saying things. About how girls shouldn't be stronger than boys. About how I should lose on purpose so the boys wouldn't feel bad. And then he started doing it in practice. Hitting too hard, when I didn't have my guard up. Saying it was 'training.'"

"..."

"Nobody said anything. The advisor didn't see. The other girls were scared of him. I didn't want to cry in front of him, so I just... kept getting hit."

The wind moved her bob across her cheek. She didn't brush it away.

"Then one day, Haruto walked into the dojo. He wasn't even in the club. He'd come to return a textbook to somebody. And he saw the senpai hit me after the call, and he walked right onto the floor in his socks, which is super against the rules, and said, 'Hey, that's not fair. That wasn't a point.'"

She laughed, a short, surprised huff through her nose.

"He didn't even know what a point *was*. He just knew it wasn't fair."

"..."

"He got in so much trouble. The senpai shoved him. Haruto shoved him back. They both got called to the staff room. But after that, the advisor started watching, and the senpai stopped."

She finally looked at Kuze. Her gray eyes were very steady.

"So yeah. He's different."

"..."

Kuze didn't have anything to say to that.

He had spent nine days building a theory. He had built it carefully and logically, one observation at a time, like stacking stones: the drinks, the folder, *some guy from class*, the explanation over the fence. And every stone had been real. He hadn't made any of them up.

But he'd been looking at the wall, and he hadn't once asked what was on the other side of it.

*He walked onto the floor in his socks.*

Kuze tried to imagine himself doing it. Walking into a room full of strangers and a senpai twice his size, for a girl he didn't know, over a rule he didn't understand.

He couldn't. He would have seen it, counted it, and put it in the ledger. He would have thought about it very carefully for a very long time.

And he wouldn't have walked onto the floor.

The sediment in his chest didn't go away. It never did. But something stirred it, a current from somewhere underneath, and for the first time, it didn't settle back in quite the same shape.

"...I see," he said.

"That's it? 'I see'?"

"What else should I say?"

"I don't know! Something! I just told you a whole *thing!*"

"It was a good thing. Thank you for telling me."

Rin opened her mouth, closed it, and turned bright red again.

"D-Don't say it all *sincerely* like that! It's weird!"

"You told me to say something."

"Not *that!*"

She punched his arm. Again, not very hard.

4

"Okay," Rin said after a while, all business. "So here's the deal."

She'd finally opened her own lunch box, which contained the same food, only neater, and was eating the karaage with the brisk efficiency of a soldier.

"You taste-test. Every day, until I get it right. Then I give Haruto a real one."

"Every day?"

"Until I get it *right.*"

"What if you never get it right?"

"Then every day forever."

"That seems like a bad deal for me."

"It's a *great* deal for you. You get free lunch."

"I get free experimental lunch."

"Free is free!"

Kuze considered it.

The rational answer was no. Every day on the roof with Rin Tachibana was every day of whispers in 2-3. It was more of Amamiya's eyes on the back of his neck, more Kujou watching from across the room with that faint, unreadable smile, and more people noticing him. He had spent a year perfecting the art of not being noticed.

"...What do I get? Really."

"I just *said*. Lunch."

"Lunch is payment for tasting. What do I get for keeping it a secret?"

Rin's chopsticks stopped halfway to her mouth.

"You said you wouldn't tell. Yesterday. You said it wasn't yours to tell."

"I did."

"So you *shouldn't* get anything. You already promised."

"I did."

"Then why are you asking?!"

He didn't know, exactly. It was the ledger. It was a habit so old he no longer noticed it running, like breathing. Every exchange had two sides, and every favor was a debt. He didn't want anything from her. He just wanted to know what the terms were.

"...Forget it," he said. "Lunch is fine."

Rin was looking at him strangely.

"You're weird, you know that?"

"I've heard."

"Like, you keep score. Of everything. I can see you doing it." She pointed her chopsticks at his face. "Right there. You're doing it right now."

"I'm not."

"You *are.* Your eyes go all..." She squinted, badly, in what he assumed was supposed to be an impression of him. "Like a cashier."

"I don't look like a cashier."

"You look *exactly* like a cashier."

"..."

He didn't say anything, because the terrible thing was that she was right.

And the second terrible thing was that she'd noticed. Nobody noticed. Daichi noticed, but Daichi had had twelve years. Rin had needed two days and one terrible omelette.

"...I don't like owing people," he said finally. "Or being owed. It's easier when things are even."

Rin considered this, chewing.

"Me neither," she said. "That's why I'm making you lunch. So I don't owe you for keeping quiet."

"..."

"What?"

"Nothing."

*She keeps a ledger too.*

He looked at her properly, maybe for the first time. The straight back and the crossed arms. The glare that went up like a shield the instant anyone got close. A girl who sat alone against a water tank with two lunch boxes, one of them practice, because she had nobody to practice on.

She wasn't scary. She was just careful. She'd figured out, earlier than most people, that the world keeps accounts, and she was determined never to end up on the wrong side of them.

He knew that feeling. He'd just never seen it from the outside.

"Deal," he said.

"Huh?"

"Taste-testing. Every day until you get it right."

Rin blinked. Then she stuck out her hand, very formally, like they were signing a treaty.

"Deal."

He shook it. Her grip was hard, calloused across the palm from the shinai.

"And the tablespoon thing," she added, not letting go. "You're going to *show* me. Tomorrow. With a real spoon."

"You want me to bring a spoon to school?"

"Bring *two.* So I can see the difference."

"..."

"What?! It's efficient!"

The bell rang, far below them, faint and cheerful through the concrete.

Ding-dong-dang-dong.

Rin let go of his hand as if it had burned her, snatched up both lunch boxes, and fled for the stairwell without another word. The door shrieked open and banged shut.

Kuze sat alone by the water tank for a moment longer.

His own lunch was still in his bag, uneaten. His stomach was full of terrible omelette.

He didn't feel as bad about it as he'd expected to.

5

He took the west stairs down, to avoid arriving at the classroom thirty seconds after Rin.

It was a good plan. It was a rational plan. It failed on the third-floor landing, where Sayo Kujou was standing by the window with a book open in one hand, as if she'd simply happened to choose that exact spot to read in.

"Good afternoon, Kuze-kun."

"...Kujou."

"My. You know my name. I'm flattered."

She closed the book without marking the page.

"You were on the roof, weren't you?"

"Was I?"

"You have a cherry petal in your hair."

Kuze reached up. There was, in fact, a cherry petal in his hair. He removed it. Kujou watched him do it with the mild pleasure of someone watching a cat fall off a sofa.

"Rin-san has been very... *energetic* this week," she said. "Taking cooking videos to kendo practice. Asking the home economics teacher about the difference between measuring spoons." A small, graceful tilt of the head. "I wondered who she was practicing for. And now I wonder who she's practicing *on*."

"..."

Kuze said nothing. It was the only safe thing to say.

*How does she know about the spoons?*

Rin had only found out about spoons twenty minutes ago.

Unless she hadn't. Unless she'd asked the home economics teacher last week, gotten an answer, and still come to the roof with the wrong omelette, and today's lesson hadn't been the first time.

Or unless Kujou was guessing, and she'd just watched him react to the guess.

He kept his face very still.

Kujou smiled.

"You're interesting, Kuze-kun. You notice things. I noticed you noticing, at the vending machine yesterday." She tucked the book under her arm. "Haruto didn't buy you a drink. You counted the cans."

"..."

The silt in his chest went cold.

"Don't worry. I don't think anyone else saw. Everyone else was looking at Haruto." She began walking down the stairs, unhurried, her long black hair swaying behind her like a curtain. "That's the thing about Haruto. Everyone always is."

She paused on the landing below and looked back up at him.

"Everyone except you."

Then she was gone, and her footsteps faded down the stairwell, soft and even as a metronome.

Kuze stood alone on the landing.

*She saw me counting.*

He'd been so certain he was the only one in the room who was watching. He had sat in the audience for a year, and it had never once occurred to him to check whether anyone was sitting behind him.

"...What *is* she?"

The stairwell didn't answer.

6

The rest of the afternoon passed the way afternoons do when something has happened at lunch: slowly, loudly, and with eyes.

Rin didn't look at him once. She was so careful not to look at him that it was worse than looking. Every time Kuze glanced up, she was staring fiercely at something else: the board, the window, her pencil, the back of Amamiya's head. Once, when their eyes accidentally met during roll call, she turned so red that the girl in front of her asked if she had a fever.

Amamiya looked at him three times during classical Japanese. Kuze counted.

He hated that he counted.

Kujou didn't look at him at all. That was the most unsettling of the three.

It was worse, somehow, than being stared at. When someone stares, you at least know where they are. Kujou sat by the window with her book and her porcelain calm, turning pages at perfectly even intervals, never once lifting her eyes, and Kuze could feel her attention anyway. It lay on the room like a thin layer of frost, invisible until you touched it.

*Everyone except you.*

What had she meant? That he was the only one who didn't watch Amamiya? That was false. He watched Amamiya constantly, and that was the whole problem. Or had she meant he was the only one who watched him *wrong*, from the outside, counting instead of admiring?

Or had she been telling him, very politely, that she watched too?

He wrote *Heike* in his notebook four times without meaning to. Then he crossed all four out.

After school, Kuze exercised his rights as a member of the go-home club and tried to go home.

He got as far as the shoe lockers.

"Kuze! Hey, Kuze, hold up a sec."

Amamiya jogged up the corridor alone. There were no girls at his elbows and no chatter trailing behind him. It was strange to see him like that. He looked smaller, the way a stage looks smaller when the lights come up after the show.

"You heading home?"

"Go-home club."

"Ha! Right, right." Amamiya leaned against the lockers beside him, arms folded, easy. "So, uh. Some of us are going to karaoke on Saturday. Club thing, kinda, but not really. Just hanging out. You should come."

Kuze paused with one shoe half off.

"...Me?"

"Yeah! You. We're classmates, right? It's weird that we've been in the same class and never hung out. That's no good. Everybody should get along, you know?"

He said it as though it were a law of physics. *Everybody should get along.* It was a nice sentence and a generous one. It was the kind of sentence that won class-rep elections.

And Kuze couldn't stop turning it over.

*Why now?*

For nine days, Kuze had sat four rows behind Haruto Amamiya and been invisible. He'd been "some guy from class." He'd been the fifth can nobody bought. And today, on the same day Rin Tachibana had sat on his desk, Amamiya had come over twice: once to explain her, and now to invite him somewhere she would be.

*Everybody should get along.*

Or: *I want to see what you two are like in front of me.*

Or: *It's easier to keep an eye on something when it's sitting next to you.*

Or, and this was the one that made the sediment shift uncomfortably, maybe Amamiya really was just a nice guy who'd noticed a classmate existed. Maybe he'd been planning to invite Kuze all along and Rin was a coincidence, and the whole theory was nothing more than a bitter boy in the back row assembling a villain out of spare parts.

He thought of the dojo, and socks on the wooden floor. *That wasn't a point.*

He genuinely didn't know. That was becoming a pattern.

"I'll pass," he said.

"Aw, come on. Why?"

"I don't sing."

"Nobody *sings* at karaoke. Everyone just yells. Sayo does enka, it's hilarious. Hina does the idol songs with the dances. Rin--" He stopped. Something moved across his face and was gone again. "Rin usually doesn't come. But she might this time."

"..."

*She might this time.*

He'd said it lightly. He was watching Kuze's face as he said it.

"I'll pass," Kuze said again.

Amamiya let out a breath through his nose and scratched the back of his head. For a second, he looked genuinely disappointed, and that was the most confusing thing yet.

"Okay, okay. Got it. Offer stands, though." He pushed off from the lockers. Then he hesitated, one hand on the edge of the locker door. "Hey, Kuze."

"What?"

"Rin's... she's important to me, you know? Like, she's been through some stuff. So, uh."

He didn't finish. He seemed to be waiting for Kuze to finish it for him.

Kuze didn't.

The silence stretched between the lockers. Somewhere outside, the baseball club was running laps, their voices coming and going with the wind.

"...So be nice to her," Amamiya said finally. "That's all."

"I'm not anything to her. I eat her omelettes."

It had slipped out. He'd meant it to sound dismissive. It came out sounding like a confession.

Amamiya blinked.

"Her *what?*"

"..."

*Idiot.*

"Nothing. I'm leaving."

Kuze shoved his indoor shoes into the locker, jammed his feet into his outdoor ones without undoing the laces, and walked out into the afternoon before Amamiya could say anything else.

He didn't look back. He could feel Amamiya standing there in the entrance, watching him go, and he could feel the question he'd left behind him like a dropped glove.

*Be nice to her.*

As if it were Amamiya's to ask. As if he were the one who'd sat on the roof with the terrible omelette and the story about the dojo.

Kuze stopped himself there. That wasn't fair either. Amamiya *had* been in the dojo. That was the whole point of the story.

The sediment settled into a shape he didn't recognize, heavier on one side than the other.

7

He was halfway to the station, walking under the last of the cherry trees along the river, when he heard footsteps pounding up behind him.

"Kuze-kun! Kuze-kuuun! Wait!"

He turned. Hina Matsuri was running toward him, ponytail flying, school bag bouncing against her hip, one hand raised high over her head.

She skidded to a stop in front of him, doubled over, panting.

"Hah... hah... you walk... *so fast...*"

"I walk normally."

"You walk like... someone's chasing you..."

"Is someone?"

"*Me!* I'm chasing you!"

She straightened up, took a huge breath, and held something out to him in both hands, formally, like a diploma.

It was a can of coffee.

Black coffee. The exact brand from the vending machine on the second floor.

"...What's this?"

"I said I owe you one! For class duty!" Hina beamed. "So, one! Black, no sugar! Even though it tastes like sadness!"

"..."

Kuze looked at the can.

He looked at Hina, flushed and out of breath, a light sheen of sweat at her hairline, grinning at him with every one of her teeth.

*I owe you one.*

He had written it in the ledger in disappearing ink. He had filed it with all the others, the thousand small promises people make on their way out the door, which everyone understands are never meant to be kept. He hadn't expected to collect. He'd been sure of it, the way he was sure of most things about people.

And she had run after him down the riverbank to pay it.

"...You didn't have to."

"I did! Hina never forgets a debt, remember? I told you!" She pushed the can into his hand and folded his fingers around it with both of hers. It was cold from the machine. "Now we're even! Okay?"

"...Okay."

"Okay!"

She let go, spun around, and started back the way she'd come, then stopped and turned around again, walking backward.

"Oh! And Kuze-kun?"

"Yes?"

"*Polite speech!*"

"...Yeah. What?"

"Better!" She laughed. "Rin-chan was really happy today, you know! At lunch! I don't know where she went, but she came back all red and smiley and pretending she wasn't!" She was still walking backward, getting smaller. "I don't know what you did, but thanks! Rin-chan doesn't smile enough!"

"I didn't do anything."

"Liar~!"

And she was gone, around the corner by the bridge, ponytail last.

Kuze stood under the cherry tree, holding the can.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he cracked it open.

It didn't taste like sadness at all.

He made a mark in the ledger. *Hina Matsuri, one class duty. Paid.*

It was the first entry in a long time that had closed itself.

On the east stairwell, one floor below the roof, the fire door had a small square window of reinforced glass set into it at head height.

At lunchtime, anyone standing on the landing could see through it to the top step, and past the top step, through the broken door that never quite closed, to a slice of roof, a water tank, and two figures sitting a precise meter apart against it.

Momo Shirasagi had been standing on that landing at 12:41.

Now, at 9:12 that night, she lay on her stomach on a pink bedspread, heels kicking slowly in the air, and scrolled through the photos.

Rin Tachibana, holding out a lunch box with both hands, her face red.

A boy from the back row with a slice of something yellow and brown halfway to his mouth.

The two of them, shaking hands.

"Hmm~"

Momo zoomed in on the handshake. She zoomed in on Rin's face, and then on the boy's. She tilted her head and hummed a little tune through her nose.

"Kuze-kun, was it?"

She opened Chirp. Not her main account, which had four hundred followers and a profile picture of a strawberry parfait and nothing but cheerful, harmless posts about cute cafés and cuter animals.

The other one.

It had no picture and no name, only a string of random letters and numbers. It had eighty-three followers, and every single one of them went to Seiryou.

She selected a photo. She cropped it carefully, so that you couldn't quite see the boy's face, but you could see Rin's very well.

Her thumb hovered over the button.

"Umyu... not yet, I think~"

She saved the draft, locked her phone, and rolled onto her back, staring up at the glow-in-the-dark stars on her ceiling.

"Rin-chan's got a secret~" she sang softly to the empty room. "And secrets are only fun..."

She smiled. It wasn't the smile she used for Haruto.

"...when you pick the right time to tell them."
