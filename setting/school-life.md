# School Life: Writing a Japanese High School That Feels Real

> **Purpose:** Give the writer an authentic, detailed working model of a Japanese high school (calendar, daily rhythm, spaces, systems, customs, and social order) and show how each piece becomes story material in the light novel voice.
> **Load when:** Any story set in (or flashing back to) a Japanese school, and whenever you plan an arc, a chapter, or a scene that uses a school event, room, or routine.
> **Related:** [Scene Playbook](../scenes/scene-playbook.md), [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md), [School Drama & Slice of Life](../genres/school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md), [Genre Index](../genres/genre-index.md), [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md), [Diction & Japanese Terms](../core/diction-and-japanese-terms.md), [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md), [Social Media & Texts](social-media-and-texts.md), [Style Bible](../core/style-bible.md)

## 1. Why school texture matters to the voice

The light novel voice this library teaches runs on **observation of small social details**: who got offered a drink, who was left out of a "we," who sat where. See [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md). A Japanese school is a machine that *produces* such details all day long. It has fixed seats, rotating duties, cleaning groups, attendance numbers, class reps, club hierarchies, shoe lockers, and group assignments for the school trip. Every one of those systems forces two people together who would not otherwise talk, or publicly sorts people into insiders and outsiders.

That is the secret of school-life writing in this genre: **the institution is the plot generator.** A cynical loner like Minato Kuze never needs to *choose* to interact with the heroines. The duty roster chooses for him. Here is how that reads on the page:

> Kuze's attendance number was eleven. Tachibana's was twelve.
> It had never mattered before. Then the class duty rotation reached the K's and the T's in the same week, and suddenly the only thing standing between him and the train home was a girl who looked at him like he was gum on her shoe.

Get the systems right and the scenes write themselves. Get them wrong, with American lockers, prom, "detention hall," or a cafeteria with trays and a jock table, and readers of the genre will feel it immediately, even if they can't say why.

The rest of this file is the reference model. Use it the way a set designer uses a floor plan.

## 2. The school year (April to March)

The Japanese academic year starts in **April**, runs through **three terms**, and ends in **March**. Students are "first-year," "second-year," and "third-year" (ichinensei, ninensei, sannensei). Never write "sophomore," "junior," or "Grade 11."

| Month | Events | Story use |
|---|---|---|
| **April** | Opening ceremony (shigyoushiki), entrance ceremony for first-years, cherry blossoms, **class reshuffle** lists posted on a board, self-introductions, seat assignment by attendance number, health checkups, club recruitment week | The classic series opener. A new class list means a new cast. Self-introductions are a free character-establishing scene, and club recruitment puts kouhai in the halls. |
| **Late April / early May** | **Golden Week** (a cluster of national holidays) | The first "outside school" outing. Who invited whom is a loaded question. |
| **May** | First **midterms** (chuukan tesuto) in some schools; some schools hold the **sports festival** here | Study-group arcs, and the first ranking comparison |
| **June** | **Uniform change** (koromogae) on June 1 to summer uniforms; the rainy season (tsuyu) | Umbrella scenes, see-through-shirt jokes (only within the [ecchi safety rule](../genres/ecchi-and-fanservice.md)), damp gloomy chapters, and class pool lessons starting |
| **July** | **Finals** (kimatsu tesuto), end-of-term ceremony, **report cards**, summer break starts around July 20 | Make-up tests and remedial classes for the failures, plus pre-summer plans |
| **Late July to August** | **Summer break**: summer homework, supplementary classes, **club training camps** (gasshuku), summer festivals in yukata, fireworks, the beach, Obon in mid-August | The "whole cast off-campus" arcs: festivals, the beach, camp, and a last-day-of-summer homework panic |
| **September** | Second term begins. Many schools hold the **sports festival** or the **cultural festival** now | Festival arcs dominate autumn |
| **October** | Uniform change back to winter on October 1; midterms; the **student council election** in many schools | Election arcs, speech scenes in the gym |
| **Oct–Nov** | **Cultural festival** (bunkasai) at many schools; the **school trip** (shuugaku ryokou) for second-years, commonly autumn to early winter | The two biggest set-pieces of a school LN |
| **December** | Finals, end of the second term, **Christmas Eve** (a couples' holiday in Japan, not a family one), winter break | Confession pressure and "who are you spending Christmas Eve with?" |
| **January** | New Year and the first **shrine visit** (hatsumode) with fortunes (omikuji), third term begins, and for third-years the **Common Test** university entrance exam in mid-January | Kimono/furisode visuals, a bad fortune as foreshadowing, and senpai under exam stress |
| **February** | **Valentine's Day**: girls give chocolate; obligation chocolate (giri choco), true-feeling chocolate (honmei choco), and friend chocolate (tomo choco). University exams | The heroine's chocolate-type ambiguity *is* the plot |
| **March** | **White Day** (March 14: boys return gifts), third-year **graduation ceremony**, year-end finals, spring break, and next April's class reshuffle looming | Farewells, the "second button" of a graduating boy's gakuran given to the girl he likes, and the fear of being split into different classes |

**Rules of thumb:**
- A school year is a natural **season of a series**. Planning around the calendar gives each arc a built-in deadline. See [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md).
- **Festival timing varies by school.** Pick dates for your school and record them in the [story bible](../templates/story-bible.md). Don't hold the sports festival in both May and October.
- Seasonal markers are cheap atmosphere: cicadas in July, the summer uniform's short sleeves, a kotatsu at a friend's house in January, and cherry blossom petals on the entrance ceremony.

## 3. A school day, hour by hour

A realistic daily timeline (adjust for your school):

| Time | What happens | Details to use |
|---|---|---|
| ~7:00–8:15 | **Commute**. Train or bicycle is common, walking for locals. The commuter pass (teiki) is tapped at the gates. | Crowded trains, the same-car-every-morning encounter, a friend waiting at the station, rain soaking a bike rider |
| ~8:15 | **Arrival at the entrance (shoukouguchi)**. Change from outdoor shoes to indoor shoes (uwabaki) at the **shoe lockers** (getabako). | Love letters and threat notes in shoe lockers. A missing indoor shoe is a bullying tell. |
| ~8:30–8:40 | **Morning homeroom** (short homeroom, SHR), taken by the homeroom teacher (tannin). Attendance, announcements. | The class duty student calls "Stand. Bow. Sit." (kiritsu, rei, chakuseki). A teacher's "Oh, and Kuze, see me after school" is a hook. |
| 8:50–15:20 | **Six periods** of about 50 minutes, with 10-minute breaks. Some schools run a seventh period. Students mostly **stay in their homeroom**, and the **teachers move** between rooms. | Breaks are for bathroom trips and quick chats. Special classes (chemistry, music, art, PE) move students to special rooms, so the class walks the halls in clumps. |
| ~12:30–13:20 | **Lunch** (hiru yasumi) in the classroom, often by pushing desks together, or at the cafeteria (gakushoku), the school store (baiten or koubai) bread rush, the courtyard, or the rooftop if it's open. | Who eats with whom is the social map. Homemade bento is a statement. The yakisoba bread and melon bread sell out in minutes. |
| ~15:20 | **End-of-day homeroom** | Handouts, reminders, sometimes a seat change lottery |
| ~15:30–15:50 | **Cleaning** (souji). Students clean their classroom, halls, stairs, and assigned areas in rotating groups. There are no janitors in the student-facing story. | Forced pairings: taking out the trash to the collection area, wiping windows, sweeping the stairs. The source novel uses a trash run as a first-contact scene. |
| ~16:00–18:30 | **Club activities** (bukatsu), or going home for "go-home club" members (kitaku-bu). | Sports clubs yell on the field. The brass band practices scales in stairwells. The go-home club walks out through all of it. |
| ~18:30–19:00 | **Final school-leave time**. A chime or broadcast tells students to leave; the gates close. | Being "the last ones in the building" is a scene. Staying late for festival prep needs teacher permission. |
| Evening | Cram school (juku), part-time jobs (if permitted), homework, phone | Night-time phone scrolling is where the POV reads [posts](social-media-and-texts.md) and spirals. |

**The bell** is often a Westminster-style chime (the source novel renders it as a four-note onomatopoeia). A standalone chime line is a classic section opener or closer. See [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md).

## 4. The spaces of a school, and what each is for in a story

Think of the campus as a **stage with specific emotional functions**. Every location has a default mood and a default scene type. Using the right room is half the atmosphere.

### 4.1 The classroom
- **Layout:** the teacher's podium/platform (kyoudan) at the front, a blackboard (chalk, sometimes whiteboard), the date and duty names written in the corner of the board, desks in rows of about 30–40, cubbies or small lockers at the back, windows along one side, and a sliding door at the front and back.
- **Seats matter.** The **window seat at the back** is the classic protagonist seat: it gives him a view outside and a view of the whole class. Seats are first assigned by attendance number, then changed by lottery (sekigae) once a term or so. A seat change is an event: who ends up next to whom?
- **After school, the classroom empties** and becomes a private stage lit by sunset through the windows. Confessions, arguments, and sudden honesty happen here.
- **Story uses:** public humiliation (bullying is *public*), the class as a jury, and overheard gossip (the POV "sleeps" on his desk and listens).

### 4.2 Hallways and stairwells
- Long corridors, windows onto the courtyard, club posters, a trophy case. **Stairwell landings** are semi-private and good for quick, whispered exchanges.
- **The "peeking from behind a corner" shot** belongs here. A girl watches her crush from behind a wall, and the POV catches her doing it.

### 4.3 The shoe lockers (getabako)
- A bank of small cubbies at the entrance. Every student's indoor shoes live here.
- **Story uses:** love letters, anonymous notes, bullying (shoes hidden, garbage stuffed in), and waiting for someone "by chance" at the lockers when they leave.

### 4.4 The rooftop (okujou)
- The iconic LN lunch spot, with sky, wind, and a fence. **In reality most rooftops are locked.** Use that: a character with a key, a broken lock, a "no entry" sign everyone ignores. It makes the rooftop a *secret* place rather than a public one, which is better for the story anyway.
- **Story uses:** lunch with the one person who understands you, a confrontation, crying where no one can see.

### 4.5 Behind the gym (taiikukan-ura) and behind the school building
- The **confession spot** and the **fight/threat spot**. The stock phrase "Come behind the gym after school" is ambiguous on purpose. Is it a love letter or a beating? Play with that ambiguity.
- The storage shed on the edge of the sports ground is where people go to hide.

### 4.6 The staff room (shokuinshitsu)
- Students say "Excuse me" (shitsurei shimasu) when entering and leaving. Desks are crowded with papers; teachers eat lunch here.
- **Story uses:** being summoned, delivering the class diary, hearing a teacher's offhand comment about another student, and the formal "resolution meeting" after an incident.

### 4.7 The nurse's office (hokenshitsu)
- A school nurse (often a relaxed, perceptive adult), white beds with curtains, and a place to skip class "because of a headache."
- **Story uses:** a curtain between two beds, and two students who can't see each other's faces saying things they couldn't say face to face.

### 4.8 The library
- Quiet, with a library committee member at the desk. It is the natural habitat of the shy bookworm.
- **Story uses:** whispered conversations (forced intimacy through the need for quiet), a book as a shared secret, and study groups before exams.

### 4.9 Special classrooms and clubrooms
- Music room, art room, science labs, the home economics room (cooking class, so homemade sweets), the broadcasting room (the source's harem is a broadcasting club; microphones make private words public by accident), and the **club building** (bushitsu-tou) with small rooms for culture clubs.
- **Story uses:** each club room is a "home base" for a group. An outsider entering one is a scene in itself.

### 4.10 The student council room
- A long table, a president's chair, stacks of paperwork, and sometimes tea.
- **Story uses:** power, as the council controls budgets and festival approvals. A scene with a student council president is a scene with an authority figure the same age as the POV.

### 4.11 The sports ground, gym, and pool
- The ground has a track and baseball nets, and sports clubs shout practice calls. The gym hosts assemblies, ceremonies, and speeches. The pool is outdoor, used in summer PE.
- **Story uses:** assemblies (the entire school watching one person), PE (pairing up, which is the loner's nightmare), and after-hours practice.

### 4.12 The courtyard (nakaniwa) and vending machines
- Benches, a flowerbed ledge, and vending machines that sell **hot and cold canned drinks**. In winter a hot canned coffee warms cold hands.
- **Story uses:** a friend tossing you a can, which is a whole emotional language. A choice of drink reveals a character (black coffee, milk tea, a strawberry milk carton).

### 4.13 The school gate and bike parking
- The gate is the boundary between "school self" and "real self." Waiting at the gate means *I wanted to walk home with you*. Bike parking is where the bike rider and walker part ways.

### 4.14 Off-campus haunts
| Place | Typical use |
|---|---|
| **Family restaurant** (famiresu): cheap menus, a **drink bar** (unlimited refills), a call button on the table | Group meetings, study sessions, awkward three-person meals. Who sits beside whom is the drama. |
| **Convenience store** (konbini) | Late-night encounters, buying fried chicken, part-time job scenes |
| **Karaoke box** | Group bonding, and song choice as confession |
| **Arcade / sticker-photo booths (purikura)** | Date scenes, and a photo that later becomes evidence |
| **Station and trains** | Commutes, "we get off at the same stop," missing the last train |
| **Shopping mall** | Group "dates," food court politics |
| **Shrine** | Festivals, New Year, making a wish, fortunes |
| **Cram school (juku)** | Meeting students from other schools |

## 5. The systems: duties, roles, and rules

These are the machinery that pushes characters together. Know them precisely.

### 5.1 Attendance numbers (shusseki bangou)
Students are numbered in **Japanese syllabary order** (a-i-u-e-o order, gojuuon) by surname. It is often split by gender in older-style schools and mixed in many modern ones. The number decides the first seats, the order for duties, and who stands in line next to whom.
- **Story use:** "Her number came right after his" explains the first forced pairing without any contrivance. Surnames that sound close put characters next to each other for a year. Choose your cast's surnames with this in mind: Kuze (K) and Kujou (K) would be adjacent, which could be useful for a duty-pairing plot.

### 5.2 Class duty (nicchoku)
Two students per day, rotating by attendance number. Typical tasks:
- Writing the date and both duty names in the corner of the blackboard
- Calling "Stand, bow, sit" at the start and end of lessons
- **Erasing the blackboard** after each period
- Filling in the **class diary** (gakkyuu nisshi): each period's subject and notes, and a comment on the day
- Closing the windows and turning off the lights after school
- **Delivering the diary to the homeroom teacher in the staff room**

**Story uses:** it gives two students a reason to stay after school together, and a reason to walk to the staff room (where a teacher can hand them an errand). The source's opening chapter uses class duty for its first scene because nothing else is needed: *the system* pairs the loner with a heroine and shows him the harem from the edge of the room. The "partner ditches duty to go to her club" moment is a perfect small injustice for the POV to chew on.

### 5.3 Cleaning duty (souji touban)
Groups of four to six clean assigned zones daily. **Taking out the trash** to the collection point is the classic two-person errand with an off-screen walk, so it doubles as a private conversation.

### 5.4 The class rep (iinchou) and committees
- The **class representative** (and often a vice rep) runs homeroom meetings, relays teacher messages, and herds the class for events. The glasses-wearing, strict, secretly soft class rep is a trope. See [Character Tropes](../cast/character-tropes.md).
- **Committees** (iinkai): library, health, beautification, broadcasting, festival executive, and so on. Every student joins one, and usually the leftover slots go to whoever didn't volunteer fast enough.
- **Story use:** "Kuze, you're on the festival committee." "I didn't volunteer." "That's why you're on it." Committee assignment pairs characters for a whole arc.

### 5.5 Long homeroom (LHR)
A weekly period used for class decisions such as festival themes, sports festival events, and school trip groups. **Majority votes and blackboard tallies.**
- **Story use:** public decision-making exposes the social hierarchy. The top-caste group proposes a maid café; nobody dares object. The loner's hand stays down. Or it goes up once, and the room goes silent.

### 5.6 Clubs (bukatsu)
- **Sports clubs** (undoubu: baseball, soccer, basketball, volleyball, tennis, kendo, track, swimming) are hierarchical, with early mornings and strict senpai.
- **Culture clubs** (bunkabu: brass band, art, literature, light music, tea ceremony, broadcasting, drama, computer, and many invented ones in LNs) are smaller and often a group's private hangout.
- Clubs need a **minimum membership** and a **faculty advisor** (komon). The "save the club from being disbanded" plot comes from this rule.
- **The go-home club (kitaku-bu)** isn't a real club. It's the joke name for having no club. For the POV it's an identity statement: he has no team, no senpai, and no after-school obligations, so he's *free* and *alone* at once.

### 5.7 The student council (seitokai)
Elected officers: president, vice president, secretary, treasurer, and so on. They run assemblies, approve club budgets, and manage the festival. Elections have candidates, speeches in the gym, and campaigning at the gate in the morning.
- **Story use:** elections are popularity contests with rules, which is perfect for the cynical narrator's analysis of why people vote the way they do.

### 5.8 Uniforms and appearance rules
- **Boys:** a gakuran (black high-collar jacket with gold buttons) or a blazer with a tie. **Girls:** a sailor uniform (seifuku with a scarf/ribbon) or a blazer with a ribbon and pleated skirt.
- Rules on skirt length, hair color, makeup, earrings, and phones are common. "Natural" brown hair may need a certificate at strict schools.
- A **ribbon or tie color often marks the year** (e.g. first-years red, second-years blue). Use it: the POV reads a stranger's year from her ribbon before she speaks.
- **Story use:** breaking the rules is characterization. The gyaru's short skirt, the delinquent's open collar, and the class rep's perfect ribbon each tell you who someone is.

### 5.9 Exams, rankings, and remedial classes
- Midterms and finals by subject. Some schools **post the top ranks** in the hallway, which makes a crowd, a rivalry, and a surprise name at #1.
- A failing score (akaten, the "red mark") means **make-up exams** (tsuishi) and **supplementary lessons** (hoshuu), sometimes during summer break.
- **Story use:** the study group, the tutor relationship, the smart loner forced to teach the genki girl, and the "if you fail, you can't go to the festival/beach" stake.

### 5.10 Discipline
There is **no "detention hall."** Instead:
- Being called to the **staff room** or the **guidance room** (seitoshidou-shitsu)
- Writing a **reflection essay** (hanseibun)
- Parent-teacher meetings, and home suspension (teigaku) for serious cases
- **Story use:** after a big incident (a fight, a public outburst), the resolution is a meeting in the staff room with everyone involved and a teacher mediating, which is exactly how the source novel resolves its bullying climax. See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md).

### 5.11 Phones, the class group chat, and rumor
- Most students have smartphones. Schools may ban them during class. There's usually a **class group chat**, and the loner might not be in it, or might be in it and never speak.
- Rumor travels faster than anything, via chat, social media, and whispered hallway talk. See [Social Media & Texts](social-media-and-texts.md).

## 6. The big events, in writer's detail

These are the set-pieces an arc is built around. For each: the practical details and the story engine.

### 6.1 The class reshuffle and self-introductions (April)
- The class lists are posted on a board at the entrance, with crowds, squeals, and groans. Friends are separated or reunited.
- On day one, **self-introductions**: each student stands and says their name, former middle school, hobbies, and a closing line ("Please take care of me," yoroshiku onegaishimasu).
- **Engine:** a self-introduction is a free character portrait. The loner's is two sentences long. The genki girl's gets a laugh. The transfer student's is mysterious. One botched introduction can define a character's whole year.

### 6.2 The sports festival (taiikusai)
- Teams by class or by color (red vs. white is classic). Events include relays (the anchor runner is a star position), 100m sprints, tug-of-war, the **borrowing race** (karimono kyousou: runners draw a card with an item like "someone you like" or "glasses" and must bring it to the finish), the **cavalry battle** (kibasen: teams carry a rider who grabs headbands), three-legged races, cheer squads (ouendan), class T-shirts, and tents for each class.
- **Engine:** the borrowing race card is the most famous rom-com device in school fiction. "Someone important to me" is read aloud over the loudspeaker. The three-legged race forces physical closeness. Relay selection shows who the class values. The loner who secretly runs fast is a reveal.

### 6.3 The cultural festival (bunkasai)
- Every class and club puts on something: a **maid/butler café**, a **haunted house**, a food stall (yakisoba, takoyaki, crepes), a **play** (Romeo and Juliet, Cinderella), an **indie film screening**, an exhibit, or a band concert in the gym.
- **Prep weeks:** long homeroom meetings to choose the theme, after-school work sessions, cardboard and paint, costume fittings, and permission to stay late. Some schools hold an eve festival (zenyasai).
- **The day(s):** outside visitors (parents, other schools' students, siblings), a stamp rally, class rivalries over visitor numbers.
- **The after-party (kouyasai):** often a **bonfire** on the sports ground with a folk dance where partners rotate. Legend has it that the couple dancing when the music ends will be together forever.
- **Engine:** the festival is the biggest ensemble set-piece of the year, with casting disputes (who plays the heroine?), rehearsal intimacy, sabotage, and a finale. The source novel's second arc is built entirely around festival prep (a play team vs. an indie film team) and uses casting as its battlefield. The bonfire dance gives a built-in "who will you dance with" climax.

### 6.4 The school trip (shuugaku ryokou)
- Usually for **second-years**. The destination is Kyoto/Nara, Okinawa, Hokkaido, or sometimes abroad. There are three to four days, **free-action groups** (han) of four to six chosen in homeroom, a group itinerary, a large inn with shared tatami rooms, a big bath, bedtime roll call, and teachers patrolling hallways.
- **Night talk:** the classic "so, who do you like?" in the dark of the tatami room. Sneaking out to meet someone. Getting caught.
- **Engine:** **group formation** is the loner's crucible ("Form groups of five," and he's the leftover). Free-action day is a date in disguise. Night talk forces confessions. A famous temple, a fortune slip, and a wish at a shrine give lyrical settings.

### 6.5 Summer break
- Summer homework (a stack of workbooks plus an essay), supplementary classes for failures, club training camps, a summer festival with yukata, goldfish scooping, candied apples, and **fireworks**. There's the beach, the pool, the countryside grandparents' house for Obon, and the last-week homework panic.
- **Engine:** summer takes the cast *out of the institution*, which removes the systems that forced them together. Who still meets up without a reason? Being invited or not invited is the story. Fireworks give a built-in "a line drowned out by the explosion" device (use it once per series at most, because it's famous).

### 6.6 Christmas, Valentine's, and White Day
- **Christmas Eve** is a couples' date night. Being single on Christmas Eve is a running comedic misery. Illuminations, a cake, and a present exchange.
- **Valentine's Day:** girls give chocolate. **Giri** (obligation) for classmates and coworkers, **honmei** (true feelings) for the one, **tomo** (friends) among girls. Homemade chocolate signals effort. Shoe lockers and desks get checked all morning.
- **White Day** (March 14): boys return the favor, traditionally "triple" the value.
- **Engine:** "Was this giri or honmei?" is a perfect ambiguity for an overanalyzing narrator. He can spend a whole section on the wrapping paper.

### 6.7 Graduation (sotsugyoushiki)
- A formal ceremony in the gym, speeches, singing, crying underclassmen, flowers, and the **second button** of the gakuran (closest to the heart) given to the one you love. Signing albums. The last walk out of the gate.
- **Engine:** the senpai farewell, and deadlines for unspoken feelings.

## 7. Social order: caste, cliques, and the loner

### 7.1 School caste (sukuuru kaasuto)
Japanese school fiction openly discusses an informal **caste system** within each class:
- **Top caste:** attractive, socially fluent students, often from sports clubs or with a flashy style. They set the class mood and speak first in homeroom meetings.
- **Middle:** ordinary students who go along with the top.
- **Bottom:** otaku, quiet students, the socially awkward, and the loner ("bocchi," literally "all alone").

**How to use it:** the caste is never official, but everyone knows it. The POV narrator's value is that he *names* it plainly while everyone else pretends it doesn't exist. Caste explains why nobody objects to the top group's festival idea, why bullying targets are chosen, and why the harem lead is untouchable.

### 7.2 Cliques and "groups"
Girls' friend groups in particular are rendered as tight units that eat lunch together, move together, and police membership. Being dropped from a group is a disaster. A group's leader (like Reika Hoshino in the default cast) can **steer the class through public opinion** without ever doing anything a teacher can punish.

### 7.3 The loner's daily life
Write the loner's experience **concretely**:
- He eats lunch at his desk, reading, or somewhere nobody goes. The extreme version is eating in a bathroom stall (benjo meshi), which is a byword for social death.
- "Pair up for stretches" in PE is a small daily humiliation.
- When a group forms, he steps back one pace. Narrate that step. See [Description & Portraits](../core/description-and-portraits.md) on body language.
- He knows everyone's name and nobody knows his. Or the reverse, as with the source's protagonist, who didn't know a classmate's name and got scolded for it. Either is a characterization choice.
- He has **one friend**, often from another class, and that friend is his lifeline. See the confidant in [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md).

### 7.4 Senpai/kouhai hierarchy
Seniority is real, especially in clubs. Kouhai use polite speech to senpai and bow. Senpai may be casual downward. A first-year calling a third-year by name without an honorific is a transgression, and therefore a scene. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).

## 8. Small customs that make it feel authentic

Sprinkle these in. Each is one line of texture and often a hook for a small scene.

- **Bento culture:** lunchboxes with rice, tamagoyaki (rolled omelette), octopus-shaped sausages, karaage. A bento made *for* someone is a romantic declaration. Comparing bentos is a conversation opener.
- **Indirect kiss:** sharing a drink or chopsticks. Characters (especially tsundere) freak out about it.
- **Walking home together** is noticed and gossiped about. Being "seen" together at a station is the kind of thing that becomes a rumor or a photo.
- **"Itadakimasu" / "Gochisousama"** before and after eating. Characters say these in the voice's dialogue; keep them unitalicized.
- **Taking off shoes** at homes and changing into slippers. Visiting a friend's house is formal: "Sorry to intrude" (ojama shimasu).
- **Omiyage:** bringing back souvenir snacks from any trip for classmates or club members. Forgetting to is noticed.
- **Name-calling etiquette:** switching from surname to given name is intimate. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).
- **Bowing:** a small head bow as a thank-you, a deep bow as an apology. The source protagonist gives a "polite bow" to a teacher.
- **Hot canned drinks** from vending machines in winter, and **kotatsu** heated tables at home.
- **Teachers' nicknames:** students call a young teacher by a nickname or "-chan" behind her back. Other teachers scold her for being too casual with students (a detail from the source's homeroom teacher).
- **Umbrella-sharing (aiaigasa):** two names under an umbrella drawing, a classic "they're a couple" tease on the blackboard.
- **"Excuse me for leaving before you"** (osaki ni shitsurei shimasu): politeness when leaving a group still working.

## 9. The Americanism trap: what NOT to write

These instantly break the illusion.

| Don't write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| Hallway lockers with combination locks | Shoe lockers at the entrance; cubbies at the back of the classroom |
| Students moving between classrooms every period | Students stay in the homeroom; teachers move (except special classes) |
| Detention | A staff room summons, a reflection essay, or supplementary lessons |
| Prom, homecoming, a pep rally | Cultural festival, sports festival, after-party bonfire |
| "Sophomore," "junior," "Grade 11" | "Second-year" |
| A cafeteria with trays and a jock table | Lunch at desks in the classroom, the school store bread rush, a cafeteria of noodles and set meals |
| Janitors cleaning | Students cleaning in groups |
| "Mr. Ogata," "Ms. Ogata" | "Ogata-sensei" |
| A school year starting in September | April |
| A school bus as the default | Train, bicycle, walking |
| Valentine's as a mutual couples' day | Girls give chocolate on Feb 14; boys return it on White Day |
| Christmas as a family holiday | Christmas Eve as a couples' date; New Year as the family holiday |
| Cheerleaders | A cheer squad (ouendan) of students in headbands and gakuran |
| First-name basis by default | Surname basis by default, with given names as a sign of intimacy |

## 10. How the POV narrator *reads* the institution

This is where school life stops being set dressing and becomes the voice. The cynical narrator treats every system as **a machine that exposes people**. See [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md).

**The duty roster as a moral test:**
> Hina had apologized for leaving him with class duty, clapped her hands together, and run for the clubroom. That was fine. Clubs mattered to people.
> What stayed with Kuze was that she hadn't apologized to the board. It was still covered in the math teacher's equations, and it would still be covered in them if he left too.
> So someone always stays. And in this class, the someone was a go-home club member.

**The seat change as fate:**
> The lottery slip said thirty-one. Window side, back row.
> The best seat in the room, by every measure Kuze cared about. He could see the sky, the sports ground, and the entire class without turning his head.
> Then Tachibana unfolded her slip, looked at the board, and looked at him.
> Thirty-two.
> "..."
> "Don't talk to me."
> "I wasn't going to."

**The school trip group as a verdict:**
> "Okay~ Form groups of five!"
> The classroom broke apart and re-formed in the time it took Kuze to close his book. Four groups of five, one of four, and one boy sitting by the window like a leftover piece of a jigsaw puzzle.
> It was efficient, really. Nobody had to be cruel. The system did it for them.

Notice the technique: **concrete institutional detail, then the narrator's dry verdict on what it reveals.** Every system in Sections 5–6 can be run through this treatment.

## 12. Checklist for school-life writing

Before submitting a school chapter, confirm:

- [ ] The **date on the school calendar** is clear or inferable, and seasonal detail matches it (uniform, weather, events).
- [ ] At least one **institutional system** (duty, cleaning, committee, club, seat, group) is doing plot work, not just decoration.
- [ ] **Spaces** are used for their emotional function (the after-school classroom for honesty, behind the gym for confessions or threats, the staff room for resolution).
- [ ] No **Americanisms** from Section 9.
- [ ] **Honorifics and year labels** are consistent (second-year, Ogata-sensei, the senpai/kouhai forms).
- [ ] The **caste and clique structure** of the class is visible in who speaks, who sits where, and who eats with whom.
- [ ] The POV narrator **reads at least one system for what it reveals about people** (Section 10).
- [ ] Customs (bento, shoes, bowing, itadakimasu) appear as texture in one or two lines each, not as a lecture.
- [ ] Nothing is explained like a travel guide. Terms are shown in use and understood from context. See [Diction & Japanese Terms](../core/diction-and-japanese-terms.md).

For genre-specific school variants (fantasy academies, idol schools, sports-school arcs), see the [Genre Index](../genres/genre-index.md), [Fantasy](../genres/fantasy.md), and [Sports, Idol & Club](../genres/sports-idol-and-club.md).
