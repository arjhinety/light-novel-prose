# Honorifics & Address

> **Purpose:** How characters address each other: the honorific system, names, nicknames, and pronouns of address, and how shifts in address become plot.
> **Load when:** Introducing any character, writing any dialogue, or when a relationship changes. Also when continuing someone else's text, to extract the existing address forms.
> **Related:** [Dialogue Mechanics](dialogue-mechanics.md), [Character Voices](character-voices.md), [Diction & Japanese Terms](../core/diction-and-japanese-terms.md), [Character Design](../cast/character-design.md), [Story Bible Template](../templates/story-bible.md), [School Life](../setting/school-life.md)

## 1. Why honorifics stay in the English

Some translations strip honorifics and render "Kuze-kun" as "Kuze." That's a legitimate choice for a general audience, but **not for this voice**. LN readers expect them, and more importantly they carry information English has no other compact way to carry:

- **Distance.** "Kuze-kun" (polite, friendly) vs. "Kuze" (blunt or close) vs. "Kuze-san" (formal, cool).
- **Hierarchy.** Senpai, kouhai, and sensei.
- **Intimacy.** First name with no suffix is, between teenagers of opposite sexes, practically a declaration.
- **Personality.** A girl who calls everyone "-san" is reserved or proper. One who invents nicknames has no walls.

In the reference novel, honorifics appear on nearly every spoken name (-kun ~320 times, -san ~120, -sensei ~50, -chan ~40 across 100 chapters). They are not decoration. They are part of how the reader reads relationships.

**Narration vs. dialogue:** the narration refers to characters by **surname without honorifics** ("Kuze", "Tachibana", "Kujou", "Amamiya"). Honorifics live only inside quotation marks and inside the POV's quoted-back thoughts. This split is important: the narrator is neutral ground, and dialogue is the social battlefield. (Exception: teachers are often "Ogata-sensei" in narration too, or simply "Ogata". Pick one and hold it.)

## 2. The core system

### 2.1 Suffixes

| Suffix | Default use | Emotional reading | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(none), surname** | Boys to boys; blunt people; teachers to students; rivals | Casual, rough, or cold, depending on tone | "Kuze." from Rin is hostile early and close later. Context decides. |
| **(none), given name** | Family, childhood friends, lovers, the harem lead to his girls | Intimate | The rarest and most loaded form. |
| **-kun** | Girls to boys of equal/lower status; teachers to boys; polite boys to boys | Friendly-polite | Default for a girl addressing a male classmate. |
| **-san** | Neutral politeness to anyone; strangers; adults | Respectful, or deliberately distant | A girl who calls a boy "-san" instead of "-kun" is keeping him at arm's length. |
| **-chan** | Girls to girls; to children; affectionate; cutesy | Warm, familiar | Boys using "-chan" on a girl read as either childhood-close or flirty-cheeky. |
| **-senpai** | To upperclassmen (club, school) | Respect plus hierarchy | Can replace the name entirely ("Senpai!"). |
| **-sensei** | Teachers, doctors, authors, anyone with a mastery | Deference | Students almost never drop it for a teacher. When they do, it's a scene. |
| **-sama** | Very high respect; servants; fans; sarcasm | Worship or mockery | "Kujou-sama" from a fanboy is comedy. From a maid it's setting. |
| **-dono** | Archaic, samurai-drama politeness | Comic in modern settings | Gon's signature. |
| **-tan** | Baby-talk "-chan"; otaku fan-slang | Cutesy or creepy | Fanboys saying "Hina-tan" is a genre joke. |
| **-cchi** | Playful nickname suffix | Gyaru/genki friendliness | "Rincchi", "Sayocchi". |
| **-nee / -nii** | Older sister/brother; honorary | Familial affection | "Kasumi-nee" (Amamiya calling the teacher by a childhood nickname) is a *reveal*. |

### 2.2 Titles and roles used as names

- **"Sensei"**, bare, used to address any teacher.
- **"Kaichou"**, the student council president, addressed by title. ("Kaichou, the budget.")
- **"Senpai"**, bare, used as a name.
- **"Onee-san" / "Onii-san"**, to older strangers (shop clerks), or by the teasing older-sister archetype about herself ("Onee-san will take care of it~").
- **"Ojou-sama"**, used by servants or, sarcastically, by classmates.
- **"Iincho"**, the class representative (sometimes rendered "Class Rep").

### 2.3 Pronouns of address

English flattens Japanese pronouns (anata, kimi, omae, anta, temee) into "you". You can recover some of that texture:

| Japanese | Feel | English rendering strategy |
|---|---|---|
| anata | polite, or wifely | Neutral "you". A girl calling a boy "anata" can be rendered as "dear" in a joke. |
| kimi | gentle, slightly superior | "you" with warm framing |
| omae | rough, familiar, male | "you" plus blunt diction; "hey, you" |
| anta | rough-casual, often a tsundere girl | "Hey. **You.**" The pointed "you" as a name-substitute |
| temee / kisama | hostile | "you bastard", "you" snarled |

The strongest English tool is **"you" as a substitute for a name**. Rin calls Kuze "you" for the first ten chapters ("Hey, you, got a second?"). It's rude, dismissive, and also a marker of the relationship's starting point. The first time she says "Kuze", it's a scene.

## 3. The default cast's address ledger

Keep a ledger like this in every [story bible](../templates/story-bible.md). Rows are the speaker; columns are who they're addressing.

| Speaker to Target | Kuze | Amamiya | Rin | Sayo | Hina | Momo | Daichi |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Kuze** | (self: "I") | Amamiya | Tachibana | Kujou | Matsuri-san, later Matsuri | Shirasagi | Daichi |
| **Amamiya** | Kuze? (hesitant) | (self) | Rin | Sayo | Hina | Momo | Enomoto |
| **Rin** | you, later Kuze | Haruto / idiot Haruto | (self) | Kujou | Hina | Shirasagi | -- |
| **Sayo** | Kuze-kun | Haruto-kun | Tachibana-san | (self) | Matsuri-san | Shirasagi-san | Enomoto-kun |
| **Hina** | Kuze-kun | Haru | Rinrin | Sayocchi | (self) | Momocchi | Enomoto-kun |
| **Momo** | Kuze-kun (flat) | Haruto-kun~ (sweet) | Tachibana-san | Kujou-san | Hina-chan | (self) | -- |
| **Daichi** | Mina | Amamiya | Tachibana | Kujou | Matsuri | Shirasagi | (self) |
| **Ogata-sensei** | Kuze | Haruto (slip!), corrected to Amamiya | Tachibana | Kujou | Matsuri | Shirasagi | Enomoto |

Read the table for story:
- **Sayo** calls everyone with a suffix except... no one. Even "Haruto-kun" keeps the -kun. Her self-proclaimed "lawful wife" routine never actually drops the formality, which is a crack the POV can notice ("Does someone who calls herself his wife really keep saying -kun?").
- **Momo** uses "~" and "-kun" for Amamiya but flat "-kun" for Kuze. Her two faces are visible in suffixes alone.
- **Ogata-sensei's slip**, "Haruto", reveals a family connection. (In the reference novel, a similar slip tells the reader the teacher knows the lead personally. It is a strong device.)
- **Hina** nicknames everyone. It's her no-walls personality, but she calls Kuze "Kuze-kun", which means he's the one person she hasn't fully let in, or the one person she's careful with. Leave it ambiguous.

## 4. Name escalation as plot

The most powerful thing honorifics do is **change**. Each change is a small earthquake in the relationship, and the prose should register it.

### 4.1 The escalation ladder (girl addressing boy, school setting)

1. **"You" / no name**: stranger, or hostile
2. **Surname + -san**: polite stranger, distance
3. **Surname + -kun**: classmate, friendly
4. **Surname, bare**: comfortable, rough-close (tomboys and tsunderes may jump here early)
5. **Given name + -kun**: crush territory
6. **Given name, bare**: couple, family, or childhood friend
7. **Nickname**: deepest intimacy, or teasing comfort

(Boy addressing girl: surname + -san, then surname bare, then given name + -chan (rare, teasing), then given name bare.)

### 4.2 How to stage a shift

A shift should be **noticed**, by the other character, by the POV, or by an onlooker. Three staging modes:

**The accidental slip.** The character doesn't realize they did it:
> "--so just wait there, Minato."
> "..."
> "...What?"
> "Nothing."
> *Minato.* She'd never once said it. She didn't seem to have noticed she'd said it now.
> He decided not to tell her. If he told her, she'd never say it again.

**The deliberate request.** One side asks for the change:
> "Hey. Can you... stop calling me Tachibana?"
> "What should I call you, then?"
> "You know what."
> "I really don't."
> "Rin! Call me Rin, you idiot! Are you trying to make me say it!?"
> "Yes."
> She hit him.

**The public shift.** Someone uses the new form in front of others, and the room reacts:
> "Morning, Rin."
> The conversation at the next desk stopped. Two girls turned around. One of them mouthed *Rin?* to the other.
> Rin went scarlet to the collar and smacked the back of his head with a rolled-up notebook.
> "D-Don't just say it in the classroom!"

### 4.3 Backward shifts are even stronger

When a relationship breaks, address goes **backward**. This is devastating on the page because it's so small:

> "Good morning, Kuze-san."
> *San.*
> Not "Kuze-kun". Not even "you". *San*, like a stranger at a bus stop. Like someone you apologize to for bumping shoulders.
> He had known it was over. He hadn't known it would sound like that.

Use backward shifts at the arc's low point ([Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md)). Undo them at the reconciliation.

### 4.4 The polite-speech running gag

A cheerful girl scolding the protagonist for using polite speech (keigo) with her is a strong recurring bit. It establishes her no-walls personality and his reflexive distance at the same time:

> "Ugh! You're doing it again! We're the same year! No polite language!"
> "Sorry."
> "*Sorry* is still polite!"
> "...My bad."
> "Better!"

English doesn't have keigo, so render it with a **stiff register**: full sentences, "I apologize", "Is something the matter?", "Thank you very much". The shift to casual is contractions and slang ("My bad", "What's up?"). The gag lives in that contrast.

## 5. Edge cases and rules

### 5.1 Teachers
- Students **always** use -sensei. A student who calls a teacher by name alone is either a delinquent or has a secret personal connection.
- Teachers call students by surname, bare, or with -kun/-san if they're formal. A young, casual teacher might use given names, which other teachers scold her for.
- "Sensei" in narration: pick "Ogata-sensei" or "Ogata" and be consistent across the whole work.

### 5.2 Siblings
- Younger to older: "Onii-chan", "Nii-san", "Onee-chan", "Nee-san", "Aniki" (rough).
- Older to younger: given name, bare.
- The imouto's choice of form is characterization. "Onii-chan" is sweet or clingy, "Nii-san" is cool or distant, and "Aniki" is a tomboy.

### 5.3 Upperclassmen and underclassmen
- A kouhai calls the POV "Kuze-senpai" or just "Senpai". A kouhai who always says only "Senpai" is marking the POV as *her* senpai. That is possessive, and cute.
- The POV calls a kouhai by surname bare, or "-san" if he's awkward with girls.

### 5.4 The student council and clubs
- The president is "Kaichou" to her face, "the president" in narration, and her surname only when someone is being defiant.
- Club captains are "Captain" or "Buchou". This is less common in English LNs, so prefer "Captain" or the name with senpai.

### 5.5 Strangers and adults
- Shop clerks: "the clerk", or "Onee-san" if a child speaks.
- Parents of friends: "[Surname]'s mother", addressed as "Ma'am" or "Obasan".
- Adults to teens: "-kun"/"-chan" affectionately, or bare surname.

### 5.6 Fantasy and isekai
- In a European-style fantasy setting, the Japanese honorific system often **converts to titles**: "Lady Sayo", "Sir Minato", "Your Highness", "Master". Keep the *function* (distance, hierarchy, and intimacy shifts), change the *forms*. See [Fantasy](../genres/fantasy.md) and [Villainess & Otome](../genres/villainess-and-otome.md).
- A reincarnated protagonist may still *think* in honorifics ("Her Highness... no, Sayo-sama, whatever"). It's a fun seam between the two worlds. See [Isekai & Reincarnation](../genres/isekai-and-reincarnation.md).

### 5.7 Nicknames
- **Invented nicknames** ("Rinrin", "Momocchi", "Haru") are personality signatures. Only one or two characters should generate them.
- **Unwanted nicknames** are comedy ("Don't call me Rinrin." "Rinrin~").
- **The protagonist's nickname** from his childhood friend ("Mina") should appear only between the two of them. When someone else overhears it and uses it, it's either a violation or a gift, depending on who does it.

### 5.8 Addressing a group
- "Everyone" / "You guys" / "Minna" (keep in English: "Everyone!").
- A lead who says "Hina, Rin, Sayo, Momo--" listing names in order is revealing a hierarchy the POV will notice. Who came first?

## 6. Consistency discipline

Nothing breaks immersion faster than a "-kun" that silently becomes "-san" with no reason. Before drafting, and again during revision:

1. **Build or update the ledger** (§3) in the story bible.
2. **Search the draft for every name** and check each instance against the ledger.
3. **Every deviation must be motivated**: a slip, a request, a break, or sarcasm. If it isn't, fix it.
4. **Record every shift** in the bible with the chapter number, so later chapters stay consistent.
5. **Narration uses surnames only.** Check that you haven't slipped "Rin" into the narration just because Kuze started calling her that in dialogue. (The narration *can* eventually switch, but that's a deliberate, late, once-per-series move.)

## 7. Don'ts

- **Don't italicize honorifics.** They're part of the name: "Kuze-kun", not "Kuze-*kun*".
- **Don't hyphenate given-name nicknames inconsistently.** "Rinrin" or "Rin-rin". Choose one.
- **Don't translate honorifics into Mr./Miss** in a school setting. "Mr. Kuze" from a classmate is jarring. Use it only if a character is being mockingly formal, as a joke.
- **Don't overload a single line with suffixes.** "Kuze-kun, Tachibana-san, Kujou-san, Matsuri-chan, and Shirasagi-san" is a roll call, not dialogue. Let people address one person at a time.
- **Don't explain honorifics** to the reader in narration ("-kun, a suffix used for boys..."). LN readers know. If the audience is new to the genre, one natural in-scene hint is enough (the polite-speech gag teaches it painlessly).
- **Don't let the lead and the POV share a form.** If Amamiya calls Rin "Rin" and Kuze calls her "Rin", you've lost the contrast. Kuze has to earn it.

| "Haruto." (Ogata-sensei) | They have history |

## 9. Checklist

- [ ] Ledger exists in the bible, and every character's form for every other is set.
- [ ] Narration uses bare surnames; honorifics appear only in speech or quoted thought.
- [ ] Every address change in the draft is motivated and noticed.
- [ ] At least one character's address form reveals personality (nicknames, universal -san, the "you").
- [ ] Backward shifts are reserved for real breaks.
- [ ] No Mr./Miss in school dialogue unless it's a joke.
- [ ] Fantasy settings convert forms to titles but keep the functions.

## 10. Worked example: a whole relationship told in address forms

The following are six moments from one arc, showing how Rin's address for Kuze carries the relationship with no narration about feelings at all.

**Chapter 4: the first contact.**
> "Hey. You. Got a second?"
> "Who are you?"
> "Huh? You don't know the name of someone in your own cleaning group? Unbelievable. Tachibana. Remember it."

She calls him "you". He doesn't know her name. The imbalance is the joke, and it's also the starting line.

**Chapter 9: grudging acknowledgment.**
> "Oi, Kuze. You're on trash duty with me again."

The first time she uses his name, it's as an order. The narration can notice it in a single line ("*Kuze.* So she did know it.") and move on.

**Chapter 17: comfort.**
> "Kuze. Wait up."

The same name, softened by the verb. "Wait up" is a request, not a command. No narration is needed. The reader who has been paying attention will feel it.

**Chapter 26: the rift.**
> "Kuze-kun. I've got nothing to say to you."

She has never once used "-kun" for him. The added politeness is a wall. The narration should stop dead on the suffix: *Kun.* She'd never called him that. Not once.

**Chapter 33: the slip.**
> "--Minato, you idiot, why didn't you tell me!?"
> "..."
> "...What?"
> "Nothing."

His given name, used accidentally, in anger that is really fear for him. Neither acknowledges it.

**Chapter 40: the new normal.**
> "Kuze. Coffee. Black, right?"

Back to the bare surname, but now it sounds like his given name used to sound. Rin never says "Minato" again on purpose. That's the point. The slip was the confession, and they both know it.

Six lines, six stages. That is how much weight the address system can carry. Use it.

## 11. Address in inner monologue

The POV's private thoughts have their own address rules:

- When he thinks *about* someone, he uses the narrative form: bare surname ("Tachibana would be furious.").
- When he **quotes someone back to himself**, he keeps their exact address form, honorific and all: *"Kuze-kun." Since when did she call me that?*
- When he thinks *to* someone, in direct inner address, he may use a form he'd never say aloud, which is a quiet way to show hidden feelings: *Please, Amamiya. You're the only one she'll listen to. Please.* Or, late in the arc: *Rin. Don't cry.* That he calls her "Rin" only in his head, chapters before he says it aloud, is a slow-burn tool.

Keep these three modes distinct, and the reader will feel the gap between what the POV thinks and what he lets himself say.

## 12. Extracting address forms from someone else's text

When continuing a user's story or revising their chapter, build the ledger **before** writing a word:

1. **List every named character** that appears in the provided text.
2. **For every line of dialogue that contains a name**, record: speaker, target, the exact form used (surname or given name, suffix, nickname), and the chapter/section.
3. **Note conflicts.** If Hina calls Kuze "Kuze-kun" in chapter 2 and "Kuze" in chapter 5, check whether the text motivates it. If it does, record the shift and its cause. If it doesn't, ask the user which is canon, or follow the most recent usage and flag it in the post-prose notes.
4. **Note the narration's convention.** Does the author's narration use surnames, given names, or a mix? Match it exactly, even if it differs from this file's default. The user's established voice outranks the house style.
5. **Note romanization.** "Yuuto" vs. "Yuto", "Kujou" vs. "Kujo", "Ryuu" vs. "Ryu". Match the source. Never "correct" a user's romanization.
6. **Note untranslated terms.** If the user writes "senpai" lowercase, don't switch to "Senpai" mid-work, except as a direct address at the start of a line, where capitalization is standard.

Only then draft. This takes minutes and prevents the most common continuity complaint LN readers make.

## 13. Sarcastic and weaponized address

Address forms can be used **against** someone:

- **Over-politeness as contempt:** a bully calling her target "Tachibana-*sama*" or "Miss Popular" is mockery. Render it with a tilde or a pause before the suffix ("Tachibana... *sama*~").
- **"-chan" as belittlement:** from a peer who isn't close, "-chan" condescends. Reika's "Tachibana-chan~" is an insult precisely because it's cute.
- **Refusing the name:** calling someone "that girl", "him", or "you there" when their name is known is erasure. Kuze does this in his narration early on ("one of Amamiya's girls") and stops doing it as each girl becomes a person to him. That shift in the *narration's* naming is a quiet arc of its own.
- **Title as a weapon:** "Kaichou" said flatly by someone who despises the student council president becomes a sneer. "Sensei" dragged out ("Sen-*sei*~") is a student baiting a teacher.
- **The full name:** using someone's full name ("Minato Kuze.") signals seriousness, anger, or a formal declaration, like a parent calling a child in trouble. Save it for a moment that deserves it.

## 14. Address in groups of friends

Among close groups (Kuze, Daichi, and Gon, for example), address forms loosen into a private dialect: nicknames, shortened names ("Gon", "Mina"), and in-joke titles ("our esteemed director"). Outsiders stumbling onto this dialect is a comic device, and a way to show who belongs. When a girl from Amamiya's circle first uses one of these private names, even jokingly, it signals that she has crossed from one social world into another.
Track these private dialects in the story bible alongside the main ledger.
