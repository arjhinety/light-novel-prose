# Tone & Comedy

> **Purpose:** How to hold the light-novel tone (cynical but not cruel, funny through underreaction, and capable of dropping all irony at the peaks) and how to build comedy in prose rather than in panels.
> **Load when:** Drafting any scene with humor or banter, balancing a dark chapter, or revising prose whose tone feels either sneering and cold or sugary and generic.
> **Related:** [Style Bible](style-bible.md), [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md), [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md), [Description & Portraits](description-and-portraits.md), [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md), [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md), [Rom-Com & Comedy](../genres/romcom-and-comedy.md), [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md), [Anti-Robotic Pass](../revision/anti-robotic.md)

## 1. The tonal contract

The reader of a school-drama light novel signs up for three feelings at once:

1. **Comfort**: the familiar rhythm of school days, banter, bells, and archetypes they already love.
2. **Bite**: a narrator sharp enough to say the things everyone thinks and no one says.
3. **Ache**: a loneliness under the jokes that will eventually be addressed head-on.

Tone management is the art of keeping all three in play. A chapter that is pure bite reads like an internet rant. Pure comfort is filler, and pure ache is melodrama. The source novel shifts between these every few paragraphs: a petty suspicion, then a gag from a side character, then a sudden pang of self-disgust, then the bell rings.

The most important tonal rule is this:

> **The narrator is cynical about others and even more cynical about himself.**

That second half is what separates a likable misanthrope from an unbearable one. Every time Kuze sneers at Amamiya, the prose should, often within the same paragraph, turn the knife on Kuze: *Or was he just jealous?* See [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md) for how self-suspicion works as a device.

## 2. Cynical, not cruel

### 2.1 The difference

| Cynical (good) | Cruel (bad) |
|---|---|
| Suspects motives | Assumes the worst and enjoys it |
| Notices hypocrisy with precision | Mocks appearance, weakness, or pain |
| Doubts his own reading | Is never wrong |
| Aims up (at the popular, the righteous, the crowd) | Aims down (at the weak, the lonely, the bullied) |
| Has a code ("outsiders shouldn't meddle") | Has only contempt |
| Feels guilty afterwards | Feels nothing |

The source's protagonist is scathing about the harem lead's "hypocrisy" and the class's mob justice, but when he actually hurts someone (a heroine, with a cutting remark), the narration immediately registers disgust at himself. When a bullied girl is humiliated, the narrator does not find it funny. Ever. The bully's laughter is written as ugly.

### 2.2 Aim the joke up

A rule of thumb for any joke line: **who pays for the laugh?**

- Kuze deflating Amamiya's speech: fine, because it is aimed at the powerful.
- Kuze deadpanning at Rin's tsundere outburst: fine, because Rin is strong and will hit back.
- Kuze grading Daichi's pun "two points": fine, because it is a friend's game.
- The narration mocking a crying girl, or a heavy student, or someone's poverty: never, unless the *bully* is saying it and the narration frames it as vile.

### 2.3 The guilt beat

After a cutting remark, add a **short guilt beat**. Keep it one or two lines, not a paragraph of remorse:

> "Maybe think about what your parents meant when they named you."
> He regretted it before she'd even finished scowling. Not the words. The pleasure he'd taken in them.

This beat keeps the reader on his side.

## 3. Deadpan and underreaction

### 3.1 The core comic engine

The single most reliable laugh in this register is **a big reaction met with a tiny one**:

> "Kuze! You're coming to karaoke with us and that's final! I already told everyone! There's a room booked! There are *snacks*!"
> "No."
> "You can't just say no!"
> "I just did."

Hina gives four exclamations, Kuze one syllable. The rhythm difference *is* the joke. See [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md).

### 3.2 Underreaction forms

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| Monosyllable | "No." / "Done." / "Sure." |
| Literalism | "I'll try." (when told to remember her name) |
| Silent reply | `"..."`, which works as a whole comic beat |
| Pedantic correction | "It wasn't an order. It was a suggestion. The nuance matters." |
| Scoring | "Lame. Two points." |
| Tangent | He had been told off, and he was thinking about the vending machine restock schedule. |
| Narrative understatement | "The conversation went about as well as conversations with Tachibana went." |

### 3.3 Don't explain the deadpan

Wrong:
> "No," Kuze said flatly, his deadpan response a stark contrast to her energy, which made the moment comical.

Right:
> "No."

If the reader has to be told something is funny, it isn't. Cut every "comically", "hilariously", "in a humorous contrast", and "much to everyone's amusement". See [Anti-Robotic Pass](../revision/anti-robotic.md).

## 4. Tsukkomi and boke in prose

Japanese comedy runs on a duo: the **boke** (the one who says something absurd) and the **tsukkomi** (the one who calls it out). In LN prose, the tsukkomi is often the narrator himself, delivered as inner voice.

### 4.1 Spoken tsukkomi

> "I've decided to become a vampire for the haunted house," Daichi announced, green face paint drying on his cheeks.
> "Vampires aren't green."
> "Sick vampire."
> "Why would a sick vampire be scary?"
> "Because it's *contagious*, Mina."

### 4.2 Silent tsukkomi (narrator)

> Amamiya declared that friendship was the most important thing in the world, which was a bold thing to say with one girl on each arm.

The narrator's tsukkomi usually comes in the form of **a flat restatement that exposes the absurdity**. It needs no exclamation marks. The flatness is the point.

### 4.3 Rotating roles

Let the roles swap. Kuze is the tsukkomi to Hina's boke, but Daichi is the tsukkomi to Kuze's overthinking ("Mina. You wrote a *flowchart* about whether she likes you?"). A narrator who is never the butt of a joke becomes smug. See [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md).

## 5. Meta rom-com awareness

The POV **knows what genre he is in**. He has read the manga and watched the anime. When a scene conforms to a trope, he notices:

> Rin twirled a forkful of pasta, hands shaking, and held it out toward Amamiya's mouth.
> When had this turned into a rom-com? Kuze thought, a little late.

### 5.1 Rules

1. **About once per chapter.** More than that and the book becomes parody.
2. **Use it to judge, not to wink.** The trope observation should carry a point about Amamiya's behavior or the girls' feelings ("Rom-coms never show the girls who weren't chosen").
3. **Never break the fourth wall.** The narrator knows tropes as a *reader of fiction* inside the world, not as a character who knows he's in a book (unless the genre is explicitly meta; see [Isekai & Reincarnation](../genres/isekai-and-reincarnation.md)).
4. **Let reality subvert the trope.** The classic "bread in mouth, collision at the corner" scene can be staged by a character *on purpose*, which reveals her as calculating. That is meta awareness used as a plot device.

### 5.2 The rom-com thesis

The strongest meta move is a **thesis about the genre**, delivered at a key moment:

> Rom-coms always cut away after the hero chooses. Nobody draws the chapter where the other girls walk home.
> Kuze wondered, not for the first time, which of them would be walking home.

This turns genre-savviness into theme.

## 6. Running gags

Running gags are the connective tissue of a long serial. They reward the returning reader.

### 6.1 Gag types that suit the voice

| Gag | Example with the default cast |
|---|---|
| **The address correction** | Hina scolds Kuze every time he's polite: "No polite language! We're the same year!" |
| **The title claim** | Kujou calling herself Amamiya's "lawful wife" at every opportunity |
| **The violent deflection** | Rin flinging Amamiya aside whenever she blushes |
| **The scoring game** | Kuze grading Daichi's jokes, with the scores escalating absurdly ("Fifteen points. Seven times better.") |
| **The unison crowd** | `"""Guess so."""` from the background boys, who always agree |
| **The archaic friend** | Gon's "verily" appearing at the worst moments |
| **The black coffee** | Everyone commenting on Kuze drinking it black, and his refusal to explain |
| **The chorus of envy** | Boys biting handkerchiefs over Amamiya ("Why only him...!") |
| **The teacher scolded** | Ogata-sensei being told off by another teacher for her language |

### 6.2 Rules for running gags

1. **Establish in three.** A gag needs to appear three times before it is a running gag: introduce it, repeat it, then vary it.
2. **Vary the payoff.** On the fourth appearance, change something. Hina scolds, and Kuze answers in exaggerated keigo. Or Hina *doesn't* scold, and the absence is loud.
3. **Weaponize gags at the climax.** The best emotional beats reuse a gag with the comedy drained out. The day Rin doesn't hit Amamiya when she blushes is the day the reader knows something has broken. The day Daichi tells a bad joke *during* the thesis speech, and Kuze laughs through tears, is the release.
4. **Never explain a callback.** Don't write "Just like last time, when she..." Trust the reader.

## 7. Earnest peaks: dropping the irony

### 7.1 Why the drop works

A narrator who has been ironic for thirty chapters earns an enormous payoff when he stops. The contrast is the emotional mechanism. If he is sincere all the time, sincerity is free. If he is sincere once, it costs something.

### 7.2 What changes at a peak

| Normal mode | Peak mode |
|---|---|
| Hypothesis chains ("Was it...? Or...?") | Single, repeated certainties ("He wanted to help her. That was enough.") |
| Wry verdict lines | Plain statements of feeling |
| Varied rhythm | Hammering repetition ("He hated... He hated...") |
| Comic expressions | Physical facts (shoulders, tears, breath) |
| Meta commentary | None |
| Jokes | None, or one broken joke that *causes* the release |
| Long exhale sentences | Fragments |

### 7.3 Example of the shift

Normal mode:
> Amamiya had offered her a handkerchief, which he would no doubt wash and return, which would require a meeting, which was probably the point. Kuze filed it under "strategic hygiene."

Peak mode:
> He had watched. That was all he'd done. He'd watched, and called it being rational, and gone home, and eaten dinner.
> It had tasted like nothing.
> He had watched.

No jokes, no filing, no hypotheses. See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md) and [Emotional Dialogue](../dialogue/emotional-dialogue.md).

### 7.4 Returning from the peak

Coming back from a peak needs a **small, human, slightly funny beat** so the reader can breathe. The source does this with a hot canned coffee dropped in the dirt and a friend laughing at it. That's the model:

> "Hot!" He dropped the can. It rolled into the dirt.
> "Your coffee's all sandy now, Mina."
> "Your fault."

The gag is small and physical, and it restores the friendship dynamic before the serious talk. The ache stays underneath.

## 8. Tonal switching: the mid-scene pivot

### 8.1 The pivot line

Scenes often pivot from comic to dark on a single line, usually:
- A silence (`"..."`)
- A one-line paragraph of observation ("But she wasn't laughing.")
- An arrival ("The classroom door opened.")
- A sound ("Ding-dong-dang-dong." can end a comic scene *or* cut off a dark one)

### 8.2 Pivot example

> "Fifteen points! Seven times better!" Daichi high-fived the air, since Kuze hadn't raised his hand.
> "It's still a bad joke."
> "It's a *growing* joke."
> Behind them, someone laughed, too loudly, the way people laugh when they want to be overheard.
> Hoshino was sitting on Rin's desk.

The comic rhythm runs for three lines. The fourth line changes the sound quality of laughter. The fifth line is a one-line paragraph and a location, and the tone has flipped. No transition sentence is needed.

### 8.3 Don't pivot too often

Every pivot costs a little of the reader's trust. Two or three per chapter is plenty. A chapter that ping-pongs every paragraph feels unstable in a bad way.

## 9. Tone by character voice

Tone isn't only narration. Each character carries a default tonal color that the narrator reacts to:

| Character | Tonal color | Narrator's typical reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Amamiya | Earnest, righteous, oblivious | Suspicion, irritation, grudging fairness |
| Rin | Hot, prickly, fragile underneath | Deadpan provocation, then guilt |
| Kujou | Cool, amused, dangerous | Respect, unease |
| Hina | Sunny, wall-less | Discomfort, a dazzled retreat |
| Momo | Sugary, with something off | Creeping dread |
| Daichi | Goofy, then piercing | Relaxation, then exposure |
| Hoshino | Performative cruelty | Contempt, then fear, then fury |
| Ogata-sensei | Breezy, then sharp | Mild exasperation, then surprise |
| Gon | Theatrical | Tolerant amusement |

The narrator's tone **bends toward the person he's with**. With Daichi he's lighter. With Kujou he's guarded. With Hina he's flustered. This keeps a single-POV book from going monotone. See [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md) and [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md).

## 10. Comic timing on the page

### 10.1 Line breaks are beats

In prose comedy, the paragraph break is your pause. Put the punchline on its own line:

> Kujou set down her teacup and looked at him with polite interest.
> "Kuze-kun. Are you, perhaps, an idiot?"

The first line is a setup with a pause built in. The second line is the punch.

### 10.2 Rule of three, broken

Two normal items and a third that swerves:

> Amamiya was good at sports, good at talking to people, and good at not noticing that five girls had been glaring at each other over his head for the last ten minutes.

### 10.3 The delayed reaction

> "By the way, your skirt's tucked into your bag strap."
> "Thanks." Momo walked three more steps. "*Fueee?!*"

### 10.4 The button

End a comic scene on a **button**, a small, final line that closes the gag and releases the reader:

> "Settled! Everyone's going!"
> Kuze had not been asked. This, too, was settled.

## 11. Tonal failure modes

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Smugness** | The narrator is always right, never teased | Let others score on him, and give him wrong guesses |
| **Misery wallowing** | Chapter after chapter of self-hatred with no comedy | Insert Daichi, Gon, or a teacher gag, and let side characters be funny |
| **Sitcom sugar** | Everyone is nice, nothing stings | Restore the POV's suspicion, and let the bite back in |
| **Explained jokes** | "which was funny because..." | Cut the explanation |
| **Tonal whiplash** | A gag in the middle of a bullying climax | Keep peaks joke-free, and put the release after |
| **Edgelord cynicism** | Contempt for everyone, including victims | Aim up, add guilt beats, and show his code |
| **Parody drift** | Meta jokes every page | Keep to one meta beat per chapter |
| **Generic warmth** | "They laughed together, their bond stronger than ever." | Replace with a specific shared gag or silence |

## 12. Calibrating the tone mix by chapter type

Different chapters want different blends of comfort, bite, and ache. Use this as a planning dial (see [Chapter Plan](../templates/chapter-plan.md)):

| Chapter type | Comfort | Bite | Ache | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arc opener / new event | High | Medium | Low | Establish the rhythm, introduce the situation, one sting at the end |
| Heroine-bonding chapter | Medium | Low | Medium | The POV's guard drops. Comedy comes from her, not his sneering |
| Group outing (harem date, festival prep) | High | High | Low | Bite aimed at Amamiya's behavior, comfort from group chatter |
| Rumor / pressure chapter | Low | High | Medium | Jokes thin out, and the bully's tildes turn sinister |
| Low point | Very low | Low | Very high | Almost no jokes. Silence and repetition |
| Climax | None | Explosive | Very high | The outburst is bite turned into catharsis |
| Aftermath / confidant | Rising | Low | High, then easing | A small gag releases, and the thesis lands |
| Epilogue / reset | High | Medium | Low (lingering) | Return to normal, slightly changed |

A reader should be able to feel the dial turning across an arc. If every chapter has the same blend, the arc feels flat even when the plot moves. See [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md).

## 13. A full tonal sequence, annotated

The following short sequence runs through three tonal registers in about 250 words. Study where each shift happens.

> Hina slapped a flyer onto his desk. "Karaoke! Friday! Everyone's going!"
> "Everyone isn't going. I'm here, and I'm not going."
> "Then everyone minus one is going!" She beamed, as if she'd solved something.

*(Comfort and comic deadpan: Hina's energy against Kuze's flat logic.)*

> Across the room, Amamiya was already telling Kujou and Rin about it, and both of them were already saying yes, and Kuze couldn't help noticing that Hina had come to *his* desk first.
> Was that strategy? Charity? Or had Amamiya sent her, to keep the head count comfortable?

*(Bite: the analytical monologue turns a friendly act into a question.)*

> "Kuze-kun?" Hina tilted her head. "You're making the face again."
> "What face?"
> "The one where you think everyone's lying."
> "..."
> She wasn't laughing when she said it. That was the part that stayed with him.

*(Ache: a character sees through him, and the silent line and one-line observation land it.)*

> "Anyway! Friday!" She was gone before he could say no a second time.
> He looked at the flyer. Someone had drawn a small, lopsided cat on the corner.

*(Button and return to comfort, with a small, human detail as the release.)*

Four registers in four short blocks, each shift made by one line. That is the density of tonal play the voice runs on.

## 14. Genre-specific tone notes

The tonal contract above is the school-drama default. Other genres move the dials:

- **Rom-com** pushes comfort and comedy up and keeps bite light. The POV's cynicism becomes a straight-man function rather than a worldview. See [Rom-Com & Comedy](../genres/romcom-and-comedy.md).
- **Harem** needs the bite *aimed at the harem structure itself*, or at the lead, to keep the reader from finding the setup smug. See [Harem](../genres/harem.md).
- **Fantasy and isekai** often use an OP or modern-knowledge narrator whose deadpan works against the world's grandiosity: a guildmaster's dramatic speech met with "So, is that a yes on the loan?" See [Isekai & Reincarnation](../genres/isekai-and-reincarnation.md).
- **Mystery and psychological** stories strip out most comfort. The tilde and the smile become threats, and the deadpan becomes clinical observation. See [Mystery, Psychological & Horror](../genres/mystery-psychological-horror.md).
- **Romance and melodrama** keep the ache high throughout and use comedy as sunlight between rainclouds: small, domestic, fond. See [Romance & Melodrama](../genres/romance-and-melodrama.md).
- **Ecchi** comedy runs on embarrassment and slapstick timing, never leering narration, with the strict age rules in [Ecchi & Fanservice](../genres/ecchi-and-fanservice.md).

Whatever the genre, the two universal rules hold: the narrator doubts himself as much as others, and the peaks go joke-free.

A quick test for any scene: if you removed every joke, would the scene still matter? If you removed every sting, would it still be funny? If the answer to either is no, the tonal blend is doing structural work, which is good. If both answers are yes, the tone is decoration. Rewrite until the comedy and the ache depend on each other.

## 15. Checklist

- [ ] Every sneer at another character is shadowed, somewhere nearby, by self-suspicion or guilt.
- [ ] Jokes aim up or sideways, never down at the vulnerable.
- [ ] At least one deadpan underreaction per comic scene.
- [ ] No explained jokes, and no "comically" or "humorously".
- [ ] At most one meta rom-com observation per chapter, and it makes a point.
- [ ] Running gags appear, vary, and are never explained.
- [ ] Emotional peaks contain no jokes. A small human beat follows them.
- [ ] Tonal pivots are made with a single line (silence, sound, arrival), and there are no more than two or three per chapter.
- [ ] The narrator's tone bends toward whoever he's with.
- [ ] Comic scenes end on a button.

See [Rom-Com & Comedy](../genres/romcom-and-comedy.md) for genre-level comedy structure (misunderstanding engines, escalation arcs) and [Before & After](../examples/before-after.md) for tonal repairs on real drafts.
