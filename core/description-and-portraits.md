# Description & Portraits

> **Purpose:** How to describe people, faces, bodies-in-motion, and places in the economical, anime-visual way of a translated light novel, so the reader "sees the illustration" without wading through paragraphs of scenery.
> **Load when:** Introducing any character, staging any scene, or revising a passage whose description feels either thin (floating heads) or bloated (novelistic scenery).
> **Related:** [Style Bible](style-bible.md), [Narration & POV](narration-and-pov.md), [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md), [Tone & Comedy](tone-and-comedy.md), [Character Design](../cast/character-design.md), [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md), [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md), [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md), [Anti-Robotic Pass](../revision/anti-robotic.md)

---

## 1. The governing principle: people are drawn, places are sketched

A light novel is written for a reader who has absorbed a thousand anime and manga panels. That reader will fill in a classroom, a train platform, or a convenience store from a single noun. They will **not** fill in a character. The character is the thing they came for, so they need the hair, the eyes, the pose, and the one gesture that makes this girl different from the last girl.

The source novel spends almost nothing on setting: roughly one or two sentences per location, often just a label ("a family restaurant near the school, known for its cheap prices"). It spends two to five sentences on every significant character's first appearance, and then a steady drip of expression-and-gesture beats for the rest of the book.

Copy that ratio:

| Element | Budget on first appearance | Budget afterwards |
|---|---|---|
| Recurring location (classroom, club room, station) | 1-2 sentences | A noun, or a single sensory beat |
| New one-off location (a mall, a festival booth) | 2-3 sentences | Rarely revisited |
| Major character | 3-5 sentences (the portrait) | 1 expression or gesture beat per scene |
| Minor character (a waiter, a teacher in the hall) | A role plus one trait | Nothing |
| Crowd | A collective verb ("the class buzzed") | Reaction lines, not description |

The reason is the POV. The narrator is a teenager who has sat in this classroom four hundred times. He does not notice the chalkboard. He notices *who is sitting next to whom*. Description follows attention, and attention follows social stakes. See [Narration & POV](narration-and-pov.md).

## 2. The portrait formula

Every major character gets **one portrait**, delivered the first time the POV really *looks* at them. (This is often not the first time they appear. A girl can be "one of Amamiya's entourage" for a whole scene before Kuze bothers to look.)

The portrait has five parts, in roughly this order:

1. **Hair**: color, length, and one quirk.
2. **Eyes / face**: shape plus one adjective of temperament.
3. **Build or bearing**: size, posture, or how they move.
4. **Reputation**: what the school thinks of them.
5. **The crack**: the detail that contradicts or complicates 1-4.

### 2.1 Worked example: Rin Tachibana

> Her silver-ash hair was cut in a short bob that stopped just above her jaw, as if she'd told the stylist to take off anything that might get in the way. Her gray eyes were sharp enough to make first-years step aside in the hallway, and she walked the way people from the kendo club walked, back straight, heels barely touching the floor.
> Half the boys in the second year were afraid of her. The other half wanted to be.
> And yet, when she grinned, one canine showed, small and pointed, and for a second she looked less like a swordswoman than like a kid who had just gotten away with something.

Count the parts: hair (1), eyes (2), bearing (3), reputation (4), and the crack (5). The crack is doing the most work, because it tells the reader *there is more to her*, which is exactly the promise a heroine intro has to make.

### 2.2 Worked example: Sayo Kujou

> Sayo Kujou's black hair fell straight to her waist without a single strand out of place, and her almond-shaped eyes had the calm of someone who had never once in her life been in a hurry.
> She was the daughter of some company whose name appeared on the side of trucks. She had the grades and the posture to match, and the teachers addressed her with a politeness they didn't use on anyone else.
> The problem was her mouth.
> Kujou could take a person apart in three sentences, calmly, without raising her voice, and then thank them for their time.

Notice the paragraphing. The crack gets its own one-line paragraph ("The problem was her mouth."). That is the LN rhythm doing descriptive work: a short line turns the portrait. See [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md).

### 2.3 Worked example: Hina Matsuri

> Hina Matsuri was the kind of girl people described with their hands. *Oh, you know, the one with the ponytail,* they'd say, and bounce their fingers to show how it swung.
> Honey-brown hair, tied high. Big, round eyes that never seemed to blink quite enough. She was a head shorter than most of the class, and somehow always the loudest person in it.
> She was called the school's idol, half as a joke and half not.
> What made Kuze uneasy wasn't any of that. It was that she talked to everyone the same way: the principal, the lunch lady, the boy who ate alone by the window. As if walls were something other people had.

Here the order is shuffled (reputation first, then hair), and the crack comes from *the POV's discomfort*. Shuffling is fine. What matters is that all five beats are there and the last one tilts.

### 2.4 Portrait anti-patterns

| Don't | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Catalogue every feature: hair, eyes, nose, lips, height, cup size, legs | It reads like a character-creator menu. It is also the most "AI" move there is. | Pick three physical details and one behavioral detail. |
| "She was beautiful." | It carries no information, and every heroine is beautiful. | Show *how* the beauty operates ("first-years stepped aside"). |
| Describe the portrait in the middle of a fast dialogue exchange | It kills the momentum. | Deliver it on a lull: a silence, a first look, or the POV being called out. |
| Use a mirror scene for the POV's own appearance | It is a cliché, and this POV doesn't care what he looks like. | Let others comment on him, or state it flatly ("Ordinary height, ordinary face, ordinary grades."). |
| Give the portrait without reputation | It loses the social layer, and this genre is about social position. | Always say what the school thinks. |
| Omit the crack | It makes a flat archetype. | Always add the contradiction. See [Character Design](../cast/character-design.md). |

### 2.5 The protagonist's self-portrait

The cynical POV describes himself **flatly and quickly, as a list of averages**, early in chapter 1:

> Minato Kuze. Second year, Class 2-3. Go-home club. Grades in the upper-middle of the pack, face in the middle-middle. Born to an ordinary family, living an ordinary life, with the single distinguishing feature of being unable to take anything at face value.

This is not self-pity. It is the deadpan inventory of a person who has already decided he is a background character, and the reader will spend the book watching him be wrong about that.

## 3. Anime expression shorthand

In this register, expressions are **codified**. A reader who sees "she puffed out her cheeks" gets a full panel: pouting, childish annoyance, probably not really angry. That is a feature. Use the shorthand confidently instead of inventing literary alternatives that the reader has to decode.

### 3.1 The expression lexicon

| Shorthand | What it signals | Typical character |
|---|---|---|
| Cheeks puffed out | Pouting, playful sulk | Genki, airhead, imouto |
| Flush that reaches the ears | Embarrassment she can't hide | Tsundere, dandere |
| Face turned beet red / "as if on fire" | Overwhelm (comic) | Anyone caught out |
| Half-lidded eyes | Smug, teasing, unimpressed | Onee-san, bully queen, kuudere |
| Narrowed eyes / a cold glance | Suspicion, a threat | Ojou, rival |
| Eyes sparkling | Delight, fandom | Genki, otaku |
| Eyes that "didn't smile" | A smile hiding hostility | Two-faced girl, mastermind |
| Canine flash in a grin | Wild or mischievous charm | Tsundere, tomboy |
| Finger raised to the lips | "Secret," or teasing | Idol, onee-san |
| Hands clasped together, head bowed | Apology or a plea ("Pretty please!") | Genki |
| Arms making an X | "No!" (cute refusal) | Genki, idol |
| Hand on the chin, head bowed | Deep thought | The cynical POV |
| Wry smile / scratching the cheek | Awkward, forgiving amusement | Kind heroine, harem lead |
| Smile "like a flower blooming" | Pure happiness | Airhead, idol |
| Twirling a lock of hair | Coyness or nerves | Two-faced girl, dandere |
| A vein throbbing / a twitching eyebrow | Comic anger | Tsundere, the straight man |
| Shoulders jumping | Startled, afraid | Victim, shy girl |
| Lifeless eyes | Despair (serious) | Bullied heroine at the low point |

### 3.2 Rules for using the shorthand

1. **Assign signature expressions.** Rin gets the canine grin, the ear-flush, and the throbbing eyebrow. Kujou gets the unsmiling eyes and the slow blink. Hina gets the sparkle, the X-arms, and the puffed cheeks. Momo gets the flower-bloom smile *and*, later, the eyes that don't smile. If everybody puffs their cheeks, nobody has a face.
2. **One expression beat per exchange, max.** An expression on every line of dialogue is noise.
3. **Put the expression before the line when it tells us who is speaking**, and after the line when it undercuts what they said. Either way, the expression gets **its own paragraph** (R1):
   > Rin's ears had gone red.
   >
   > "I-It's not like I was waiting for you."

   > "I wasn't waiting."
   >
   > She said it to the wall. Her ears said otherwise.
4. **Serious scenes use the same vocabulary with less of it.** At a climax, you don't write "her face turned beet red." You write "Her shoulders shook." Fewer, heavier beats. See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md).
5. **The POV narrates expressions as evidence.** Kuze doesn't just see Rin blush. He files it: *Ears red. So she does care about the lunch invitation. Or about who's inviting.* See [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md).

## 4. Body-language tags (gesture as speaker attribution)

In LN prose, **gesture sentences replace "said"**. A gesture like *Rin slammed both hands on his desk.* carries the attribution, the emotion, and the staging all at once. It always sits in **its own paragraph**, never glued to the quote (the pure-dialogue rule, R1; see [Punctuation & Typography](punctuation-and-typography.md#91-pure-dialogue-lines-r1)).

### 4.1 The pattern

```
<Gesture paragraph naming the speaker.>

"<Line.>"
```
or
```
"<Line.>"

<Gesture paragraph naming the speaker, or reacting.>
```

Example:

> Hina clapped her hands together, loud enough to make the boys in the back row jump.
>
> "Okay! Then everyone's coming to karaoke, yup?"
>
> "I'm not."
>
> Kuze didn't look up from his book.
>
> "Everyone except Kuze-kun, yup!"

The first paragraph is a gesture placed *before* the line. The third line has no tag, and the gesture that follows it (*didn't look up from his book*) attributes it after the fact while adding the POV's deadpan. The last line needs nothing, because the voice is unmistakable. That is three different attribution strategies in five paragraphs, with zero "said", and the variety is part of what keeps it from sounding mechanical. See [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md).

### 4.2 Gesture banks per archetype

- **Tsundere (Rin):** slams hands on desks, grabs collars, flings the harem lead aside, looks away sharply, crosses arms, stomps off, and comes back.
- **Ojou (Kujou):** raises a teacup or a pen, tilts her head a few degrees, covers her mouth with the back of her fingers to laugh, and folds her hands on the desk like a board chair.
- **Genki (Hina):** trots, peers into faces from below, bounces on her heels, hugs arms, jabs a finger in the air, and gives a thumbs-up.
- **Airhead / two-faced (Momo):** trips, clutches her phone to her chest, tugs sleeves, snuggles up to a shoulder, and angles the phone for a selfie.
- **Harem lead (Amamiya):** scratches the back of his head, pats heads, puts hands on girls' shoulders to steer them, and stretches after exiting a building.
- **Cynical POV (Kuze):** props an elbow on the table, rests his chin in his hand, looks out the window, counts passing cars, trails a step behind, takes a step back, and smooths out a wrinkled collar.
- **Confidant (Daichi):** tosses canned drinks, sits down without asking, bumps shoulders, and doesn't look at Kuze while talking seriously.
- **Bully queen (Hoshino):** sits on desks, props her feet on chairs, slaps shoulders and heads "playfully", claps to start chants, and pulls hair.

### 4.3 Spatial choreography as meaning

The source does something subtle and effective: it **narrates physical distance as social distance**. When the harem lead and two girls start chatting, the POV "took a step back" and "trailed behind." At a family restaurant, the pair sits on one side and the POV sits alone on the other. On the walk home he walks at the rear, counting cars.

Use space deliberately:

> Amamiya and Hina fell into step side by side, their voices already folding into one of those conversations that didn't have room for a third person.
> Kuze slowed. One step. Two.
> Nobody noticed the gap open, which was, he supposed, the point of gaps.

Seat charts, walking order, who stands in the doorway, who sits on the desk: all of it is description *and* characterization at once. Track it the way you'd track honorifics.

## 5. Scenery economy

### 5.1 The one-line set

Most locations need one line, sometimes two, and the line should carry **a mood or a social fact**, not just furniture.

- Weak: "The classroom had rows of desks and a blackboard at the front."
- Strong: "After school, the classroom emptied in the usual order: club kids first, then couples, then everyone else."
- Weak: "The family restaurant was brightly lit and had booth seating."
- Strong: "It was the family restaurant every student within three stations used, because the drink bar was unlimited and the staff had long since given up on shooing anyone out."

### 5.2 Sensory choice: one sense, the right one

Pick **one** sense per location and make it specific. The cheerful bell. The smell of floor wax in the empty hall. The hum of a vending machine behind the gym. The squeak of indoor shoes. The cicadas in July. A single sensory anchor per scene makes the place real. Three per scene makes it a creative-writing exercise.

### 5.3 Weather is emotional punctuation

The source ends a devastating day with the protagonist at home, staring at the sky: overcast and cloudy. Weather in this register is **pathetic fallacy used without apology**, but it is placed as a short closing beat, never as an opening paragraph of meteorology.

> He didn't go to school the next day.
> The sky outside his window was the flat gray of a whiteboard nobody had bothered to clean.

### 5.4 Time stamps instead of transitions

Replace "Later that day, after classes had ended and most of the students had gone to their clubs..." with a stamp:

> After school--

or

> The next morning--

The dash signals the cut and the reader supplies the rest. See [Punctuation & Typography](punctuation-and-typography.md) and [Chapter & Section](../structure/chapter-and-section.md).

### 5.5 When to spend more on setting

Spend more only when the setting **is** the plot:
- A place is new and the POV is anxious in it (a heroine's mansion, a mixer, a festival crowd).
- The place will return as a callback, so plant one memorable detail (the booth by the window, the drink bar that only has melon soda left).
- A genre shift demands it (fantasy, isekai). See [Fantasy](../genres/fantasy.md) and [Isekai & Reincarnation](../genres/isekai-and-reincarnation.md), where worldbuilding needs a larger but still disciplined budget.

## 6. Description of objects: the telling prop

Objects get described when they carry **evidence**. The source makes a whole chapter's worth of paranoia out of a menu order: pastas that can be shared versus a hamburger that can't. That is the model. Props are clues.

| Prop | What the POV reads from it |
|---|---|
| Who ordered what | Intimacy, sharing, exclusion |
| A handkerchief lent and "washed and returned" | A pretext for a second meeting |
| A CD / file / notebook passed via an intermediary | Gatekeeping, possessiveness |
| A canned coffee tossed to him | Friendship without words |
| A hairpin bought and immediately worn | Performance of affection |
| Papers taped to a desk | Bullying designed to leave no trace |
| A phone angled for photos | Social-media motive |
| An uneaten dessert in the trash | Hollowness behind the post |

Describe the prop plainly, then let the POV interpret it. Never have the narrator announce that it is symbolic.

## 7. Describing the harem lead: the unreliable portrait

The harem lead (Amamiya) gets a portrait that is **deliberately filtered through envy**:

> Haruto Amamiya was, by any fair measure, a decent-looking guy. Tall enough. Hair that looked like it had been styled without being styled. A laugh that carried across the room, which it did often.
> None of that explained it.
> None of it explained why five of the most talked-about girls in the second year orbited him like he'd been assigned as their homeroom.

The POV insists that the lead's appearance *doesn't* explain his popularity. That insistence is the character question of the whole story. Portraits can be arguments.

## 8. Description at emotional peaks

At a climax, description **shrinks and hardens**:

- Adjectives drop out. Verbs and body parts remain: "Her shoulders shook. Tears hit the desk."
- Expressions shift from comic shorthand to plain, physical fact.
- Space narrows: the POV sees one face, one hand, one object (a marker cap on the floor).
- A single, strange detail can freeze the moment: the ink stain on a skirt, a chair still rocking after being kicked.

> The marker rolled off the desk and clicked against the floor.
> No one picked it up.

See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md) and [Emotional Dialogue](../dialogue/emotional-dialogue.md).

## 9. Describing the POV character's own body

The cynical POV reports his own body **only when it betrays him**, meaning when feelings he claims not to have show up physically:

- "His cheeks warmed, slightly. He took a step back."
- "He realized his fists were clenched."
- "Something lodged in his throat."
- "He noticed he was crying."

Using "noticed" and "realized" here is correct: the narrator is always a beat behind his own body, which is the whole point of his character. Elsewhere, cut "noticed" as a filter word (see [Anti-Robotic Pass](../revision/anti-robotic.md)). Here it is characterization.

## 10. Fanservice and body description

Portraits describe **hair, eyes, bearing, expression, and reputation**. They do not linger on bodies. The source uses at most a phrase ("a well-developed figure") and moves on. Any content that sexualizes characters must follow the hard rule in [Ecchi & Fanservice](../genres/ecchi-and-fanservice.md): suggestive framing is only for unambiguous adults, and never for high-school characters. For school casts, keep physical description to what an anime character sheet would show in a school uniform.

## 11. Re-description: keeping portraits alive after the intro

A portrait is delivered once, but the character has to stay visible for a hundred chapters. Three techniques keep them alive without repeating the portrait:

1. **The anchor detail.** Pick one element of the portrait to recur, such as Rin's canine, Kujou's unmoving hair, or Hina's ponytail. Mention it when the character's mood changes: "Hina's ponytail, for once, didn't swing." The anchor detail becomes an emotional barometer.
2. **The changed detail.** When a character goes through something, alter one piece of their visual signature: dark circles under Rin's eyes during the bullying arc, Kujou's hair tied back for the first time, Momo without her phone. One change is louder than a paragraph of explanation.
3. **The outsider's eye.** Let a side character re-describe someone the POV has stopped seeing: a first-year gasping at Kujou in the hall, or fanboys fawning over Hina. It reminds the reader of the reputation layer without the POV having to care.

## 12. Checklist

Before you move on from a scene, confirm:

- [ ] Every major character's first real look includes a full portrait (hair, eyes, bearing, reputation, crack).
- [ ] Each character has 2-4 signature expressions and gestures, and they are not shared.
- [ ] No more than one expression beat per exchange.
- [ ] At least some dialogue is attributed by gesture rather than "said".
- [ ] Locations get 1-2 lines carrying mood or social fact, not furniture.
- [ ] One sensory anchor per scene, and it is specific.
- [ ] Props that matter are described plainly and interpreted by the POV.
- [ ] Physical distance tracks social distance (steps back, seating, walking order).
- [ ] At climaxes, description gets shorter and harder, not more ornate.
- [ ] No lingering body description, and fanservice rules are observed.
