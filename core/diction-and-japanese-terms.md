# Diction & Japanese Terms

> **Purpose:** Set the word-level register of light-novel English (plain, contemporary, lightly colloquial) and provide a large working glossary of Japanese terms, school vocabulary, onomatopoeia, and interjections, each marked **KEEP**, **TRANSLATE**, or **AVOID**, with usage notes.
> **Load when:** Choosing words for any draft, deciding whether to leave a Japanese term untranslated, writing sound effects or interjections, or revising prose that sounds either too Western-generic or too weeb-sprinkled.
> **Related:** [Style Bible](style-bible.md), [Punctuation & Typography](punctuation-and-typography.md), [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md), [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md), [School Life](../setting/school-life.md), [MTL vs. Natural](../revision/mtl-vs-natural.md), [Anti-Robotic Pass](../revision/anti-robotic.md), [Genre Index](../genres/genre-index.md)

## 1. The register in one paragraph

Light-novel English is **plain, contemporary, and conversational, with Japanese texture at the edges**. The narration uses contractions and everyday words ("He didn't get it", not "He did not comprehend it"). Dialogue uses teen speech ("Seriously?!", "No way", "Got it, got it"). A small, stable set of Japanese words stays untranslated because English has no equivalent that carries the same social information: honorifics, senpai, the culture-specific school systems, and a few foods and customs. Everything else is translated into natural English. The result should feel like *Japan, told by someone fluent in both languages*, not like a textbook and not like a fan who just learned twenty words.

## 2. Register rules

### 2.1 Narration

1. **Contractions everywhere**, including in narration: *didn't, wasn't, he'd, it'd, that's.* Uncontracted narration reads as stiff translation. Use full forms only for emphasis ("He did *not* want to go.").
2. **Short, common words beat Latinate ones**: *get* over *obtain*, *see* over *perceive*, *start* over *commence*, *help* over *assist*, *seem* over *appear to be*.
3. **Idioms in moderation.** A few native idioms ("third wheel", "left a bad taste in his mouth", "out of the blue") make the translation feel fluent. A dense pack of them feels American-sitcom.
4. **No British/American slang markers that break setting**: no "y'all", no "mate", no "bloody", no baseball metaphors for dating unless a character is a baseball kid.
5. **The narrator's vocabulary is slightly more formal than the dialogue's**, like that of a smart kid who reads. Kuze can think "hypocrisy", "pretext", "possessive", and "status quo". He would not think "henceforth", "ergo", or "verily". That last word belongs to Gon.
6. **Filter words sparingly.** Avoid *felt that, seemed to, began to, started to, couldn't help but* as reflexive padding. The source leans on "couldn't help but" (about 8 times in 100 chapters is already the ceiling). See [Anti-Robotic Pass](../revision/anti-robotic.md).

### 2.2 Dialogue

1. **Teen voice, not adult voice.** "Seriously?!" not "Are you serious?" "No way." not "That is impossible." "My bad." not "I apologize."
2. **Each character has a lexicon** (see [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md)). Rin swears lightly ("idiot", "jerk", "what the hell"). Kujou never swears, and her insults are polite ("How very like you."). Hina uses "yup", "totally", and nicknames.
3. **Sentence-final particles are translated into English sounds, not transliterated.** *-yo* becomes emphasis or "you know". *-ne* becomes "right?" or "huh?". *-na* becomes "huh" or "man". *-wa* (feminine softener) becomes a gentler phrasing. Never write "Sugoi ne~!" in an English book unless a character is code-switching as a gag.
4. **Stutters and trailing dots are dialogue-only.** See [Punctuation & Typography](punctuation-and-typography.md).
5. **Mild profanity only**: *damn, hell, crap, jerk, idiot, scum, trash*. Harsher swearing clashes with the teen-drama register unless a delinquent character owns it.

## 3. The KEEP / TRANSLATE / AVOID framework

For any Japanese term, ask three questions:

1. **Does English have a word that carries the same social meaning?** If yes, translate. ("Classroom" for *kyoushitsu*: obviously translate.)
2. **Is the untranslated word already familiar to LN/anime readers and does it carry nuance?** If yes, keep. ("Senpai" carries hierarchy plus affection. "Upperclassman" loses the affection.)
3. **Would keeping it feel like decoration rather than meaning?** If yes, avoid. ("Kawaii" in narration is decoration.)

Mark every term in your story bible if you have any doubt. Consistency matters more than which choice you make: pick "cultural festival" and never switch to "bunkasai" in the next chapter.

## 10. Names

1. **Order:** Given name then surname in English narration on first introduction ("Minato Kuze"), then surname alone in narration. This is the modern LN translation standard. Pick one order and never mix.
2. **Narration uses surnames** for everyone except characters the POV is genuinely intimate with, and even then the narration often keeps surnames while dialogue shifts. The shift in narration from "Tachibana" to "Rin" is a major event.
3. **Name meanings as plot.** The source uses a kanji breakdown of a heroine's name as a barb. Design names with meanings you can use: Kuze (九世, "nine generations", a weight of family expectations) or Hina (雛, "chick", small and loud). Keep the explanations short and in-world.
4. **Avoid famous-character names** (no "Haruhi", no "Asuna") and avoid names whose romanization is ambiguous or unpronounceable to English readers without reason.
5. **Long vowels:** pick a convention (Kujou, Kujō, or Kujo) and hold it. This library uses "ou" (Kujou, Seiryou) because it's typeable and common in web translations.
6. **Nicknames** follow character voice: Hina makes "-cchi" nicknames, Daichi calls Kuze "Mina", fanboys use "-tan", and the bully uses "-chan" sarcastically. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Kawaii", "sugoi", "baka" in narration | Weeb decoration | English equivalents |
| "Senpai noticed me" jokes | Meme, dated | Actual scene beats |
| "Waifu", "best girl" (in narration) | Fandom vocabulary | Only in a character's otaku dialogue |
| "Uwu", "owo" | Internet speak | Nothing |
| "Desu" tacked onto English | Meme | Nothing |

## 12. Explaining a term without a footnote

LN readers tolerate unfamiliar terms if the context teaches them. Three techniques:

1. **An appositive clause on first use:**
   > He was in the "go-home club", which was what people called not being in a club at all.
2. **A character asks:**
   > "What's a chuunibyou?"
   > "Someone who thinks their right arm has a sealed demon in it," said Kujou. "Gon-kun, for example."
3. **Show the practice:**
   > Kuze and Hina were on class duty today, since their attendance numbers were next to each other. So the two of them were the ones wiping the blackboard, emptying the bins, and writing up the class log.

Never footnote, and never use translator's notes (T/N). They are the most MTL signal of all. See [MTL vs. Natural](../revision/mtl-vs-natural.md).

## 14. Word-level voice: the POV's personal lexicon

Beyond Japanese terms, the cynical POV has **pet words** that recur and build his voice. Design five to ten for any POV character and track them in the [Story Bible](../templates/story-bible.md). For Kuze:

| Pet word / phrase | Use |
|---|---|
| "rational" / "irrational" | His self-justification, which becomes the thing he must break at the climax |
| "pretext" | What he suspects every kind act is |
| "the ledger" | His mental accounting of favors owed |
| "the sediment" | His leitmotif for sour feelings (see [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md)) |
| "hypocrite" | His ultimate verdict word, saved for when it lands |
| "entourage" | His dismissive word for Amamiya's girls, which should soften over the arc into their names |
| "status quo" | What he thinks everyone is protecting |
| "background character" / "mob" | His self-image |
| "meddling" | His word for others' uninvited kindness |
| "fine" | His word for things that are not fine |

**The vocabulary arc:** track how the POV's pet words change as he does. When Kuze stops calling them "the entourage" and starts using names, that shift *is* character development, delivered at the word level. When "rational" disappears from his narration for three chapters after the climax, the reader will feel it even without noticing why.

**Other characters' pet words** work the same way: Amamiya's "everyone" (he always wants *everyone* together), Kujou's "Indeed", Hoshino's "Seriously~", and Daichi's "Mina". A character's pet word appearing in *another* character's mouth signals influence: Rin says "rational" sarcastically at Kuze, which means she's been listening.

## 16. Handling a code-switching character

Sometimes a character *should* use Japanese words in English prose: a returnee who mixes languages, a foreign exchange student, an otaku who says "kawaii" ironically, or a character in an isekai who clings to Japanese as a memory of home. Rules:

1. **It's characterization, so it must be noticed.** Another character reacts ("Did you just say 'sugoi' out loud?"), or the narrator notes it.
2. **Keep it to one or two words per scene** from that character, and always context-clear.
3. **Never let the habit bleed into narration or other characters.**
4. **Track it in the bible** as a speech tic, like any other. See [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md).
5. **Pay it off.** A code-switcher who, in a vulnerable moment, drops the habit and speaks plainly delivers a strong emotional beat. The sudden absence of their verbal signature tells the reader that the mask is off, with no narration needed.

## 17. Checklist

- [ ] Narration is contracted, plain, and modern. Dialogue is teen-colloquial per character.
- [ ] Every Japanese term used is on the KEEP list or has a deliberate reason to be there.
- [ ] No "baka / sugoi / kawaii / nani" left untranslated.
- [ ] Interjections are used as emotional texture (Haa, Tch, Eh, Nn), with character-specific ones reserved for their owners.
- [ ] Sound-effect lines are in English phonetics and stand alone.
- [ ] The name order and long-vowel convention are consistent.
- [ ] Terms are explained in-world, never by footnote or T/N.
- [ ] No therapy-speak, corporate-speak, or fandom memes in narration.
