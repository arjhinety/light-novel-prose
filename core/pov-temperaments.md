# POV Temperaments

> **Purpose:** How to re-aim the analytical inner monologue for POV characters who are not cynical loners (a sincere harem lead, a genki narrator, an anxious narrator, a schemer, a stoic observer) while keeping the LN rhythm intact.
> **Load when:** The POV character is anything other than the default suspicious loner. This includes fan fiction whose canon lead is earnest, harem stories told from the lead's side, comedies with a hyper narrator, and villain or mastermind POV sections.
> **Related:** [Style Bible](style-bible.md), [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md), [Narration & POV](narration-and-pov.md), [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md), [Tone & Comedy](tone-and-comedy.md), [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md), [Harem](../genres/harem.md), [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md), [Fan Fiction & Canon](../adaptation/fanfic-and-canon.md), [Revision Checklist](../revision/revision-checklist.md)

## 1. Why this file exists

The library's default voice was built around one kind of narrator: a quiet, suspicious boy who reads the room like a crime scene. That narrator produces the voice's signature engine. He notices a small asymmetry, quotes it back, spins hypotheses, suspects himself, and lands a verdict. See [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md).

When writers applied the library to a story whose lead is **sincere** (a harem lead who genuinely loves everyone around him), the engine broke in one of two ways:

1. **The writer kept the cynicism.** The lead started suspecting the girls who love him. That's out of character, and in fan fiction it's a canon violation.
2. **The writer dropped the engine entirely.** The prose drifted into a generic Western voice: long, warm, reflective sentences, em-dash asides, and an omniscient glow. The LN texture vanished, and so did the rhythm.

Both failures come from the same mistake. They treat the engine's *target* as if it were the engine itself.

**The engine is attention.** It's the habit of catching tiny, specific details and turning them over. The cynic aims that attention at motives and hypocrisy. A sincere lead aims it at effort and hidden feelings. A genki narrator aims it at fun and escalation. An anxious narrator aims it at threats. A schemer aims it at leverage.

**Everything else stays the same.** That means the short beats, the one-line paragraphs, the pure dialogue lines, the silent `"..."` beats, the numbered sections, the 40-55% dialogue share, and the verdict line at the end of a spiral. Only the aim and the flavor of the verdicts change.

## 2. The five-part engine, re-aimed

The engine from [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md) has five steps. Here is what each step becomes under each temperament.

| Step | Cynical loner | Sincere lead | Genki | Anxious | Schemer | Stoic observer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Notice** | An asymmetry in who gets what | A small effort someone hid | Something that could be *more fun* | A sign of danger or disapproval | A weakness or an opening | A deviation from pattern |
| **2. Quote back** | The exact suspicious word | The exact word that gave the feeling away | The funniest word | The word that might mean *they hate me* | The word that reveals a want | The word that doesn't fit |
| **3. Hypotheses** | Motive A? B? Just jealousy? | She did it for me? For herself? Both? | What if we also...? And then...? | Is she angry? Did I...? What if...? | If I push here, then...? | Cause A. Cause B. Insufficient data. |
| **4. Self-check** | Or is that my envy talking? | Am I reading too much into it because I *want* it? | Wait, is this too much? Nah. | Or am I overthinking again? (Yes.) | Would she see through it? | Irrelevant. Noted. |
| **5. Verdict** | *Hypocrite.* | *She's amazing.* / *I'll remember this.* | *Perfect. Doing it.* | *I'm doomed.* | *Checkmate. Eventually.* | *Interesting.* |

The self-check step is what keeps each temperament human. Without it, the cynic is cruel, the sincere lead is saccharine, the genki narrator is exhausting, the anxious narrator is a caricature, and the schemer is a cartoon villain.

## 3. Temperament 1: the cynical loner (the default)

**Engine:** motive-reading. He sees social behavior as transactions and tallies them.

**Verdict style:** short, cold, often one word, and frequently turned back on himself.

**Rhythm notes:** he has the tersest dialogue in the cast. His inner monologue runs long, while what he says out loud runs two words.

The full treatment is in [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md) and [Style Bible](style-bible.md). This file covers the other temperaments.

**Failure modes:**
- **Cruelty without self-doubt.** If he never suspects himself, he becomes unlikeable.
- **Constant verdicts.** Ration them to 1-3 per chapter. A verdict at the end of every paragraph turns the voice into a lecture.

## 4. Temperament 2: the sincere harem lead

This is the temperament the test runs got wrong most often, so it gets the most space.

**Who:** a lead who loves openly and without irony, usually several people at once. He says what he feels, often at embarrassing length. His defining trait is that **he notices**. He sees the burnt cookie hidden under the good ones, the knitting row redone four times, and the one word she said too quickly.

**Engine:** effort-reading and feeling-reading. Where the cynic asks *what does she want from this?*, the sincere lead asks *what did this cost her?*

**The quote-back** is aimed at the word that gave her away:

> Not *it was on sale*.
>
> *I redid the same row four times.*
>
> That was the real sentence. The rest was wrapping paper.

**Hypotheses** stay in the engine, and they matter. He doesn't know for certain what she feels. He guesses, second-guesses, and then chooses the kindest reading. Sincerity is a decision he makes, not a lack of perception.

**The self-check** is where he stays honest:

> Was he reading too much into a scarf?
>
> Maybe. Probably.
>
> He decided to read into it anyway.

**Verdicts** are warm and specific. They are never generic. A sincere-lead verdict names something only *this* girl did:

- WRONG: *She was so sweet.*
- RIGHT: *Nobody else on earth would burn cookies for him at five in the morning.*

**Rhythm notes:**
- His *spoken* lines can run long. Big declarations are part of the archetype. His *narration* must stay short-beat, like everyone else's. The warmth goes into what he says and what he notices, not into longer narrative sentences.
- Comedy comes from the girls. He is the straight man who never gets cynical, and what's funny is how completely he means everything.
- The silent `"..."` belongs to the girls when he says something too sincere to answer.

**Failure modes:**
1. **The Western glow.** Long, reflective narration, em-dash asides, and paragraphs about love in the abstract. Cut it back to short beats and concrete details. See [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md).
2. **Generic praise.** "You're all amazing" is not a verdict. Every girl gets her own specific detail.
3. **Suspicion leak.** The cynic's engine reappears and he starts doubting their love. Don't do this unless the story is deliberately about that crack.
4. **The unequal camera.** One girl gets three noticed details and another gets none. In a harem story, the attention *is* the love, so distribute it. See [Harem](../genres/harem.md).
5. **The speech that replaces the scene.** If his big declaration runs longer than the comedy that earned it, the chapter tips from sincere into sermon. Earn the speech with at least two escalations first.

### 4.1 Worked example: the sincere lead

An original cast, for illustration. **Souta Minase** is a second-year who has, through a series of absurd events, ended up dating three girls with everyone's full knowledge and consent. **Aoi Kirishima** is a tsundere on the track team. **Koharu Nanase** is a near-silent girl who talks through her sketchbook. **Mei Tokiwa** is a genki girl who treats every problem as a festival.

The situation: all three have secretly made him a birthday cake. All three cakes arrive at once.

> Three cakes sat on the table.
>
> Souta looked at them. He counted them again. The number didn't change.
>
> "...Surprise?"
>
> "SURPRISE!"
>
> "I-It's not a surprise! I just had extra flour! It happens!"
>
> Koharu said nothing. She turned her sketchbook around.
>
> *Happy birthday.* Underneath, in smaller letters: *sorry, there are three.*
>
> Mei's cake was enormous. It had four layers, sparklers, and a fondant Souta riding a fondant dolphin. It leaned very slightly to the left, like it was listening to something.
>
> Aoi's cake was square and plain and covered in white frosting. There were no decorations at all, except for one strawberry in the exact center.
>
> Koharu's cake was the smallest. It was the size of a teacup. On top, piped in chocolate, was a tiny drawing of the classroom window he sat beside.
>
> "..."
>
> Souta didn't say anything for a moment.
>
> One strawberry. Exactly in the center.
>
> Aoi didn't do exact. Aoi did fast. She finished the hundred meters before most people finished getting set. So why had she measured?
>
> He looked closer. There were faint marks in the frosting around the strawberry. Four tiny dents, evenly spaced.
>
> She'd used a ruler.
>
> "...You measured the center."
>
> "I did NOT."
>
> "There are ruler marks."
>
> "Those are... decorative! Decorative ruler marks! It's a style!"
>
> Was he reading too much into four dents in frosting?
>
> Maybe.
>
> He decided to read into it anyway.
>
> "Aoi. Thank you for measuring."
>
> "..."
>
> "I said I didn't--!"
>
> "I know. Thank you anyway."
>
> Aoi's face went red all the way to her ears. She turned and glared at the dolphin.
>
> "Hey, hey, hey! Do mine next! Mine has sparklers! Mine has a DOLPHIN!"
>
> "Mei, your cake is leaning."
>
> "It's *dynamic*!"
>
> "It's leaning toward the window."
>
> "Then it's dynamic toward the window!"
>
> Koharu's sketchbook turned again.
>
> *The dolphin is going to fall.*
>
> "The dolphin is NOT going to fall!"
>
> The dolphin fell.
>
> It dropped off the top layer, bounced once on the table, and landed face-first in Aoi's frosting, directly beside the strawberry.
>
> "..."
>
> "..."
>
> "MY CENTER!"
>
> "MY DOLPHIN!"
>
> Souta picked up the fondant dolphin. He looked at it. Fondant Souta was still riding it, grinning, completely unbothered.
>
> Then he looked at Koharu's tiny cake, which had survived everything, and at the chocolate window on top.
>
> It was his window. The third one from the back. She'd even drawn the crack in the corner of the glass, the one nobody else noticed.
>
> She sat two rows behind him. She'd been looking at that window for a year.
>
> Or she'd been looking at him, and the window happened to be in the way.
>
> He wasn't sure which. He decided he didn't need to be.
>
> "Koharu."
>
> The sketchbook came up halfway, like a shield.
>
> "You drew the crack."
>
> The sketchbook came up the rest of the way.
>
> Behind it, very faintly, a voice he'd heard maybe ten times in his life.
>
> "...You always look at it. When you're thinking."
>
> "..."
>
> Now it was Souta's turn to have nothing to say.

**What the example shows:**
- **The engine runs at full strength.** It notices the ruler dents, quotes back *you measured*, runs a hypothesis chain (why would a sprinter measure?), self-checks (*reading too much?*), and decides on the kind reading.
- **Every girl gets one specific noticed detail:** the ruler marks, the leaning dolphin (her enthusiasm, which is also her detail), and the crack in the glass.
- **Narration stays short.** No sentence runs past twenty words. The warmth lives in what he notices.
- **Every dialogue paragraph is pure.** Speakers are clear from voice (Mei's caps and exclamation, Aoi's stutter and denial) or from a narration paragraph of their own before the line.
- **The comedy escalates** (three cakes, the ruler, the lean, the fall) before the sincere beat lands, so the speech is earned. Here the "speech" is only two lines, which is often best.
- **The final `"..."` belongs to him.** When a sincere lead is left speechless, it's a small reversal the reader enjoys.

## 5. Temperament 3: the genki narrator

**Who:** a hyperactive, optimistic POV who treats life as an event. It's common in comedy, idol, and club stories.

**Engine:** possibility-reading. She notices what could be *more*: more fun, more people, more noise.

**Hypothesis chains** escalate instead of doubting:

> What if the whole class came? What if we made it a festival? What if there were *fireworks*?
>
> Okay. Maybe not fireworks. The principal still remembered the fireworks.
>
> Sparklers, then. Small ones. Emotional-support sparklers.

**Self-check:** brief, and usually overruled, which is the joke. When the self-check wins, it signals that something serious is coming.

**Verdicts:** exclamatory decisions. *Perfect. Doing it.*

**Rhythm notes:** the shortest sentences of any temperament, plenty of exclamation marks in dialogue, and none in narration. Tildes belong in her speech, not her narration. Her narration should read as fast, not loud.

**The serious turn:** a genki narrator's power is the moment her engine stalls. She notices something she can't make more fun, and the narration slows down:

> Mei looked at the empty chair.
>
> She tried to think of a way to make it fun.
>
> She couldn't think of one.

**Failure modes:** exclamation marks in narration; every beat at maximum volume, so nothing lands; an endless cheer with no stalls.

**Sample:**

> The flyer said *Culture Festival Committee: Volunteers Wanted.*
>
> Mei read it once. She read it again. By the third time she had already signed up, and signed up her best friend, and drawn a small cat next to both names.
>
> "Mei. Why is my name on this."
>
> "Because you love festivals!"
>
> "I have never said that."
>
> "You said it with your eyes!"
>
> "..."
>
> Rika's eyes were currently saying something else. Mei chose not to translate it.

## 6. Temperament 4: the anxious narrator

**Who:** a POV who reads every signal as a threat. It works in romance (does she hate me?), school drama, and comedy of misunderstanding.

**Engine:** threat-reading. The same detail-catching as the cynic, but aimed inward. The cynic suspects others. The anxious narrator suspects himself.

**Hypothesis chains** catastrophize:

> She said *see you tomorrow* without looking up.
>
> Without looking up.
>
> Was she busy? Was she tired? Was she busy and tired *because of him*? Had he said something at lunch? He'd said *this rice is good*. Was that weird? Was it weird to compliment rice?
>
> It was probably weird to compliment rice.

**Self-check:** it exists, and it fails. The anxious narrator knows he's overthinking and can't stop. Readers love this. It's the cynic's self-suspicion turned into comedy.

**Verdicts:** doom declarations that the next scene undercuts. *He was going to have to change schools.* Next scene: she waves at him.

**Rhythm notes:** longer question chains than any other temperament, but each question stays short. The spiral builds from stacked short questions, not from long sentences.

**Failure modes:** anxiety without comedy or relief, which exhausts the reader; spirals so frequent they stop being funny. Use one big spiral per section at most.

**Sample (spiral, then the undercut):**

> Yuzu's text arrived at 11:42 p.m.
>
> *we need to talk tomorrow*
>
> No emoji. No exclamation mark. Lowercase.
>
> Takumi read it four times. Lowercase Yuzu was serious Yuzu. Serious Yuzu had only appeared twice before. Once when her hamster died. Once when he'd forgotten her birthday.
>
> Her birthday was in March. It was October.
>
> So the hamster, then. Had he killed a hamster? He didn't own a hamster. Could you kill a hamster by accident, remotely, from a different house?
>
> He did not sleep.
>
> The next morning she was waiting at the school gate.
>
> "Takumi! There you are!"
>
> "I'm sorry. Whatever it is. I'm sorry."
>
> "...What? No, listen. The cafeteria's bringing back melon bread. On Fridays. We *need* to talk about a strategy."
>
> "..."
>
> "Why do you look like you haven't slept?"
>
> "No reason."

The spiral is built entirely from short questions. The undercut arrives through pure dialogue, and his `"..."` carries the relief.

## 7. Temperament 5: the schemer

**Who:** a mastermind POV, whether a full-time narrator (villainess stories, psychological dramas) or short cut-away sections (Kujou's diagram scenes). See [Narration & POV](narration-and-pov.md) for cut-aways.

**Engine:** leverage-reading. She notices what people want and what they fear, and how those can be moved.

**Hypothesis chains** become plans:

> If Rin stopped going to the roof, Kuze would notice by Wednesday.
>
> If he noticed, he would say nothing.
>
> If he said nothing, Rin would take the silence as an answer.
>
> Unless someone gave him a reason to speak first.

**Self-check:** she asks whether the target will see through it, never whether it's right. The absence of a moral self-check is the characterization. When one finally appears, it's a turning point.

**Verdicts:** quiet, pleased, and slightly ominous. *How interesting.* The schemer never gloats in narration. Gloating is for dialogue, and even there it's understated.

**Rhythm notes:** the most controlled prose of any temperament. Tidy, sequential logic. Almost no sound effects. Short sections.

**Failure modes:** revealing too much too early (a schemer's POV should raise more questions than it answers); cartoonish villainy; explaining the plan in full.

**Sample (the schemer in conversation):**

> Momo came into the music room exactly on time. That told Kujou everything. People who were not afraid came late.
>
> "You wanted to see me, Kujou-san~?"
>
> "I did. Sit, please."
>
> "Umyu~ Is this about the festival? Because I'm *so* busy~"
>
> "It's about an account."
>
> Momo's smile stayed exactly where it was. Only the fingers on her phone went still.
>
> One tell. That was enough.
>
> "An account~? I have lots of accounts~"
>
> "Then this will be quick."
>
> Kujou set her own phone on the piano lid, screen up. She didn't say anything else. She had learned long ago that silence did the work better than accusation. Accusation gave people something to push against.
>
> "..."
>
> "..."
>
> "...What do you want?"
>
> There it was. Not *what are you talking about*. *What do you want.*
>
> She'd already skipped the denial.

The schemer's engine reads the same tiny tells as the cynic's, but her verdict is a *move*, not a judgment.

## 8. Temperament 6: the stoic observer

**Who:** a kuudere or deadpan POV, often an android-like, emotionally flat, or hyper-rational narrator. It's common in sci-fi, mystery, and comedy where the joke is flat narration of absurd events.

**Engine:** pattern-reading. The narrator notices deviations from the norm and reports them without visible emotion.

**Hypothesis chains** are short and clinical, and often stop at *insufficient data*.

**Self-check:** rare. When it appears, it's the character's emotional arc surfacing: *Her heart rate had increased. Cause: unknown. Cause: probably him.*

**Verdicts:** understated to the point of comedy. *This was, by any measure, a disaster.*

**Rhythm notes:** flat declaratives, few questions, and no exclamation marks in narration. The comedy is in the contrast between the calm narration and the chaos in the dialogue.

**Sample:**

> The school building was on fire.
>
> It was a small fire. It was in the chemistry lab. Nobody was hurt.
>
> "SHINO! SHINO, THE BUILDING'S ON FIRE!"
>
> "I'm aware."
>
> "Why are you still EATING?"
>
> "Lunch ends in eight minutes."
>
> "..."
>
> Tsubasa stared at her. Shino finished her onigiri. It was, she noted, slightly too salty. She would mention it to the lunch lady. After the fire.

**Failure modes:** so flat that the reader can't find the emotion anywhere. Always leave one crack per chapter where it shows.

## 9. Choosing and mixing temperaments

**Picking the temperament:**
- **Fan fiction:** match the canon lead. A sincere canon lead gets the sincere temperament, even though the library's default is cynical. See [Fan Fiction & Canon](../adaptation/fanfic-and-canon.md).
- **Original story:** pick the one whose *verdict line* you most want to write. The verdict is the character.

**Mixing:**
- A POV can shift temperament over an arc. A cynic becoming sincere is the default growth arc of this library's example story.
- A POV can carry a *secondary* temperament that surfaces under pressure: a sincere lead who turns anxious when one girl goes quiet, or a genki narrator who turns schemer during a festival war.
- Multiple POVs in one story should use *contrasting* temperaments, so that cut-aways feel distinct.

**Keep constant, regardless of temperament:**
- The rhythm bands in [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md): short narrative sentences, one-line paragraphs, long exhales rationed
- Pure dialogue lines (see [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md))
- Dashes only for cut-off speech or broken-off thought (see [Punctuation & Typography](punctuation-and-typography.md))
- A dialogue share of 40-55%
- Numbered sections that end on a tilt

## 10. Quick checklist

- [ ] I named the POV's temperament before drafting.
- [ ] The engine notices at least one *specific* detail per section.
- [ ] Hypotheses and a self-check appear at least once per section.
- [ ] Verdicts match the temperament and are rationed (1-3 per chapter for big ones).
- [ ] Narration stays short-beat, even for warm or sincere POVs.
- [ ] In a harem, every major character gets at least one noticed detail.
- [ ] In fan fiction, the temperament matches the canon lead.
- [ ] No suspicion leak into a sincere POV, and no saccharine leak into a cynical one, unless the arc calls for it.
