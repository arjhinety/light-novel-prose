# Narration & POV

> **Purpose:** Define the narrative stance of light-novel prose (close third person anchored to one character), including surname anchoring, free indirect thought, how and when to cut away to other characters, and how narration handles knowledge, time, and distance.
> **Load when:** Starting any draft, deciding whose head a section lives in, writing a cut-away scene, or fixing narration that feels distant, omniscient, or inconsistent.
> **Related:** [Style Bible](style-bible.md), [Inner Monologue](inner-monologue.md), [Rhythm & Flow](rhythm-and-flow.md), [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md), [Chapter & Section](../structure/chapter-and-section.md), [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md), [Mystery, Psychological & Horror](../genres/mystery-psychological-horror.md)

## 1. The default stance: close third, one anchor

The standard LN school-drama voice is **third person, past tense, locked tightly to one character's perception**. The narrator is not a neutral camera. It is a voice that has absorbed the POV character's attitude, vocabulary, and blind spots.

- The narrator **knows only what the POV sees, hears, infers, or remembers.**
- The narrator **shares the POV's biases.** If Kuze thinks Amamiya is a hypocrite, the narration leans that way, even when it might be wrong.
- The narrator **speaks in the POV's register.** It is casual, contracted, and wry for Kuze, and it would be brisker and more flustered for Tachibana.

Why third and not first? Many LNs *are* first-person, and this skill supports that (see §9), but close third gives two advantages this voice relies on:

1. **Ironic distance.** "Kuze did not have a thinking face. He had a face." The third person lets the narrator be *slightly* outside the character, enough to be funny about him.
2. **Controlled cut-aways.** It's easy to hop briefly to another character's scene at a section break without the jarring shift of changing "I".

## 2. Surname anchoring

In narration, the POV character is referred to by **surname**: "Kuze", not "Minato". The same goes for the other characters in narration, **unless** the POV is on intimate first-name terms with them, and even then surname narration is common in the school register.

| Situation | Narration uses | Dialogue uses |
|---|---|---|
| POV character | "Kuze" | whatever the speaker calls him ("Kuze", "Kuze-kun", "Mina") |
| Classmates | Surname ("Amamiya", "Tachibana") | Speaker-specific ("Haruto", "Amamiya-kun", "Rin") |
| Teacher | Surname or surname + sensei ("Ogata" or "Ogata-sensei"), and stay consistent | "Ogata-sensei", "Kasumi-nee" (if a relative) |
| Childhood friend | Surname ("Enomoto") is fine, or first name if the story's narration is warmer | "Daichi", "Mina" |

**Why this matters.** Surname narration creates the **cool, slightly formal distance** of Japanese school life, where first names are intimate and earned. When the POV (or the narration) shifts to a first name, it signals a relationship change. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).

**Consistency rule:** pick the narration name for each character in the story bible and never drift. "Tachibana" in one paragraph and "Rin" in the next, with no in-story reason, breaks immersion instantly.

### 2.1 The dialogue/narration mismatch

This voice constantly plays the narration name against the dialogue name, and that contrast is information:

> "Haruto! Wait up!"
> Tachibana ran after Amamiya, her bag bouncing.

The reader registers that she calls him by his first name, while the narrator (Kuze's lens) keeps calling him Amamiya. That is the distance between them and Kuze.

## 3. Free indirect thought

The signature technique is **free indirect discourse**: the narration slides, without quotation marks or "he thought", into the character's own words, questions, and attitude.

### 3.1 The three layers

Narration moves fluidly between:

1. **Report**, the neutral(ish) account: *Kuze finished the daily log and capped his pen.*
2. **Interpretation**, the narrator reading the scene through Kuze's bias: *It was obvious she'd rehearsed the parfait suggestion.*
3. **Raw thought**, Kuze's own mind, sometimes in present tense: *Why am I even here?*

A single paragraph can hold all three:

> Amamiya patted Tachibana's head until she sat back down. *(report)* It was the easy, practiced gesture of someone who had never once wondered whether he'd be welcome. *(interpretation)* Who does that? *(raw thought)*

### 3.2 Tense and person in raw thought

Raw thought appears in three forms, in increasing intensity:

| Form | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Past-tense free indirect** | *Why was he even here?* | The default. Blends smoothly. |
| **Present-tense free indirect** | *Why is he even here?* | Sharper, for a sudden flash of feeling. |
| **First-person present** | *Why am I even here?* | Sharpest, the character's literal words. Ration it to 1-3 per chapter. |

**No italics needed** for free indirect thought in this style. The reference text runs raw thoughts as plain sentences. Italics are reserved for quoted-back phrases and in-world text (see [Punctuation & Typography](punctuation-and-typography.md)). If your output medium makes unitalicized first-person thought confusing, italicize *only* first-person thoughts, consistently.

### 3.3 Avoid "he thought" scaffolding

✗ *Kuze thought to himself that it was strange. He wondered why Amamiya would do that. He thought that maybe it was jealousy.*

✓ *Strange. Why would Amamiya do that? Jealousy, maybe.*

"He thought", "he wondered", "he realized", and "he felt" should be **rare**, used only when the verb itself carries information ("He realized, too late, that he'd said it out loud.").

## 4. What the narrator knows

The close-third narrator is **limited**, and this discipline is what makes the monologue matter.

### 4.1 The knowledge rule

The narrator can state:
- What the POV **perceives** (sees, hears, smells)
- What the POV **infers**, marked as inference when it's uncertain ("It was probably...", "It seemed...", "Kuze pieced it together.")
- What the POV **remembers**
- What the POV **feels**, shown rather than named where possible

The narrator **cannot** state:
- Other characters' inner thoughts (except in a cut-away, §6)
- Events the POV didn't witness (unless learned later)
- The "truth" of ambiguous motives

### 4.2 Marking inference

Much of this voice's pleasure is the POV **confidently reading** others. Mark confidence levels with small words:

| Confidence | Markers |
|---|---|
| Certain | "Obviously." / "It was clear that..." / "Kuze pieced it together instantly." |
| Likely | "Probably." / "It seemed..." / "Most likely..." |
| Speculative | "Maybe." / "Perhaps." / "Or was it...?" |
| Self-doubting | "Or was that just what he wanted to believe?" |

Varying these is how the narrator stays human. A narrator who is always "obviously" right is arrogant, and one who is always "maybe" is spineless.

### 4.3 Dramatic irony through limitation

Because the narrator is locked to Kuze, **the reader can see what Kuze misses.** Kuze notices Tachibana blushing at Amamiya. He does *not* notice (and the narration doesn't comment) that she looked at Kuze first, then away. The reader catches it, Kuze doesn't, and that gap becomes romantic tension.

Plant these deliberately:
> Tachibana glanced at him, opened her mouth, closed it, and turned back to Amamiya.
> Whatever. She probably wanted him to pass the menu.

The narration reports the glance faithfully, and Kuze misreads it. The reader knows better.

## 5. Narrative distance: zooming in and out

Close third isn't fixed at one distance. It **breathes**.

| Distance | Feel | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Wide** | Almost omniscient summary, used for openings and time skips | *Graduation came, then spring, then the ritual reshuffling of friendships that everyone pretended was natural.* |
| **Medium** | Report plus interpretation, the default | *Kuze trailed a few steps behind them, counting passing cars.* |
| **Close** | Raw thought, one mind | *Why does he always get the last word?* |
| **Very close** | Fragments, sensation | *Cold. His hands were cold. Why were his hands so cold.* |

**Zoom rules:**
- **Zoom in at emotional moments**, zoom out for transitions.
- **Don't jump from wide to very close in a single line** unless it's a deliberate jolt.
- **The opening of a story is often wide** (the reference novel opens with a quasi-essay about high school debuts before narrowing onto its protagonist). This is a genre convention, so use it for chapter 1.

### 5.1 The wide opening

> Moving up to high school is supposed to change things. New classmates, new clubs, a clean slate, and for some, the chance at a "high school debut," that fabled reinvention where a nobody walks through the gate in April and walks out a somebody.
> Most people don't debut. They just move up a grade.
> Minato Kuze was one of them.

Wide, then medium, then anchored. After this, the narration stays close.

## 6. Cut-aways: stepping outside the POV

The reference style **occasionally leaves the anchor POV** for short scenes: a heroine at home, the antagonist's clique after school, the mastermind drawing a diagram of names. These cut-aways are powerful and must be controlled.

### 6.1 When to cut away

- **To show the reader something the POV can't know** that raises tension (the bully queen plotting, the mastermind's smile).
- **To reveal another character's inner life** that recontextualizes the POV's reading (Tachibana's POV reveals she's lonely, not arrogant).
- **To tease** at the end of an arc or chapter (a single ominous line from someone we don't expect).

### 6.2 Cut-away rules

1. **Only at section breaks.** Never switch POV mid-section.
2. **Short.** Usually 100-500 words, and sometimes only three lines.
3. **Establish the new anchor in the first sentence.** *"In her room, Kujou uncapped a red pen."*
4. **Match the narration to the new POV's voice.** Kujou's cut-away is cooler and more precise, and Tachibana's is jumpier.
5. **End on a hook**, usually a smile, a muttered line, or an image.
6. **Return to the main POV** in the next section. Don't linger.
7. **Don't overuse them.** 0-2 per chapter, and many chapters have none.

### 6.3 Example cut-away (the mastermind tease)

> 7
>
> In her room, Kujou set a sheet of paper on her desk and uncapped a red pen.
> Amamiya's name went in the center. Around it: Tachibana, Matsuri, Shirasagi, herself.
> Arrows. From each name, an arrow toward the middle. Neat, straight, obvious.
> Then she wrote one more name at the very edge of the page, and paused.
> Kuze.
> She tapped the pen against her lips.
> "Hmm..."
> Slowly, carefully, she drew an arrow, and she did not draw it toward the center.
> "Things are getting interesting."

This is short and cold. It gives information the reader has and Kuze doesn't, and it ends on a hook.

### 6.4 The "headless" cut-away

Sometimes the cut-away hides who the character is, for mystery:

> On a weekend morning in mid-April, a girl ran through the shopping district with toast in her mouth.
> "L-Late, I'm gonna be late...!"

The reader doesn't know yet that it's Shirasagi staging a "chance" meeting with Amamiya. The final line reveals her ("Behind him, the girl smirked, just slightly."). Use this for manipulation reveals. See [Mystery, Psychological & Horror](../genres/mystery-psychological-horror.md).

## 7. Multiple POV characters

Some LNs rotate POV between two or more leads (for example, the hero and heroine alternating chapters). Rules:

- **One POV per numbered section**, and ideally per chapter.
- **Signal the anchor immediately** in the first line.
- **Each POV needs its own monologue engine.** Different noticing, different self-suspicion, and a different leitmotif (see [Inner Monologue §12](inner-monologue.md#12-monologue-for-other-pov-types)).
- **Exploit misreadings.** Show the same event from two POVs where each misreads the other. This is the engine of the "misunderstanding romance" (see [Rom-Com & Comedy](../genres/romcom-and-comedy.md)).

> *(Kuze section)* She hadn't said a word the whole walk. Clearly still angry about the hamburger thing.
>
> *(Tachibana section)* He hadn't said a word the whole walk. Was he bored? Should she say something? No, if she said something it'd be weird. Right? It'd be weird.

## 8. Time handling in narration

### 8.1 Time stamps

LN narration uses **explicit time stamps** at section openings, often with a dash:

> The next day--
> After school--
> An hour later--
> May, second year of high school--

These are fast, clear, and very genre-typical. Use them instead of elaborate transitional paragraphs.

### 8.2 Flashbacks

- Keep flashbacks **short** and anchored in a present-moment trigger: *Watching them share the parfait, Kuze remembered something Enomoto's ex-girlfriend had once said.*
- Use **past perfect for the first sentence or two**, then slide into simple past.
- Return with a clear present-moment beat: *"We're done. Kuze, here." Amamiya passed him the menu.*

### 8.3 Summary vs. scene

LN narration **summarizes routine** briskly and **dramatizes interaction** in full:

> The day passed without incident. Kuze went home, ate dinner, and did his homework as usual.
> Then, a little after ten, he opened Chirp.

Don't dramatize logistics, and don't summarize conversations that carry emotion.

## 9. First-person variant

Many LNs are narrated in first person. The engine is the same, but the mechanics shift:

| Close third | First person |
|---|---|
| "Kuze" | "I" |
| Ironic distance from the narrator | Irony must come from self-deprecation |
| Surname anchoring | The narrator names others by surname; his own name is heard only in dialogue |
| Cut-aways at breaks | Cut-aways become "interludes" labeled with the other character's name or "Side: Tachibana" |
| "Why was he even here?" | "Why was I even here?" or present-tense "Why am I even here?" |

First-person example:
> I don't have a thinking face. I have a face. It's just that people tend to assume anyone who isn't smiling must be thinking about something.
> To be fair, I usually am.

First-person LNs lean more on **direct address and asides** ("Look, I know how this sounds."). Use them lightly, at 1-2 per chapter.

**Tense:** first-person LN narration is commonly past tense, but present tense is also common, especially in comedic or isekai stories. Pick one and hold it.

## 11. Common narration failures

| Failure | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Head-hopping** | "Kuze was annoyed. Tachibana felt embarrassed. Amamiya didn't notice either of them." | One anchor per section. Show Tachibana's embarrassment through what Kuze sees. |
| **Camera narration** | "The classroom contained 30 desks. A boy sat by the window." | Filter through the POV's attitude: "Thirty desks, and he'd somehow drawn the one by the window again. Protagonist seat. Wasted on him." |
| **Omniscient summary of motives** | "Amamiya wanted all the girls to like him." | Present as the POV's inference and question it |
| **Name drift** | "Tachibana" / "Rin" / "the girl" / "the tsundere" | Fix the narration name per character, and use epithets only rarely and in voice |
| **Epithet overload** | "the silver-haired girl", "the kendo girl", "the tsundere beauty" | Use the surname. Epithets are for first appearances and deliberate comic effect. |
| **"He thought" scaffolding** | "He thought it was strange." | Free indirect: "Strange." |
| **Unanchored cut-away** | A section starts mid-scene with no clue whose head we're in | Anchor POV and place in the first sentence |
| **Narrator smarter than the POV** | Kuze is naive, but the narration analyzes like a psychologist | The narration's intelligence equals the POV's intelligence |

## 12. Drill: one scene, three distances

Write this moment at three narrative distances: *Amamiya hands Kuze a canned coffee after school.*

**Wide:**
> Some people give gifts to make friends. Some people give gifts so they'll be seen giving them. The trick, Kuze had found, was that from the outside the two looked exactly the same.

**Medium:**
> Amamiya held out a canned coffee, black, the kind Kuze always bought. He must have noticed. That was either thoughtful or unsettling.

**Close:**
> Black coffee. His brand. How does he know that? Since when does he know that?

A good section uses all three, moving from medium to close at the key moment and wide at the transition out.

## 13. Narration register per anchor

When you cut away to another character's POV, the **narration itself** changes texture, not just the thoughts. Here is the same moment (Amamiya pats Tachibana's head in the family restaurant) narrated from four anchors:

**Kuze (default: dry, analytical):**
> Amamiya patted her head until she sat back down, easy as anything. It was the kind of gesture only someone certain of being welcome would try. Kuze filed it under things he'd never attempt, which was a long file.

**Tachibana (jumpy, defensive, physical):**
> His hand was on her head. His hand was *on her head*. In public. In front of *that guy*. Rin sat down so fast the seat squeaked, and then, because her face was on fire and she had to do something, she smacked Kuze's shoulder. It wasn't his fault. It was a little bit his fault.

**Kujou (cool, precise, amused):**
> Kujou watched from two tables away, where nobody had noticed her. Haruto's hand, Tachibana's collapse, and the boy across from them staring into his coffee like it owed him money. She took a small sip of tea. Interesting.

**Matsuri (warm, noticing loneliness):**
> Hina saw it from the drink bar: Rin all red, Haruto grinning, and Kuze-kun sitting alone on his side of the booth with his coffee. He looked like the last kid at the bus stop. She filled a second glass of melon soda without really deciding to.

What changes:
- **Sentence length**: Tachibana's are short and repeating, and Kujou's are long and controlled.
- **Narration names**: in her own section, Tachibana is "Rin" and Amamiya is "Haruto". The narration name follows the anchor's intimacy. Record per-POV narration names in the story bible.
- **What gets noticed**: Kuze notices the gesture's assumption, Tachibana notices her body, Kujou notices the whole board, and Matsuri notices who's alone.
- **The verdict**: filed away, a guilty smack, "Interesting.", and a silent act of kindness.

## 14. Transitions between anchors

When the book returns from a cut-away to the main POV, the first line should **re-anchor quickly and ideally echo the cut-away**, creating a click for the reader:

> *(end of Kujou cut-away)* She drew the arrow, and it did not point to the center.
>
> 8
>
> Kuze sneezed.
> Somebody, somewhere, was probably talking about him. Statistically unlikely, but it was a nice thought.

The reader knows who was "talking about him" and Kuze doesn't. The echo turns the transition into a joke that also carries dread.

Other re-anchor patterns:
- **Same object, other side**: the cut-away ends on Tachibana staring at her phone, and the next section opens on Kuze's phone buzzing.
- **Same time stamp**: "That same evening--"
- **Contrast**: the cut-away ends on scheming, and the next section opens on Kuze eating instant ramen and thinking about nothing.

## 14.5 Unreliable narration, lightly

Close third on a biased POV is *mildly* unreliable by nature. You can dial it up:

- **Level 1 (default):** the POV's inferences are sometimes wrong, and the narration reports facts accurately.
- **Level 2:** the narration *omits* things the POV refuses to think about. For example, Kuze never lets himself finish the thought "Maybe I like her", and the narration cuts away every time: "He didn't follow that thought any further."
- **Level 3:** the POV misremembers or re-frames events, and a later section or cut-away shows the discrepancy. Reserve this for mystery and psychological stories.

The rule at every level: **the narration never states an outright falsehood as fact.** It can omit, misread, and deflect, but it cannot lie. Readers forgive a biased narrator and resent a cheating one.

## 15. Checklist

- [ ] One anchor POV per numbered section, established in the first line
- [ ] Surname narration, consistent per character, matching the story bible
- [ ] Free indirect thought in place of "he thought / he wondered"
- [ ] First-person present-tense thoughts rationed to 1-3 per chapter
- [ ] Inferences marked with calibrated confidence words
- [ ] At least one moment of dramatic irony the POV misreads (per chapter, in romance-bearing stories)
- [ ] Cut-aways only at section breaks, short and anchored, ending on a hook
- [ ] Time stamps with dashes for transitions, and routine summarized briskly
- [ ] The narration's register matches the anchor POV's voice
