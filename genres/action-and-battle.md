# Action & Battle

> **Purpose:** Teach the writer how to put fights, duels, chases, and battle arcs on the page in light novel prose: fast, readable, tactically legible, and still filtered through a living narrator's head.
> **Load when:** A chapter contains any physical conflict (a sword duel, a magic battle, a street fight, a sports clash written like a fight, a chase), or the story is a battle-shonen, dungeon, tournament, or "power system" LN.
> **Related:** [Genre Index](genre-index.md), [Fantasy](fantasy.md), [Isekai & Reincarnation](isekai-and-reincarnation.md), [Sports, Idol & Club](sports-idol-and-club.md), [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md), [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md), [Punctuation & Typography](../core/punctuation-and-typography.md), [Emotional Dialogue](../dialogue/emotional-dialogue.md), [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md), [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md), [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md)

---

## 1. What readers of this genre want

Battle LN readers are not reading for choreography. They are reading for **three feelings, in order**:

1. **"How is he going to get out of this?"**: a problem the reader understands. The enemy is faster, the barrier is unbreakable, the rule forbids killing.
2. **"Oh, *that's* how."**: a solution that was planted earlier and makes the reader feel clever for almost seeing it.
3. **"That was cool."**: the release. An attack name shouted, a blade stopping a hair from someone's throat, a rival's grudging "...Not bad."

Everything in this file serves those three beats. A fight with no problem is a list of hits. A fight with no planted solution is a coin flip. A fight with no release is exhausting.

The base voice (see [Style Bible](../core/style-bible.md)) survives intact. The narrator is still an overthinking person with opinions. In action the overthinking becomes **tactics**. The same brain that noticed who got offered a drink now notices that the opponent drops his left shoulder before every thrust. That continuity is what separates LN action from a generic fight scene: *the analytical inner monologue is the combat system.*

## 2. How the base voice shifts in action

| Dial | Base school-drama voice | Action voice |
|---|---|---|
| POV | Close third, one anchor | Same, but you may cut to a spectator or the opponent for one short section to raise stakes |
| Sentence length | Median ~7 words | Median drops to ~5 during exchanges. The long exhale is reserved for the lull between exchanges |
| Dialogue share | ~47% | Dips to ~30% inside a fight section (taunts, attack names, allies shouting) and rises again in the setup and aftermath. The **chapter** still has to land inside the 40-55% gate ([Revision Checklist](../revision/revision-checklist.md)), so a fight chapter pays for its quiet exchanges with dialogue-heavy setup and aftermath sections |
| Rhetorical questions | Social ("Was it jealousy?") | Tactical ("Why hasn't he used his right hand?") |
| Description | Portraits, little scenery | **Terrain matters now.** Give 1-2 lines of usable space: the pillar, the wet floor, the railing |
| Comedy ratio | Frequent deadpan | Low in the fight, high in the setup and the aftermath. A single deadpan line mid-fight is a power move |
| Sound effects | 1-4 per chapter | 3-8 per fight, as standalone lines at impact points |
| Leitmotif | "The sediment" | Still present. It stirs when fear or rage rises, and the climax can be the sediment boiling over |

The biggest shift is **tempo control**. A fight alternates between *exchange* (fast, short, physical) and *lull* (slow, thinking, a breath). Never write three exchanges in a row without a lull; the reader loses the thread. Never write two lulls in a row; the fight stalls.

## 3. The exchange-lull engine

### 3.1 The exchange

An exchange is one burst of action, usually 3-8 short lines, which ends when *something changes*: a hit lands, a weapon breaks, a position shifts, or a new fact is revealed.

Rules:
- **One action per sentence.** "He ducked, spun, and slashed at her legs while shouting" is choreography soup. Break it apart.
- **Lead with the verb or the sound**, not with the subject's name every time. Vary: "Steel rang." / "Kuze ducked." / "Too slow."
- **Standalone impact lines.** Put the moment of contact on its own line.
- **Name the body part, not the technique, unless the technique has a name.** "The bokken cracked against his forearm" beats "he executed a downward strike."

> Tachibana moved first.
>
> One step, and the distance between them vanished.
>
> Kuze raised the bokken on instinct. Wood met wood.
>
> *Clack.*
>
> The shock ran up both his arms and settled in his teeth. She was already pulling back, already coming again, low this time, aiming for the knee he'd been favoring since the stairs.
>
> He gave ground. Then more ground.
>
> His heel hit the wall of the dojo.
>
> Ah. So that was the plan.

Notice the last line. The exchange ends when the *situation changes*: he is cornered. The narrator registers it with a dry, short thought. That is the voice.

### 3.2 The lull

A lull is a breath, where the camera pulls back into the head. It lasts one to three paragraphs, and it's where the long exhale lives (see [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md)). The narrator:
1. Takes stock (injury, stamina, position)
2. Reads the opponent (a pattern, a tell, a contradiction)
3. Forms a hypothesis chain
4. Commits to a plan, or admits he has none

> She wasn't breathing hard. He was. That alone said enough about how this would end if he kept playing her game, trading blow for blow, letting her choose the distance, because she had spent every morning since middle school in this room and he had spent every morning since middle school asleep.
>
> So why hadn't she finished it?
>
> Twice now she'd had the opening. Twice she'd pulled the strike a centimeter short. Was it mercy? Was it the club rule about practice matches? Or was it that Amamiya was standing in the doorway, watching?
>
> ...The last one. It was always the last one.
>
> Then the answer was simple. He just had to move to where Amamiya couldn't see her face.

The tactical deduction is also a **social** deduction. That is the house style in action: a fight scene that is secretly about the same web of feelings as the rest of the book.

### 3.3 The pattern

A standard fight follows this shape:

```
Setup (terms, stakes, terrain) -> Exchange 1 (probe) -> Lull (read)
-> Exchange 2 (escalation, the protagonist loses ground) -> Lull (despair / realization)
-> Exchange 3 (the planted solution) -> Impact line -> Aftermath (dialogue, comedy, cost)
```

Three exchanges fit a scene of 1,200-2,500 words. Arc-climax battles can run to five or six, but add a POV cut to a spectator between exchanges 3 and 4 so the reader gets outside the pressure cooker for a moment.

## 4. Tactical legibility

The reader must always know three things: **where everyone is**, **what each side can do**, and **what the protagonist is trying to do right now**.

### 4.1 Where everyone is
- Establish terrain in setup with 1-2 *usable* details: "The rooftop fence was chain-link, rusted through near the water tank." If a detail won't matter, cut it.
- Use relative positions: "between him and the stairwell", "her back to the sun". Avoid compass directions and meter-perfect distances unless the character is the type who'd measure.
- When positions change, say so in one line: "Now *he* had the sun behind him."

### 4.2 What each side can do
- **Set rules before the fight.** In a magic system, what does a spell cost? In a kendo match, what counts as a point? In a street fight, is anyone willing to really hurt someone? Rules create tension, and a fight without limits has no suspense. See [Fantasy](fantasy.md#4-magic-systems-without-lectures) for exposition techniques.
- Reveal abilities in **use**, not in narration. The first time the enemy teleports, the protagonist should be confused for one line before the narrator names it.
- Keep the **power ledger** consistent. If a fireball took three seconds to cast in chapter 4, it cannot be instant in chapter 19 without a training beat that explains why.

### 4.3 What the protagonist is trying to do
State the micro-goal in the lull, in the character's own voice:
- "Get the key. Don't win. Just get the key."
- "Three more minutes. Enomoto said three minutes."
- "Make her angry. Angry people swing wide."

A micro-goal turns a fight from a duel of HP bars into a **heist with swords**, and heists are always readable.

## 5. Attack names, shouts, and ki

Named attacks are a genre pleasure. Use them with intent.

### 5.1 When to name an attack
- **When it's a signature**, meaning it will recur and the reader should recognize it
- **When the name carries meaning** (a technique named after a dead master, or a joke name the protagonist gave it in middle school)
- **When the shout is the release beat** at the end of the planted solution

### 5.2 Formatting
Attack names go in dialogue, often with brackets or bold if the story uses a game system. Pick one convention and hold it (see [Punctuation & Typography](../core/punctuation-and-typography.md)):

> "*Tsubame Gaeshi*--!"
>
> "[Frost Lance]!"
>
> "Crescent Step!"

A Japanese name with an English gloss the first time only is a strong LN convention:

> "*Kagerou*."
> Heat Haze. The move she'd been practicing every night behind the clubhouse, the one she'd sworn she didn't care about.

### 5.3 Undercutting attack names
Because the base voice is cynical, the POV narrator may find attack names embarrassing. That is a comedy asset:

> "Ultimate Secret Art: Thousand Petal Dance!"
>
> Did he have to say it out loud? Did the petals need a number?
>
> The petals, unfortunately, turned out to be very real.

The pattern goes like this: mockery, then consequence. The narrator is wrong to mock, which humbles him and raises the threat.

### 5.4 Ki, mana, and aura description
Keep it tactile and short. Temperature, pressure, sound, and color, plus one comparison. Avoid multiparagraph aura descriptions.

> The air around him went heavy, like the moment before a summer storm when the cicadas stop.

## 6. Power scaling

Power scaling is the long-arc engine of battle LNs, and the fastest way to wreck one.

### 6.1 Rules
1. **Every win costs something.** A broken arm, a lost friend's trust, a spell the character can't cast again for a month, or public exposure of a secret power. Free wins deflate the next fight.
2. **Power-ups need a seed.** Plant the training, the trauma, the item, or the promise two or more chapters before the payoff. See [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md).
3. **Escalate the problem, not only the numbers.** The next enemy shouldn't just be stronger. They should break the protagonist's current solution. If he wins by reading tells, the next enemy has no tells.
4. **Let the protagonist lose at least once per arc.** A loss at the midpoint makes the climax earned (see [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md)).
5. **Keep a stat sheet** in the story bible, one line per character: current abilities, limits, costs. See [Story Bible Template](../templates/story-bible.md).

### 6.2 The OP protagonist
If the protagonist is overpowered (common in [Isekai](isekai-and-reincarnation.md)), the tension must move off "will he win?" and onto:
- **Will he win without revealing himself?** (a hidden-power story)
- **Will he win without killing?** (moral restraint)
- **Will he get there in time?** (a race)
- **What will it cost the people around him?** (collateral)
- **How will others react to his strength?** (the social fallout, which is the house voice's specialty)

> He could end it in one move. That wasn't the problem.
>
> The problem was that thirty classmates were watching from the gym windows, and by tomorrow morning every one of them would want to know why the go-home club guy could do *that*.

## 7. Rivalry, shonen-style

The rival is half of every good battle arc.

### 7.1 Rival design
- **A mirror with one inversion.** Same talent, opposite belief: Kuze believes in logic, the rival in instinct. Same goal, different reason.
- **Respect under hostility.** The rival must acknowledge the protagonist at key moments ("...Not bad"), which makes those moments valuable.
- **A rival voice.** Short, arrogant, direct, often using "oi" or dropping honorifics (see [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md)). Surname-only address from a rival is itself a sign of respect.

### 7.2 The rivalry ladder
1. **First clash**: the rival wins easily, or treats the protagonist as beneath notice
2. **Grudging notice**: the protagonist lands one hit, and the rival learns his name
3. **Forced cooperation**: a common enemy
4. **The real match**: fought at full strength, with a narrow result either way
5. **Acknowledgment**: they share a meal and argue about it

### 7.3 Rival sample passage

> "Oi. Go-home club."
>
> Kuze didn't turn around.
> "That's not my name."
>
> "It's what you are."
> Shiga dropped his bag at the edge of the court and rolled his neck until something cracked.
> "You took a point off me last week. Fluke."
>
> "Probably."
>
> "Don't agree with me."
>
> "Then it wasn't a fluke."
>
> "Don't *disagree* with me either!"
>
> ...What was he supposed to do, then?
>
> Shiga picked up a racket and pointed it at him like a sword. The whole gym went quiet, the way a classroom does when a teacher walks in, except nobody here was going to save anyone.
>
> "Rematch. Now. No flukes."
>
> "I have a train at five-forty."
>
> "Then you'd better lose fast."

The rivalry is comedic on the surface and sharp underneath. The narrator's deadpan keeps it in the house voice even though the scene is set up like a showdown.

## 8. Genre tropes: execution and failure

| Trope | Execute well | How it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **The hidden-strength reveal** | Seed small tells for chapters (calluses, a reflexive dodge in P.E.), and pay off in front of the worst possible audience | Revealed with no seeds, it reads as a cheat |
| **"This isn't even my final form"** | The escalation reveals *character* (why they held back) | Pure numbers inflation |
| **The tournament arc** | Every match tests a different skill or relationship. Cut the matches that don't | Twelve matches, all the same |
| **Training montage** | Compress it with a dated list or a series of one-line days; see [Sports, Idol & Club](sports-idol-and-club.md#6-training-montages-in-prose) | Three chapters of push-ups |
| **The mentor's death** | Give the mentor a funny tic you'll miss, then kill them mid-sentence | A mentor with no personality before dying |
| **The friendship power-up** | The friend's line was said chapters earlier. The protagonist remembers it at the worst moment | Deus ex friendship with no setup |
| **Protecting someone behind you** | Keep the protected person in the scene with lines, fear, or a gesture | They vanish until they're needed for the "thank you" |
| **The villain's monologue** | Short, and it reveals a belief the protagonist half-shares | Exposition dump at a stopped clock |
| **Shouting the attack name** | A release beat after a long lull | Every attack named. Nothing lands |
| **"I'll protect everyone!"** | Undercut or tested by the story | Treated as a personality |

## 9. Violence levels and aftermath

- **School-drama fights** (the base genre) are rare and **ugly**: a shove, a grabbed collar, a kicked chair. The prose should feel embarrassed by them. See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md). Nobody in class 2-3 knows how to fight, so write the clumsiness.
- **Sports-as-battle**: no injuries, but the same exchange-lull engine.
- **Fantasy battle**: injury is real. Blood is described in one line, not three. Pain goes in the lull, not the exchange.
- **Aftermath is mandatory.** After any significant fight, write at least a short section of consequence: a trip to the nurse's office, a scolding from Ogata-sensei, the rival's awkward thanks, or the protagonist's hands shaking once it's over. The aftermath is where the reader's investment gets paid back, and where comedy returns.

> The nurse's office smelled like disinfectant and the tatami that nobody had replaced since the Showa era.
>
> Tachibana pressed an ice pack to his forearm with far more force than an ice pack required.
> "You idiot."
>
> "You hit me."
>
> "You *let* me hit you."
>
> "..."
>
> "Don't go quiet on me! Say something, idiot!"

## 10. Scene recipes

### 10.1 The practice match that becomes real
**Beats:** club rules stated; a friendly start; one hit lands too hard; a hidden grudge surfaces; the exchange accelerates; an outsider stops it or one fighter pulls back; aftermath with an unspoken truth.
**POV angle:** Kuze reads the grudge before the fighters admit it.

### 10.2 Protecting someone in the back alley
**Beats:** the protagonist sees trouble and weighs whether to get involved (the ledger); commits; is outnumbered and wins by *wit*, not strength (a bluff, a phone camera, a well-timed shout for a teacher); aftermath embarrassment.
**POV angle:** the cynic acts, then hates that he acted.

### 10.3 The dungeon corridor
**Beats:** the party marches in; one line of terrain; a sound; the first contact; the party roles show through dialogue; a mistake splits the party; the lull comes in the dark; a planted item saves them.
**POV angle:** the narrator's meta awareness ("Of course there's a trap. There's always a trap on the third floor.").

### 10.4 The duel before an audience
**Beats:** terms announced; crowd chatter in stacked lines; the first exchange goes badly; a crowd reaction line; the lull; a callback solution; the crowd goes silent; the release.
**POV angle:** the crowd is a character. Their murmurs track who's winning.

### 10.5 The chase
**Beats:** trigger; route (stairs, the hallway, the station platform); obstacles as one-liners; the pursuer's footsteps as sound lines; a clever turn (doubling back, hiding in the broadcast room); stillness and breath; either discovery or escape.
**POV angle:** time perception stretches. Use short lines and present-feeling questions.

## 11. Chapter and arc shapes

- **Fight chapter:** avoid starting in the middle of the exchange. Start one beat before, with the terms or the insult, then cut to the first contact. End on either the impact line *or* the first line of aftermath that reveals a cost.
- **Split fights across chapters** at a lull, never mid-exchange. The best cut is right after the protagonist commits to a plan the reader hasn't heard: "He had one idea. It was a stupid idea." Next chapter.
- **Battle arc (8-15 chapters):** inciting threat, then the first loss, training or investigation, rival cooperation, the midpoint reversal (an ally is hurt or a secret exposed), the final fight in three phases, and the aftermath with a new question.

## 12. Naming and setting conventions

- Techniques: Japanese name with an English gloss (*Kagerou*, "Heat Haze"), or English names with katakana flavor ("Crescent Step"). Pick one per world.
- Weapons: bokken, shinai, katana, and nodachi for the Japanese setting. The fantasy default is European weapons with Japanese naming habits (a sword named "Mistletoe").
- Schools and styles: "the Mikagami style", "Tachibana-ryuu", with the family name plus "-ryuu".
- Rank systems (tournament seeds, guild ranks F-S, dan grades) must be stated once and then used consistently.

## 13. Sample passage: School-roof confrontation (low-power, realistic)

> The rooftop door slammed shut behind them. Somebody had propped it open with a folded Chirp printout, and now it was just a door again, and they were three against one.
>
> The tallest one stepped forward. Third-year. Rugby build. A tie loosened in a way that was probably meant to look cool.
> "So you're the one. The guy who told Ogata-sensei."
>
> "I told her the vending machine was broken."
>
> "Don't play dumb."
>
> Kuze wasn't playing. He genuinely had told her the vending machine was broken. He had also, in the same conversation, happened to mention whose initials were carved into the side of it. The two facts were only connected if you were the kind of person who connected things.
>
> Which, apparently, the rugby club was.
>
> The tall one grabbed his collar.
>
> His back hit the fence. Chain-link rattled. Somewhere below, the baseball team was shouting through practice drills, the same three syllables over and over, and it occurred to Kuze with total clarity that none of them would look up.
>
> Fight? No. He'd lose, and then lose again at the disciplinary meeting.
>
> Run? The door was behind them.
>
> Then--
>
> "You know, there's a security camera on the water tank."
>
> The grip loosened by a millimeter. Three heads turned.
>
> There was no security camera on the water tank. There was a rusted weather vane shaped like a rooster, which, from the right angle and with enough guilt, looked like something that might be watching.
>
> It was enough. Kuze ducked under the arm and was through the door before the rooster lost its credibility.
>
> He didn't stop until the second-floor landing.
>
> Then he leaned against the wall and laughed, quietly, at nothing, with his hands shaking so hard he had to put them in his pockets.
>
> The sediment in his chest had kicked up into a cloud. It took a long time to settle.

**What it demonstrates:** terrain set in one line (the fence, the door, the water tank); a micro-goal hypothesis chain ("Fight? Run? Then--"); the solution is wit, planted by the terrain; an impact line; the aftermath shows the cost (shaking hands, the leitmotif).

## 14. Sample passage: Magic duel (fantasy register)

> The proctor raised a hand.
> "Terms. First to leave the circle or yield. Lethal spells forfeit."
>
> Across the chalk ring, Leonhart flicked his wrist and three motes of blue fire bloomed above his fingers. He didn't even look at them. The crowd in the academy stands made a sound like a held breath.
>
> Kuze had one spell.
>
> One. [Minor Displacement]: swap the positions of two small objects within ten meters. It was the kind of spell you learned in your first week and forgot in your second, the kind of spell people used to switch salt and sugar at the dormitory as a prank.
>
> He'd learned it in his first week. He had not forgotten it.
>
> "Begin."
>
> The first mote came fast.
>
> Kuze threw himself left. Heat licked his sleeve. The chalk line smoked where he'd been standing.
>
> The second came faster. He didn't dodge that one so much as fall over.
>
> Laughter from the stands.
> "Go home, go-home club!"
> The laughter doubled.
>
> ...Rude. Accurate, but rude.
>
> He stayed down for a moment, cheek against the stone. From here he could see the proctor's boots, the chalk, and the little silver pin on Leonhart's collar: the academy crest every student was required to wear.
>
> Every student.
>
> The third mote was already growing, swelling from a candle to a lantern to something the size of a watermelon. Leonhart was going to end it.
>
> Kuze reached into his own collar, closed his hand around his own silver pin, and whispered.
>
> "[Minor Displacement]."
>
> Two small objects. Within ten meters.
>
> For an instant nothing happened. Then Leonhart looked down at his own collar, at the pin that was suddenly Kuze's, the one with the scratched-out initials, and his face did something complicated.
>
> The rules said a student was bound to the circle by their crest.
>
> Leonhart's crest was now lying in Kuze's hand, outside the chalk line.
>
> The proctor spoke slowly, as if checking each word.
> "...Leonhart Veil has left the circle."
>
> The fireball fizzled out like a birthday candle.
>
> Silence. Then, from somewhere in the stands, one person began to clap. It was Kujou. Of course it was Kujou.

**What it demonstrates:** rules stated in setup (they become the solution); a weak power made tactically interesting; mockery as pressure; an observation in the lull ("Every student."); the planted solution; the crowd as a barometer; a social hook in the final line.

## 15. Sample passage: The kendo match (sports-as-battle)

> "*Men!*"
>
> The shinai came down like a sentence being passed.
>
> Tachibana took it on her guard, pivoted, and answered with a strike to the wrist that Kuze didn't even see, only heard: a crisp slap and the referee's flag snapping up.
>
> "*Kote-ari!*"
>
> One point. Two needed.
>
> In the stands, the kendo club's first-years were screaming her name. Amamiya was standing, fists clenched, shouting something that was definitely louder than necessary.
> "You got this, Rin!"
>
> Kuze watched her shoulders instead.
>
> Up, down. Up, down. Too fast. She was breathing through her mouth under the mask, and in two years of watching her pretend not to care about anything, he had only seen her do that once: the day before the regional qualifiers, in the hallway outside the club room, alone.
>
> She wasn't winning. She was hurrying.
>
> Her opponent saw it too. The girl from Kaiou shifted her feet, just slightly, and waited.
>
> Don't, Kuze thought. Don't go in.
>
> Tachibana went in.
>
> "*Dou!*"
>
> The Kaiou girl's shinai cut across her torso, and the flags went up, and the gym made a sound like a wave breaking on the wrong shore.
>
> One-all.
>
> Tachibana stood still. Behind the mask, he couldn't see her face. He didn't need to.
>
> ...Why did he care?
>
> He took out his phone, typed *Breathe through your nose, idiot,* and deleted it. Typed it again. Deleted it.
>
> Then, before he could think better of it, he stood up and said it out loud.
>
> Not loud. He didn't do loud. But the gym had gone quiet for the final point, and his voice carried the way voices do in a place built for echoes.
>
> The mask turned. Just a few degrees. Toward him.
>
> "..."
>
> She took one slow breath. Through her nose.

**What it demonstrates:** real kendo calls (*Men*, *Kote*, *Dou*) with no gloss needed in context; a tactical read that is really an emotional read; the phone-typing beat (a social-media-era hesitation); a quiet action as the climax instead of a shout; the silent reply.

## 16. Genre-specific revision checklist

- [ ] Can the reader state, at every point, where each fighter is relative to the terrain?
- [ ] Were the rules (point system, spell cost, stakes) established *before* the first exchange?
- [ ] Does every exchange end on a change (hit, position, reveal)?
- [ ] Is there a lull between every pair of exchanges?
- [ ] Is the winning move planted at least one scene earlier?
- [ ] Is every named attack either a signature, meaningful, or a release beat? Cut the rest.
- [ ] Is there at most one action per sentence in exchanges?
- [ ] Do impact moments get their own line?
- [ ] Is the protagonist's tactical reasoning in *his* voice (petty-smart, social), not generic?
- [ ] Does the win cost something?
- [ ] Is there an aftermath section with a consequence and, usually, the return of comedy?
- [ ] Did blood and pain get one line each, not a paragraph?
- [ ] Have you removed the action AI-isms: "a flurry of blows", "time seemed to slow", "every fiber of his being", "with lightning speed", "a dance of steel"? See [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md).
- [ ] Is the power ledger consistent with earlier chapters?
- [ ] Does the chapter end on a hook, not a summary? See [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md).
