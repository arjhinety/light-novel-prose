# Villainess & Otome

> **Purpose:** Write the "reincarnated as the villainess of an otome game" genre and its relatives (noble academies, ballroom politics, doom flags, broken engagements) in the LN voice, including the double-layered ojou-sama narrator.
> **Load when:** The protagonist is (or wakes up as) a noble antagonist from a romance game or novel, or the story revolves around aristocratic academies, engagements, and court intrigue with a romance core.
> **Related:** [Genre Index](genre-index.md), [Isekai & Reincarnation](isekai-and-reincarnation.md), [Fantasy](fantasy.md), [Romance & Melodrama](romance-and-melodrama.md), [Harem](harem.md), [Rom-Com & Comedy](romcom-and-comedy.md), [Character Tropes](../cast/character-tropes.md), [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md), [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md), [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md), [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md), [Mystery, Psychological & Horror](mystery-psychological-horror.md)

## 1. What villainess readers want

The villainess genre (akuyaku reijou) is a comedy of **dramatic irony**. The narrator knows the script of the world she's in, knows she's cast as the villain, and knows how she's supposed to end: exiled, executed, or disgraced at the graduation ball. Readers want:

1. **Doom avoidance as plot engine.** Every chapter is a small attempt to dodge a scripted catastrophe, the "doom flags". The fun is in the clever or ridiculous ways she tries.
2. **The gap between exterior and interior.** Outwardly she's a flawless, icy duke's daughter with a fan and a cutting remark. Inwardly she's a panicking former office worker screaming about game routes. This double voice is the genre's signature.
3. **Misunderstanding comedy.** She tries to avoid the prince, and he finds her intriguing. She tries to be nice to the heroine, and it's read as a scheme. Her self-preservation is read as virtue, or as villainy. See [Rom-Com & Comedy](romcom-and-comedy.md).
4. **A script that breaks.** Midway through, the world stops following the game. Characters have depths the game never showed. The heroine might be a reincarnator too. The "script" becomes a trap for the narrator's assumptions.
5. **Female friendship and female rivalry,** not just romance. Many of the best villainess stories are really about two women learning to see each other.
6. **Clothes, tea, and manners.** The texture of aristocratic life, rendered with loving specificity and a touch of mockery.

## 2. How the base voice shifts

### 2.1 The double-layered narrator

The core device is a narrator with **two registers running at once**:

- **Exterior (dialogue):** formal, elegant, and cold. "Oh my." "How very interesting." "I'm afraid I must decline." Full sentences, no contractions in the most formal characters, and an ojou-sama laugh ("Ohoho") if the tone is comic.
- **Interior (narration and thought):** modern, casual, anxious, and full of gamer and otaku vocabulary. "Wait wait wait, this is the fountain event. This is literally the CG where he falls in love with the heroine. Why am I standing in the CG?!"

The comedy is the **snap between them** within a single beat:

> "Your Highness," Rosalind said, dipping into a perfect curtsy, her voice as smooth and cool as chilled silk. "What a pleasant surprise."
> *NOT pleasant. Not a surprise. Why is he here? The fountain event is Route A, week three. It's week one! Did I trigger it by standing near water?! Is water a flag?!*

**Formatting:** Interior panic often uses *italics* in this genre, unlike the base voice's unitalicized free indirect thought, because the contrast between the two layers needs a visual marker. Choose one convention per book and log it in the [Story Bible](../templates/story-bible.md). Keep calmer interior thought unitalicized, as in the base voice, and reserve italics for the loud inner screams.

### 2.2 The analytical engine becomes "route analysis"

The base voice's [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md) (notice, quote back, hypothesize, suspect yourself, verdict) maps perfectly onto the villainess's game knowledge:

1. **Notice:** The prince smiled at her instead of the heroine.
2. **Quote back:** He said "Lady Rosalind," not "Everleigh." In the game, he only uses given names at affection level 3.
3. **Hypothesize:** Is this a bug? A hidden route? Did her reincarnation change the parameters?
4. **Suspect yourself:** Or is she misreading a polite man through a game she played when she was twenty-two and sleep-deprived?
5. **Verdict:** "Whatever it is, it's a flag. Avoid."

This lets the villainess genre borrow everything the base voice does well.

### 2.3 Rhythm and comedy

- Comedy ratio: **50-70%** in comic villainess stories, 25-40% in the serious strain (court intrigue, execution-avoidance thrillers).
- Ballroom and tea scenes are **dialogue-dense**, often 60% dialogue, with subtext doing the work.
- Interior panic uses **short, stacked, exclamatory fragments**, which make a natural contrast with the long, polished sentences of aristocratic description.

### 2.4 Description density

**High for clothing, rooms, and food; low for everything else.** The genre's readers enjoy the gowns, but even here, tie description to character: the gown she chooses is a strategy ("Dark blue. Villainess red was a flag."). One or two details per outfit, never a catalog.

## 3. The doom-flag system

### 3.1 What a doom flag is

A **flag** is a game term for an event that sets a future outcome. A **doom flag** is any event that moves the narrator toward her scripted bad ending: bullying the heroine, a public argument with the prince, being seen near the scene of the "incident", and so on.

### 3.2 Using flags as structure

- **Keep a flag list** in the story bible: the scripted events of the original game, their triggers, their dates, and their outcomes. This is the villainess's version of a school calendar.
- **Each arc targets one flag.** The narrator plans to avoid it, the plan goes sideways, and the flag is dodged, altered, or unexpectedly triggered with a different result.
- **Flags evolve.** By mid-series, the narrator's flag knowledge should become unreliable. The world has diverged, and her greatest asset becomes a liability.

### 3.3 The flag tally (optional running gag)

The narrator can keep a mental count, rendered in the voice:

> Doom flags avoided this week: two.
> Doom flags accidentally triggered: one.
> New doom flags created by her own stupidity: she didn't want to count.

Use this device at most once every few chapters so it stays funny.

## 4. Genre tropes: executing well vs. failing

### 4.1 Memory awakening
**Well:** Fast, with a specific trigger (hitting her head on a teacup, seeing the prince's face, a fever). The two personalities merge rather than simply swapping. She remembers both childhoods, and that should occasionally hurt.
**Fails:** A total personality replacement with no trace of the original girl.

### 4.2 The engagement to the prince
**Well:** The engagement is a political contract with real stakes. The prince is a person, not a prize. His coldness has a reason, and so does his interest when it appears.
**Fails:** The prince exists only to be avoided, then to fall in love.

### 4.3 The heroine
**Well:** The game's heroine is one of three things. (a) A genuinely kind girl the narrator comes to respect or befriend. (b) A fellow reincarnator who wants the "happy ending" and sees the narrator as an obstacle. (c) A manipulator hiding behind innocence (see the two-faced type in [Character Tropes](../cast/character-tropes.md)). Pick one and plant clues early.
**Fails:** A flat saint or a flat villain.

### 4.4 The capture targets
**Well:** Each love interest from the "game" has a route with its own trauma and flag. The narrator knows their secrets because she played the routes, and that knowledge is both power and a moral problem. Is it right to use what she knows?
**Fails:** A lineup of pretty men with the same personality.

### 4.5 The loyal butler / maid
**Well:** The one character who was always on the villainess's side, even in the original game. They are the confidant (see the thesis speech in [Emotional Dialogue](../dialogue/emotional-dialogue.md)). Often the real love interest.
**Fails:** A servant used only for exposition.

### 4.6 The condemnation ball
**Well:** The climax of a whole arc or series. The prince (or someone) publicly accuses the villainess at the graduation ball. Whether it happens as scripted, is turned around, or is diverted entirely is the payoff for everything the narrator has done. Treat it like the outburst scene in school drama: packed room, public stakes, one voice against the crowd. See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md).
**Fails:** It's resolved by a surprise witness who was never set up.

### 4.7 The villainess becomes beloved
**Well:** Her attempts at self-preservation (being kind to servants, avoiding conflict, starting a business to fund her exile) accidentally make her admired. The comedy is her bafflement. The heart is that she was always capable of kindness.
**Fails:** Everyone loves her instantly for doing nothing.

### 4.8 The original villainess's soul
**Well:** A haunting thread. What happened to the girl whose body this was? Sometimes she lingers in memories, in reflexes, or in a diary found in a drawer.
**Fails:** Never considered.

### 4.9 The inverted premise
Variants that refresh the genre:
- She reincarnates as the **heroine** and wants to avoid the romance.
- She reincarnates as a **background mob character** and watches the drama from the gallery (this maps perfectly onto the base voice's observer POV).
- She's the **villainess's maid**, trying to save her mistress.
- The narrator is a **man** reincarnated as the villainess.

## 5. Aristocratic texture

### 5.1 Address and titles

Aristocratic settings replace Japanese honorifics with a title system. Keep it **consistent** and log it. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).

| Address | Use |
|---|---|
| Your Highness | Royals |
| Your Grace | Dukes and duchesses |
| Lord / Lady + given name | Children of high nobles, in polite company |
| Lady + surname | More formal or distant |
| Given name alone | Family, fiancés (sometimes), or scandalous intimacy |
| "Milady" / "Young Mistress" | Servants to the noble daughter |
| Given name + "-sama" | Optional if the book is Japanese-flavored in its localization |

**Name escalation is plot here too**, even more than in school settings. A prince calling her "Rosalind" instead of "Lady Everleigh" in public is a scandal.

### 5.2 Ojou-sama speech

- A formal register, full sentences, and delicate cruelty. Much can be conveyed through what is *not* said.
- A signature laugh, if the tone is comic ("Ohohoho!"), used sparingly as a mask. The narrator can be embarrassed by her own laugh.
- The fan: opened to hide a smile, snapped shut to end a conversation, tapped against the palm when thinking. Treat it as a body-language tag.
- Veiled insults: "What a *unique* dress." "How brave of you to wear that color."

### 5.3 Settings

- The academy (royal or noble), with its own class hierarchy and a scholarship commoner, usually the heroine. The school map from [School Drama §3](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md#3-the-social-map-hierarchy-as-setting) applies directly.
- Tea parties are battlefields. Seating, who pours, and who is invited are all moves.
- The ballroom: dances, dance cards, and who asks whom.
- The duke's estate: a dysfunctional family, cold parents, and a sibling who was a capture target or rival.
- The garden and greenhouse: a private meeting space, like the school rooftop.

## 6. Scene recipes

### 6.1 The awakening
**Beats:** A mundane noble moment, a trigger, a rush of memories, a mirror, the realization ("I'm Rosalind Everleigh. The villainess. The one who gets executed in the bad end."), and panic under a flawless exterior.
**Hook:** A doom-flag event is about to happen *today*.

### 6.2 The tea party ambush
**Beats:** Invitation, seating politics, veiled insults, and the heroine's arrival. The narrator must choose between the scripted villain line and something new, and her choice is misread.

### 6.3 The prince's unexpected interest
**Beats:** She tries to be boring. He finds it fascinating. Route analysis panic. A closing line where he says something that isn't in the game.

### 6.4 Rescuing a future enemy
**Beats:** She remembers a capture target's tragic backstory and intervenes years early (saving his mother, stopping his abuse, befriending him as a child), and changes the future.

### 6.5 The heroine alone
**Beats:** The narrator finds the heroine crying, or scheming, or reading the same manga she did in her old life. The heroine's true nature is revealed.

### 6.6 The graduation ball
See §4.6 and [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md).

## 7. Chapter and arc shapes

### 7.1 Opening arc (5-8 chapters)
1. Awakening and the doom revealed (ch. 1)
2. First flag, dodged clumsily (ch. 2)
3. A misread act of kindness becomes a new ally (ch. 3)
4. Meeting the prince and the unexpected interest (ch. 3-4)
5. Meeting the heroine and her true type hinted (ch. 4-5)
6. The first real divergence from the game (ch. 5-6)
7. A cut-away: someone else is watching the narrator, possibly another reincarnator (end of arc)

### 7.2 Series spine
Flag arcs build toward the condemnation ball, whose outcome depends on accumulated choices. After the ball, the story can continue into the "after the game" phase: politics, marriage, rule, an unwritten future where no route guides her. Readers find that uncharted phase thrilling, because it's the moment she stops being a player and starts being a person.

### 7.3 Chapter shape
Base voice structure: numbered sections that end on tilts. Villainess chapters often **end on a misunderstanding cut-away**, a short scene from another character's POV showing how they (mis)read the narrator's action.

> Meanwhile, in the east wing, the Crown Prince stared at the rose Rosalind had "rejected."
> "She said," he murmured to his aide, "that roses are *wasted* on her."
> "Yes, Your Highness."
> "How modest," said the Crown Prince, and put the rose in water.

## 8. Sample passages

Original cast for this file:
- **Rosalind Everleigh**: the duke's daughter and villainess of the otome game *Lilies of the Crown Academy*. Her memories from her past life are of a 26-year-old Osaka pharmacist who cleared every route twice.
- **Crown Prince Alistair**: cold in the game and puzzlingly warm to her now.
- **Marie Clement**: the commoner heroine. Type to be revealed.
- **Cedric**: Rosalind's butler, deadpan, loyal, and the confidant.

### 8.1 Awakening (≈450 words)

> Rosalind Everleigh remembered her previous life because of a scone.
> It happened at the Duchess of Ferne's spring tea, in the rose garden, at precisely the moment a footman set down a tiered stand of pastries. Rosalind, age fourteen, reached for a scone with clotted cream. Her fingers touched the plate.
> And a woman in Osaka, twenty-six, in scrubs, ate a konbini melon pan in a pharmacy break room at 2 a.m. and opened a game on her phone.
> *Lilies of the Crown Academy.* Title screen: a pink-haired girl, a golden-haired prince, and in the corner, a sharp-faced blonde in crimson with a fan and a sneer.
> *Rosalind Everleigh. The villainess.*
> Rosalind's hand froze above the scone.
> "Lady Rosalind? Are you unwell?"
> Five noble daughters were watching her. Their mothers were watching them watch her. Somewhere to her left, the Duchess of Ferne's pug was watching the scone.
> Bad End One. Exiled to the northern convent.
> Bad End Two. Stripped of title, sent to a border town, dies of fever.
> Bad End Three. Executed. In the town square. There was a CG for it. She'd skipped it because she'd felt sorry for her.
> For *her*. For Rosalind. For *me*.
> "Lady Rosalind?"
> Rosalind lifted her chin. The movement was automatic, practiced, fourteen years of posture lessons deep in her spine. Her other hand opened her fan with a crisp *snap*.
> "I'm perfectly well," she said, in a voice like chilled silk. "I was merely admiring the roses."
> *I'm going to die. I'm going to die in a town square in a very nice dress.*
> The girls exchanged glances. Of course they did. Rosalind Everleigh did not "admire" things. Rosalind Everleigh assessed them and found them wanting.
> Wait.
> She did the math. Fourteen. The game began at sixteen, on the first day of the Crown Academy, the day the heroine arrived. Two years. She had two years before the script started.
> Two years to become someone the script didn't know what to do with.
> Rosalind closed her fan.
> "Your Grace," she said to the Duchess of Ferne. "These scones are exquisite. Might I trouble your cook for the recipe?"
> The silence around the table was absolute.
> The pug sneezed.
> Rosalind Everleigh had never in her life asked anyone for anything. She watched five noble daughters and five noble mothers file the fact away, each for her own reasons, and she felt the first doom flag of her new life tremble, just slightly, on its pole.

**What to notice:** A specific trigger (a scone). The past life is anchored in one detail (melon pan, a 2 a.m. break room). The double layer (a "chilled silk" voice against italicized panic). Bad ends as a list, then the pronoun shift ("For *her*. For *me*."). The social map read from faces. A small act of divergence. A closing tilt with the flag metaphor.

### 8.2 Route analysis at the fountain (≈420 words)

> The fountain event was Route A, week three.
> Rosalind knew this the way she knew her own birthday. The heroine, Marie Clement, trips on the fountain's edge, drops her books, and the Crown Prince, passing by, kneels to help her. Soft music. Cherry petals. CG unlocked. Affection plus ten.
> It was week *one*.
> And the person kneeling by the fountain picking up books was Rosalind, because she'd been the one who tripped.
> "Lady Everleigh."
> *Do not look up. Do not look up. If you don't look up, it's not an event.*
> She looked up.
> Crown Prince Alistair stood over her, the sun behind him doing something unfair to his hair. He was holding her copy of *Advanced Botanical Alchemy*, which had landed in the fountain and was now, like her dignity, damp.
> "Your Highness." She rose and curtsied, all in one motion, the way her tutor had drilled her until her knees ached. "Forgive me. How clumsy."
> "Clumsy," he repeated.
> He held out the book. She took it. Their fingers did not touch. She made very sure of that.
> In the game, Alistair was ice. Polite to everyone, warm to no one, until Marie thawed him over three routes and a tragic backstory involving his mother. He called Rosalind "Lady Everleigh" for the entire game in a voice like a closing door.
> He was looking at her now as though she were a puzzle he'd found in a drawer.
> "You study alchemy," he said.
> "A little."
> "Botanical alchemy. It's an unusual choice for a duke's daughter."
> *Because in Bad End Two I die of fever in a border town and I am going to learn every medicine in this world if it kills me. Which is the point. That it doesn't.*
> "I find plants restful," Rosalind said. "They don't talk."
> Alistair's mouth moved.
> It took her a second to identify the expression, because it had never once appeared in the game's sprite set.
> He was trying not to laugh.
> "..."
> Wait.
> Wait wait wait.
> That wasn't a Route A reaction. That wasn't a reaction from *any* route. She'd cleared them all, twice, in a pharmacy break room. Alistair did not laugh. Alistair did not have a laugh sprite.
> Was she wrong about the game? Was the world already diverging? Or was she wrong about *him*, and had always been, and the game had simply never bothered to show him being a person?
> "Lady Rosalind," the Crown Prince said, testing the name like a new step on a staircase. "I look forward to seeing more of your alchemy."
> He left.
> Rosalind stood by the fountain holding a wet book, and felt a doom flag, somewhere, very quietly, snap its rope.

**What to notice:** The analytical engine as route analysis (the missing laugh sprite). A name escalation beat ("Lady Rosalind" instead of "Lady Everleigh"). The interior-exterior snap ("They don't talk."). The self-suspicion question chain ends on the human possibility. The flag metaphor is paid off with a variation.

### 8.3 The butler's thesis (≈330 words)

> "Milady."
> Cedric set the tea down at her elbow. Chamomile, which meant he'd decided she was upset. He was always right about that, and it was infuriating.
> "I'm not upset."
> "Of course not, milady." He poured. "May I speak freely?"
> "You always do."
> "Then." He straightened, the silver pot still in his gloved hand. "For two years you have avoided His Highness, befriended the scullery staff, learned to brew fever tinctures, and saved the Marquess's son from drowning in a lake you were never supposed to be near. You have done all of this, as far as I can tell, because you believe something terrible will happen to you."
> "..."
> "I don't know what you think is coming. I won't ask." A pause. "But I have served this house since before you were born, milady, and I would like to observe that the girl who did all those things is not someone this household would ever send to the gallows."
> "..."
> "Whatever script you think you're in," Cedric said quietly, "you stopped following it some time ago."
> Rosalind stared at the tea.
> The chamomile steamed. Her reflection in it was small and gold and trembling a little, and she realized, distantly, that the trembling was her hands.
> Two years of flags. Two years of lists and routes and bad ends. Two years of treating every person in this world as an event she had to trigger or avoid.
> The scullery maids weren't events. The Marquess's son wasn't an event. Cedric, who had brought her chamomile every bad night for two years without once asking why, was not an event.
> "...Cedric."
> "Milady."
> "The tea's too hot."
> "My apologies, milady."
> He didn't move to take it. She didn't ask him to. They stayed like that, the butler and the villainess, while the tea cooled, and for once she didn't think about the game at all.

**What to notice:** The confidant speech in a formal register. The monologue-and-silence duet, with the narrator's `"..."` replies. The realization is rendered as a list of people, not a list of events. She deflects to the tea instead of crying. The earnest peak has no joke inside it, only gentle deadpan after.

## 9. Common failure modes

1. **Interior panic nonstop.** Every line is screaming. *Fix:* Reserve loud italics for real flag moments, and let calm interior thought carry the rest.
2. **The game explained in full.** Pages of route summaries. *Fix:* Reveal game knowledge only when a scene triggers it.
3. **Everyone is a prize.** Love interests have no inner lives. *Fix:* Each capture target has a wound the game never showed.
4. **The heroine as strawman.** *Fix:* Pick her type (§4.3) and seed ambiguity.
5. **Modern slang in noble mouths.** *Fix:* Keep aristocratic dialogue formal. Modern voice lives only in the narrator's interior.
6. **No cost to divergence.** Changing the script only helps. *Fix:* Every avoided flag creates an unknown one, since her knowledge erodes.
7. **Forgetting the original Rosalind.** *Fix:* One memory, reflex, or diary thread per arc.

## 10. Genre revision checklist

- [ ] Are the exterior (formal dialogue) and interior (modern panic) registers both present and clearly distinct?
- [ ] Is the italic convention for loud interior thought consistent with the story bible?
- [ ] Does this chapter engage a specific doom flag, and is its status changed by the end?
- [ ] Is route analysis grounded in specific game details, and does the narrator doubt it at least once?
- [ ] Is at least one character shown deviating from their "game" personality?
- [ ] Are titles and address forms consistent, and is any name escalation noticed?
- [ ] Is aristocratic description tied to character or strategy, not cataloged?
- [ ] Does a misunderstanding cut-away (if used) show how another character misreads her?
- [ ] Does the heroine (and every capture target) have an inner life?
- [ ] Does the chapter end on a tilt, whether a flag snapping, a new flag, or a divergence?
