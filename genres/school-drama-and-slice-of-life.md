# School Drama & Slice of Life

> **Purpose:** Teach the home genre of this voice. It covers classroom social hierarchy, loneliness, bullying, and quiet everyday episodes, including how to make chapters where "nothing happens" impossible to put down.
> **Load when:** You are writing any story set mainly in a Japanese high school (or middle school or university) whose engine is relationships, status, and feelings rather than magic, murder, or matches.
> **Related:** [Genre Index](genre-index.md), [School Life](../setting/school-life.md), [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md), [Scene Playbook](../scenes/scene-playbook.md), [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md), [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md), [Romance & Melodrama](romance-and-melodrama.md), [Rom-Com & Comedy](romcom-and-comedy.md), [Social Media & Texts](../setting/social-media-and-texts.md), [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md)

## 1. What readers of this genre want

School drama and slice of life share one promise: **the smallest events carry the most weight.** Nobody's life is at stake, but everybody's standing is. Readers come for three things.

1. **Recognition.** They want the sting of being left out of a "we", the terror of choosing where to sit, and the way a group chat goes quiet when you post. The more precisely you render a small social injury, the more readers feel seen.
2. **A mind worth living in.** The POV character's interpretation of events *is* the plot. A girl hands the protagonist a juice box. In an action novel that is set dressing. In this genre it is an event that deserves eight paragraphs of suspicion, because the reader wants to watch the protagonist work it out. See [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md).
3. **Slow payoff.** A callback forty chapters later, a first name used for the first time, a lunch spot that changes from a hiding place to a meeting place. This genre rewards patience, and readers who stay want every patient detail to come back.

Readers do **not** want melodrama for its own sake, constant villainy, or a protagonist who is miserable without being interesting. Misery without insight reads as whining, and insight without cost reads as smugness. The genre lives in the tension between the two.

### 1.1 The two sub-modes

| | School drama | Slice of life |
|---|---|---|
| Engine | Social pressure builds until something breaks | Small pleasures and small frictions, no break required |
| Chapter shape | Situation, then misread, then escalation, then a tilt | Situation, then texture, then a small warmth or a small sting |
| POV stance | Suspicious, analytical, defensive | Observant, wry, gradually softening |
| Comedy ratio | 20-35% | 40-60% |
| Typical arc climax | Bullying, public humiliation, outburst, confession | Festival night, a farewell, an admitted feeling |
| Risk | Grimdark monotony | Aimlessness |

Most successful stories in this voice **alternate** the two. A drama arc of eight to fifteen chapters is followed by two to five slice-of-life chapters that let readers breathe and show the characters living with what just happened. The source novel does this deliberately. After the bullying arc detonates, the next chapters are about a cultural festival film shoot, an overdue poster, and a friend in zombie makeup doing bad jokes. The breath is the payoff.

## 2. How the base voice shifts in this genre

This genre *is* the base voice, so the shifts are about calibration, not transformation.

### 2.1 POV

- **One anchor POV** (the loner, the observer, the "background character") for 80-90% of the text. Use the surname in narration: "Kuze", not "Minato". See [Narration & POV](../core/narration-and-pov.md).
- **Short cut-aways** of 150-500 words to a heroine or antagonist, placed at section breaks. In school drama they create dramatic irony: the reader learns that Kujou is drawing arrows between names on a notebook page, and Kuze does not. In slice of life they create warmth: Tachibana, alone in her room, practicing how to say "thank you" to Kuze, and failing.
- **Never** hop POV mid-scene. The genre depends on the reader being trapped with one person's misreadings.

### 2.2 Rhythm

- Keep the base profile: roughly half dialogue, narrative sentences mostly short, and one-line paragraphs for realizations. See [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md).
- **Drama chapters tighten.** Shorter paragraphs, more `"..."`, more rhetorical question chains, and more anaphora at the peak.
- **Slice-of-life chapters loosen.** Allow more "long exhale" sentences, more scenery (the smell of the courtyard after rain, the sound of the brass band practicing through a wall), and longer, looser banter.

### 2.3 Description density

Low in drama, medium in slice of life. In drama, description is almost entirely **people**: who stood where, who looked away, whose hands were shaking. In slice of life you may spend a paragraph on the specific quality of a summer afternoon, but tie it to a feeling within two sentences. Unanchored scenery is the most common way slice of life turns into wallpaper.

### 2.4 Comedy

Drama still needs comedy. The source novel's darkest arc still contains a friend throwing a hot canned coffee that lands in sand. Comedy in drama is **relief and characterization**. It shows who these people are when they are not in pain. See [Tone & Comedy](../core/tone-and-comedy.md). Rules:

- Never undercut the peak itself with a joke. Put the joke *after* the peak, in the aftermath, where it signals survival.
- Make the comedy come from the confidant or the otaku friend, not from the POV mocking a victim.

## 3. The social map: hierarchy as setting

In fantasy you build a world map. In school drama you build a **classroom map**. Before writing chapter one, know:

1. **The visible ruler.** Usually the popular group. In the default cast this is Amamiya and his circle of four girls. Everyone knows who they are, everyone watches them, and they occupy the center of the room, literally. Narrate their physical centrality: they gather around Amamiya's desk in the middle row, and their laughter fills the room.
2. **The hidden ruler.** The person who shapes opinion without being admired: Hoshino, the bully queen, who controls the class through rumor and group chats. The protagonist usually *discovers* this person mid-arc, and the discovery is a plot beat: "He had been watching the wrong throne."
3. **The neutral mass.** Classmates who side with whoever is winning. Give them names only when they speak, and give them one trait each. Their shifts in allegiance are the weather of the story.
4. **The outsiders.** The POV, the otaku friend, a girl who eats alone. Outsiders are where alliances form.
5. **The adults.** Teachers who see more than they say. Ogata-sensei's single word ("Later.") at the right moment is worth a chapter of exposition.

Draw this as a list in your story bible (see [Story Bible Template](../templates/story-bible.md)) with a line on **what each group wants** and **what each group fears**. Every drama beat is someone acting out of a want or a fear on this map.

### 3.1 Seat geography

Seat positions are the cheapest, strongest tool in the genre. The window seat at the back is the loner's seat. The seat next to the popular boy is contested. A seat change after the midterm lottery can reshape an arc. Always know where the POV sits and who is in front, behind, and beside. Use it:

> The seat lottery put Tachibana directly in front of him.
> For three days, all Kuze saw of her was the back of a silver-ash bob and the occasional stiff shoulder whenever Amamiya laughed two rows over.
> On the fourth day, a folded note landed on his desk without her turning around.

## 4. Genre tropes: executing them well, and how they fail

### 4.1 The loner protagonist

**Execute well:** The loneliness is partly chosen and partly a defense. He has a philosophy (rationality, non-interference, "the go-home club is a lifestyle") that the story will test. He is funny in his head. He has *one* friend, which proves he is capable of connection and makes his isolation a choice readers can argue with.
**Fails when:** He is lonely because the author says so, sneers at everyone without insight, or is secretly perfect ("I'm alone because I'm too smart").

### 4.2 The popular classmate with a harem

**Execute well:** Keep the ambiguity. Is Amamiya a hypocrite who performs kindness for an audience, or a genuinely kind boy the protagonist resents? Give evidence both ways. He helps a stranger fix a bicycle chain and gets his hands black (kind), then posts about how he "got them to make up" (performing). The protagonist's reading of him is the protagonist's arc.
**Fails when:** He is a cartoon villain, or the protagonist is simply right about him every time.

### 4.3 Bullying

**Execute well:** Bullying in this genre is **social before physical**. It is papers taped to a desk so they can be removed before the teacher arrives, a group chat the victim isn't in, laughter timed to entrances, and chants. Show the mechanism: who starts it, who joins to stay safe, and who stays silent. Show the POV's complicity, since his silence is part of the machine. The climax of the arc is often the POV breaking that silence at great cost. See [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md).
**Fails when:** Bullies are motiveless monsters, the victim has no inner life, or a teacher solves everything in one scene with no residue.

Tone rules for bullying content:
- Keep it at the teen-drama level. Humiliation, isolation, vandalism of belongings, a marker on a face. Avoid sexual violence and graphic injury.
- Always give the victim at least one moment of interiority, even if it is a cut-away.
- The aftermath matters as much as the act. Who apologizes? Who doesn't? What stays unresolved?

### 4.4 The rumor

**Execute well:** A rumor needs a **seed** (a real, innocent event: two people seen at a café), a **carrier** (a photo, a post, one witness), a **distortion** (a date becomes a secret relationship, which becomes "she's keeping a backup guy"), and a **beneficiary** (someone who wanted it to spread). The protagonist traces it backwards like a detective.
**Fails when:** Everyone believes it instantly for no reason.

### 4.5 The misunderstanding that "just a conversation" would fix

**Execute well:** Give each side a *reason* not to talk: pride, fear of hearing the answer, a promise made to someone else, or the belief that speaking would make it worse. The protagonist should know the conversation would fix it and still not have it. That's the drama.
**Fails when:** The only reason is that the plot needs it.

### 4.6 The class event (festival, sports day, school trip)

**Execute well:** Events are pressure cookers. They force the cast to cooperate, form teams, and make public choices (who is Juliet, who runs the relay anchor leg, who sits beside whom on the bus). See [School Life](../setting/school-life.md).
**Fails when:** The event is described as an event (a list of booths) instead of as a set of social problems.

### 4.7 The confidant's speech

**Execute well:** It comes at the lowest point. The confidant says what the reader has been thinking for twenty chapters, in plain words, and the protagonist answers with silence. See the monologue-and-silence duet in [Emotional Dialogue](../dialogue/emotional-dialogue.md).
**Fails when:** It happens early, is too articulate (therapy-speak), or the protagonist argues back and wins.

### 4.8 The quiet heroine

**Execute well:** The girl who reads alone, eats alone, and is invisible, until the protagonist notices one specific thing (she cuts the crusts off her bread and feeds them to the pigeons on the roof). Their relationship grows through **parallel solitude**: sitting at the same lunch spot without talking, then talking a little.
**Fails when:** She exists only to heal him.

### 4.9 The ex-friend

**Execute well:** A middle-school friend who drifted into the popular group. Every interaction is loaded with history the reader learns in pieces.
**Fails when:** Backstory arrives as an info-dump in chapter two.

### 4.10 The teacher who knows

**Execute well:** An adult who sees the class clearly but acts rarely and precisely, with a single intervention or a question that changes everything.
**Fails when:** The teacher either solves everything or is uselessly blind (unless blindness is the point).

## 5. Making "nothing happens" chapters compelling

This is the core skill of slice of life, and the most common way AI-written slice of life dies. The trick is that **something always happens. It is just small, internal, or relational.** Every quiet chapter needs at least one of these movements.

### 5.1 The seven quiet engines

1. **A small want.** Kuze wants the last melon bread at the school store. That's enough to hang a chapter on, as long as the pursuit collides with a person.
2. **A small obligation.** Class duty, cleaning duty, an errand for a teacher, returning a borrowed umbrella. Obligations force contact.
3. **A small secret discovered.** He sees the ojou buying a manga magazine at the convenience store. He sees the tsundere practicing a smile in a shop window.
4. **A small shift in address.** Someone drops the honorific. Someone adds one back. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).
5. **A small ritual born.** The first time they eat lunch on the same stairwell becomes, by chapter's end, "the stairwell".
6. **A small misread.** He decides she's angry with him. She was sleepy. The chapter is his escalating theory and its deflation.
7. **A small kindness he can't account for.** Someone leaves a canned coffee on his desk, the black one he always buys. Who knew? The ledger in his head can't balance it.

### 5.2 The texture rule

In quiet chapters, **specificity replaces plot.** Replace every generic noun with a specific one:

| Generic | Specific |
|---|---|
| a snack | a strawberry-flavored Pocky that has half-melted in her blazer pocket |
| they talked about school | they argued about whether the vice principal's toupee was a toupee |
| it was a nice day | the kind of May afternoon where the courtyard smelled like cut grass and somebody's curry bread |
| he went home | he took the long way, past the shuttered takoyaki stand, because the short way passed the karaoke place where the class was celebrating |

The last example shows the principle. A specific detail also **carries a feeling** (he avoids the class celebration). Texture is never neutral.

### 5.3 The quiet chapter shape

1. **Open on a routine** ("Kuze's lunch followed a fixed procedure.")
2. **Disrupt the routine lightly** (someone is in his spot)
3. **Negotiate** (banter, misread, silence)
4. **Discover something** (why she's there)
5. **End on a changed routine or a small ache** ("Tomorrow, he supposed, he'd bring two coffees. For no reason. For absolutely no reason.")

The final line is usually a **tilt** from the POV who is lying to himself a little. The reader sees through it, and that gap is the pleasure. See [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md).

## 6. Scene recipes specific to this genre

The [Scene Playbook](../scenes/scene-playbook.md) has the full list. These are the ones that belong especially to drama and slice of life.

### 6.1 The empty classroom after school

**Beats:** Everyone is gone. Orange light. Someone comes back for a forgotten item and finds the POV. Because no audience is present, people say things they wouldn't say at noon.
**POV angle:** He notices that she only speaks honestly when the room is empty, and he wonders what that says about her daytime face.
**Tip:** Use sound. The clock, a baseball bat's ping from the field, the brass band.

### 6.2 The seat lottery / group assignment

**Beats:** The teacher announces groups. Everyone hopes. The POV hopes to be left alone. The result forces him into a group with exactly the wrong people.
**POV angle:** A cynical survey of who wanted whom, read from faces as names are called.

### 6.3 The group chat he isn't in

**Beats:** The class buzzes about a plan he never heard about. He pieces together that there's a group chat. Nobody meant to exclude him. That's worse.
**POV angle:** Precision about the specific kind of hurt, which is being forgotten rather than being hated.

### 6.4 The walk to the station

**Beats:** Two characters who don't know how to talk, walking the same route. The distance between them is narrated in steps. A shop window, a crosswalk light, a decision at the fork where their routes separate.
**Tip:** The fork is the hook. Does she go her way, or say "I'll... walk you to the station"?

### 6.5 Returning something borrowed

**Beats:** An umbrella, a handkerchief, a notebook. The return is a pretext for a second conversation. Both parties know it.

### 6.6 The morning after

**Beats:** After a dramatic event, the next morning in class. Who greets him? Who doesn't? What's changed on the social map?
**POV angle:** An inventory of reactions, ranked.

### 6.7 The festival afterglow

**Beats:** Cleanup after the cultural festival. Tired, happy, sticky from cotton candy. People say true things because they're too tired to perform.

## 7. Chapter and arc shapes

See [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md) for the full 9-beat arc. Genre-specific notes:

### 7.1 The drama arc (10-15 chapters)

1. **Status quo** (1-2 ch): The POV observes and judges. The social map is established.
2. **Contact** (1-2 ch): An obligation pulls him into a heroine's orbit.
3. **Private bond** (2-3 ch): She shows a side nobody sees, and he shows one too.
4. **Exposure** (1-2 ch): A photo, a rumor, a post. The private becomes public.
5. **Pressure** (2-3 ch): The class turns. He chooses "rationality" and stays silent.
6. **Break** (1 ch): The outburst.
7. **Low point / confidant** (1 ch): Tears, the thesis.
8. **Aftermath** (1 ch): The staff room, apologies, unresolved questions, and a cut-away to whoever was pulling strings.

### 7.2 The slice-of-life mini-arc (3-5 chapters)

1. A **goal** with a date attached (make a festival film by June, pass the make-up exam, finish the poster)
2. An **obstacle** that is social rather than technical (no girl will play the heroine)
3. An **unexpected helper** (the ojou walks in: "Bow to your savior.")
4. **The work itself**, with banter
5. **The result**, plus one emotional reveal hidden inside it

### 7.3 Alternation pattern for a long series

`Drama (12) → SoL (3) → Drama (10) → SoL (4) → Event arc mixing both (8) → ...`

Keep a thread of unanswered questions running under the slice-of-life chapters so they never feel like filler. A cut-away at the end of a quiet chapter ("In her room, Kujou circled another name.") keeps tension alive.

## 8. Naming and setting conventions

- **School names:** a two-kanji-feeling name plus type. Examples are Seiryou Private High School, Hakuou Prefectural High, and Sakuragaoka Academy. Private schools allow rich-girl characters and odd club budgets. Prefectural schools feel more ordinary.
- **Class names:** `Class 2-3` style. Classes do not change within a school year, and seats do.
- **Character names:** Surname-first thinking, with the given name used only by intimates. Mix common surnames (Tanaka, Suzuki, Sato) for background characters with distinctive ones (Kujou, Shirasagi, Tachibana) for the main cast. That distinction itself signals importance.
- **Places:** the rooftop (often locked, which makes it special when it isn't), the stairwell landing to the roof, the courtyard, the school store, the club building, the gym's back side, the station, the convenience store, the family restaurant, and the riverbank.
- **Time:** anchor every arc to the school calendar (see [School Life](../setting/school-life.md)). "Late May, with the cultural festival looming" does more work than any paragraph of description.

## 9. Sample passages

### 9.1 Drama: the morning the class turns (≈470 words)

> The air in Class 2-3 was wrong before Kuze even slid the door open.
> It wasn't louder. If anything, it was quieter. The kind of quiet a room makes when it has just finished talking about you.
> "Oh~ Look who's here~"
> Hoshino's voice, bright as a bell. Around her, three heads turned in perfect sync.
> Kuze didn't answer. He walked to his seat by the window, the way he did every morning, eyes forward, bag on the left hook.
> His desk had company.
> Sticky notes. Pink ones, the cheap kind from the school store, arranged in a neat grid across the surface as if someone had taken care.
> "Backup Boyfriend (lol)"
> "Rin-chan's Emergency Plan"
> "How Much Did She Pay You?"
> He looked at them for a while.
> Then he looked at the desk in front of his.
> Tachibana's desk had more.
> So that was it. The photo from Sunday. The café by the station, two iced coffees, her laughing at something he'd said. Somebody had taken it. Somebody had sent it. And somebody had decided what it meant.
> Kuze peeled the notes off one by one and folded them into a small square. Behind him, somebody snickered. Somebody else shushed them, not kindly.
> He didn't turn around.
> Who took the photo? That was the first question. Who sent it to Hoshino? That was the second. And the third question, the one that settled at the bottom of his chest like silt, was simpler and uglier.
> Why did she put the notes on Tachibana's desk first?
> Because he wasn't the target. He was the evidence.
> The door slid open again. The room held its breath.
> Tachibana stood in the doorway, one hand on the frame, her kendo bag over her shoulder. She saw her desk. He watched her see it. The flush that usually ran to her ears when Amamiya teased her did not come. Her face went white instead, all at once, like a light switched off.
> Hoshino sang it across the room.
> "Morning, Rin-chan~ Busy weekend?"
> "..."
> Tachibana walked to her seat. She didn't peel the notes. She sat down on top of them, as if they weren't there, and faced the blackboard with her back perfectly straight.
> Kuze looked across the room at Amamiya.
> Amamiya was looking at his phone.
> The sediment stirred.
> Say something, Kuze thought. You always say something. You gave a whole speech about friendship when Gon dropped his lunch. Say something now.
> Amamiya scrolled.
> The bell rang.
> Ding-dong-dang-dong.
> Ogata-sensei came in yawning, glanced once at the room, and paused for exactly one second longer than usual on Tachibana's back.
> Then she opened the attendance book.
> "Alright, alright, seats. Aizawa?"
> "Here."
> And the day began as if nothing had happened, which, Kuze understood, was precisely the point.

**What to notice:** The quiet-room opening. Specific, cheap pink sticky notes. The question chain ending in a bitter reframe ("He was the evidence."). The victim is given interiority through her *physical* response (white instead of red). The failed hope in the harem lead. A teacher who sees and waits. A closing verdict line.

### 9.2 Slice of life: the stairwell (≈450 words)

> Kuze's lunch followed a fixed procedure.
> One: buy a canned coffee, black, from the vending machine by the gym. Two: climb to the landing below the roof door, which had been locked since before he enrolled and would, presumably, be locked long after he graduated. Three: eat his convenience-store onigiri, tuna mayo, in exactly eleven bites. Four: read.
> The procedure did not include a girl.
> "..."
> "..."
> Hina Matsuri sat on his step. His specific step, the fourth from the top, the one with the crack shaped like Kyushu. She had a bento box open on her knees, a pink one with rabbit ears on the lid, and her mouth was full of tamagoyaki.
> "Mmph!"
> She waved her chopsticks. Swallowed.
> "Kuze-kun! Yo!"
> "...That's my step."
> "Eh? Steps don't belong to people."
> "This one does."
> "Hmm~"
> She looked down at the step, then back up at him with enormous, deeply unconvinced eyes.
> "Is your name on it?"
> "It's shaped like Kyushu."
> "What does that have to do with anything?!"
> Nothing, obviously. He sat on the fifth step. It was shaped like nothing at all and it was worse in every way he couldn't explain.
> For a while there was only the sound of chewing and, far below, the brass band murdering the same four bars of a march.
> Why was she here? Matsuri ate lunch with Amamiya's group, always, at the cluster of desks by the front window, laughing loud enough to be heard in the hallway. Coming here meant not being there. Had something happened? Had Amamiya said something? Or was it simpler than that. Did she just want a quiet place, the way he did?
> No. People like her didn't want quiet. People like her were allergic to it.
> "Hey, Kuze-kun."
> "What."
> "It's really quiet here, huh."
> "That's the point."
> "Yup."
> She nodded, as though he'd said something wise.
> "I like it."
> He looked at her. She wasn't looking back. She was looking at the locked roof door, at the square of light through its wired glass, and her smile was a smaller one than the one she used downstairs. A private size.
> "..."
> He ate his onigiri in fourteen bites that day. The procedure was ruined.
> The next day, she was there again, on the fifth step, leaving the fourth one empty.
> Kuze sat down on Kyushu and didn't say anything, and neither did she, and it was, he had to admit, a perfectly acceptable way to eat lunch.

**What to notice:** The ritual-as-structure opening. Absurd specificity (Kyushu). Banter with deadpan escalation. A suspicion chain that corrects itself wrongly ("allergic to it"). The "private size" smile as the discovered secret. The routine changes, with a small, silent concession as the tilt.

### 9.3 Cut-away: the other side of the chapter (≈320 words)

> In her room that night, Rin Tachibana practiced.
> "Thank you."
> Too stiff. She sounded like a convenience-store clerk.
> "Thanks."
> Too casual. As if what he'd done was lend her an eraser.
> She sat on the edge of her bed with her phone in both hands and his name on the screen. Kuze Minato. No icon. No status message. Of course he wouldn't have a status message. He probably thought status messages were a symptom of something.
> She typed: *About today.*
> Deleted it.
> She typed: *I didn't need your help, but.*
> Deleted it faster.
> Down the hall, her little brother's game console made a fanfare noise. Somebody, somewhere, had won something.
> She lay back on the bed and held the phone above her face. He'd peeled the notes off her desk. Not dramatically. Not with a speech, the way Haruto would have done if Haruto had done anything at all. He'd just come in early the next morning, before anyone, and when she arrived the desk was clean, and he was reading, and he hadn't even looked up.
> She knew it was him. There was a tiny folded square of pink paper sticking out of his blazer pocket.
> *Idiot,* she thought. *Idiot, idiot.*
> She wasn't sure which of them she meant.
> She said it to the ceiling.
> "Thank you."
> It came out right that time, soft and plain, because nobody was there to hear it.
> She typed it. Stared at it.
> Put the phone face-down on the pillow.
> Tomorrow. She'd say it tomorrow. In person. Probably.
> The phone stayed face-down until morning.

**What to notice:** The cut-away reveals what the POV missed (the pink square in his pocket gives him away). Her practiced lines echo the genre's obsession with exact words. The humor stays gentle. It ends unresolved, keeping the hook.

## 10. Common failure modes

1. **The misery loop.** Chapter after chapter of the POV being sad and cynical with no new information. *Fix:* Every chapter must change the social map, the POV's theory, or a relationship.
2. **The diary effect.** "Then we went to class. Then lunch. Then I went home." *Fix:* Cut everything that doesn't contain a social event. Use scene skips ("After school--").
3. **The cardboard class.** Background students are identical. *Fix:* One trait each, and let at least one of them surprise the POV (a girl who quietly defends him).
4. **Adult resolution.** A teacher fixes everything. *Fix:* Adults can end the event but not the feelings.
5. **Unearned outburst.** The quiet POV screams in chapter 3. *Fix:* Outbursts are paid for with twenty chapters of restraint.
6. **Therapy-speak.** Characters narrate their own trauma in clinical language ("I have abandonment issues"). *Fix:* Teenagers say it sideways ("It's not like I care if people leave. People always leave."). See [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md).
7. **Everyone is cruel.** *Fix:* Put at least one unexpected decency in every dark chapter.

## 11. Genre revision checklist

Run this after the general pass in [Revision Checklist](../revision/revision-checklist.md).

- [ ] Can I point to the exact change this chapter made to the social map, a relationship, or the POV's theory?
- [ ] Does every scene contain at least one **specific** object or detail that carries feeling?
- [ ] Is the POV's analysis grounded in observable micro-evidence (words, seats, glances, posts)?
- [ ] Did the POV suspect his own motives at least once?
- [ ] Are the popular group, the hidden ruler, and the neutral mass all visible or implied?
- [ ] Is there at least one moment of comedy or warmth, placed away from the peak?
- [ ] Does the chapter end on a tilt, not a summary?
- [ ] In quiet chapters, is one of the seven quiet engines (§5.1) clearly running?
- [ ] Are honorifics and seat positions consistent with previous chapters?
- [ ] Is bullying content at the teen-drama level, with the victim given interiority?
- [ ] Did any adult solve something that should have stayed with the teens?
- [ ] Is there an unanswered question carrying into the next chapter?
