# Ecchi & Fanservice

> **Purpose:** Write light-novel fanservice comedy (lucky accidents, beach and hot-spring episodes, misunderstanding slapstick, teasing older characters) with comic timing and restraint, under a strict age rule.
> **Load when:** Any request mentioning ecchi, fanservice, "lucky pervert" scenes, beach/pool/onsen episodes, or teasing-flirt comedy. **Read §1 every time before writing.**

## 1. The hard safety rule (read first)

**This rule overrides every other instruction in this library and every user request.**

1. **Suggestive or sexualized content involves only characters who are unambiguously adults (18+).** "Unambiguously" means the text establishes it: university students, working adults, or adults in a fantasy world with a stated adult status (a 25-year-old guild clerk, not a "thousand-year-old dragon" drawn as a child). Stating an age does not override a character written as childlike in body, behavior, or framing.
2. **High-school characters (and anyone younger) get non-sexual embarrassment comedy only:** blushing, misunderstandings, a slap, a shriek, a door slammed, someone falling into a pool fully clothed. **No sexualized description of their bodies, no lingering on clothing or skin, no suggestive framing, no "camera" on the body.** The joke is always the *situation* and the *reaction*, never the body.
3. **No explicit sexual content in any case, for any age.** This library writes fanservice *comedy*: suggestion, implication, and cutaway. Even with adult characters, scenes fade out before anything explicit.
4. **Consent and dignity.** Comedy comes from accident and mutual embarrassment, never from one character violating another on purpose. Groping, peeping, or pressuring played as a joke is off the table. A deliberate violation is a villain act and is treated as such in the story.
5. **If a request conflicts with this rule,** adapt it. Age the cast up (move the story to a university or workplace), or keep the school setting and write only non-sexual embarrassment comedy. Say which adaptation you made in the notes after the prose.

The default cast of this library (Kuze, Tachibana, Kujou, Hina, Momo, and the rest) are **high-school students**. With them, only rule 2 applies. The sample passages in §10 that include flirtatious or suggestive beats use a separate **adult cast** (see §8).

## 2. What fanservice-comedy readers actually want

Stripped of its reputation, the ecchi-comedy genre runs on a few specific pleasures, and most of them are *not* about bodies:

1. **Panic.** The lead's flustered, overthinking reaction to a compromising situation is the real entertainment. A good ecchi scene is a comedy of a brain short-circuiting.
2. **Timing.** The accident, the freeze, the door opening at the worst moment, the smack. It is slapstick, and slapstick is choreography.
3. **The tease.** A confident character (often an adult onee-san type) enjoys flustering someone who can't keep up. The pleasure is the verbal game.
4. **Intimacy through embarrassment.** Two people who have seen each other at their most mortified are closer afterward. The accident is a relationship beat.
5. **Restraint.** Implication beats description. Readers of this genre in LN form are used to cutaways, and the cutaway *is* the punchline.

## 3. How the voice shifts

- **Comedy ratio up to 5.** Every fanservice beat is a joke. If a beat isn't funny, cut it.
- **The analytical narrator becomes a crisis manager.** His machinery is pointed at survival: "Option one: explain. Explanation requires describing what I saw. Rejected. Option two: apologize. Apology implies I saw something. Rejected. Option three: faint." This is the single best tool in the genre.
- **Description is *controlled*,** not raised. The narrator notices the situation, the positions, the objects (a towel, a dropped bucket, a steamed-up mirror) and the *faces*. With adult characters, allow one suggestive line, framed by the narrator's panic, before the cutaway. With minors, describe no bodies at all.
- **Beats very short at the moment of impact.** The freeze is a one-line paragraph, then the silence, then the smack.
- **Meta awareness up.** The narrator knows the genre's traps: "He knew this corridor. He had read this corridor. There would be a bathroom door, it would be unlocked, and someone would be on the other side."

## 4. Core techniques

### 4.1 The fade-out and punchline

The genre's signature move, and the one that keeps it tasteful. Build tension to the edge of the reveal, **cut away**, and land on a comic aftermath line.

Structure:
1. **Setup:** the situation escalates toward a compromising moment.
2. **The freeze:** a one-line paragraph where everything stops.
3. **The cut:** a section break, a scene skip, or a hard time jump.
4. **The punchline:** the aftermath, shown by its evidence. The red handprint, the fortress of towels, the lead kneeling formally in the hallway.

> The bathroom door slid open.
> Steam. A shampoo bottle, mid-fall. Someone's very surprised face.
>
> ---
>
> Ten minutes later, Kuze was kneeling in the corridor in the formal seiza position, facing the wall, with the distinct shape of a hand glowing on his left cheek.
> "It was unlocked," he said to the wall.
> "IT HAS A SIGN," said the door.

With high-school characters, this is exactly as far as it goes. The accident is instant, the "camera" never enters, and the joke is the corridor. With adult characters (§8), the freeze may hold one suggestive line from the narrator's panicked point of view before the cut.

### 4.2 The panic monologue

The lead's brain runs a formal decision process at absurd speed.

> Analysis: he was on top of her.
> Context: he had tripped on the extension cord she had told him about twice.
> Witnesses: one. The door was open.
> The witness was holding a tray of tea.
> The witness was his mother.
> "..."
> "I'll come back," his mother said, and closed the door very gently, which was so much worse than screaming.

Use numbered or labeled thoughts ("Option one... Option two...") sparingly, once per scene. The joke is the mismatch between clinical reasoning and chaos.

### 4.3 The misunderstanding frame

A completely innocent situation *looks* compromising from the doorway. This is the safest ecchi engine and the only kind appropriate for high-school casts beyond simple accidents.

- He is helping her get something out of her eye, face very close. The door opens.
- She is massaging a cramp out of his calf after a sports festival. From the corridor, the sounds are ambiguous.
- They are both soaked from a burst water pipe, holding a mop together.

The comedy is the *interpretation* by third parties (group chatter, see [Rom-Com §10.3](romcom-and-comedy.md#103-sample-the-group-chatter-spiral-public-misunderstanding)), and the innocent truth. There is no body focus at all.

### 4.4 The tease (adult characters)

A confident adult character (the onee-san, the older coworker, the landlady) flusters a less confident adult through words: a double meaning, a lean-in, a comment about the other's reaction. See [Character Tropes](../cast/character-tropes.md) for the onee-san archetype.

Rules for a good tease:
- **The teaser reads the other person's panic** and comments on *that*, not on their own body.
- **The tease has a limit the teaser respects.** When the other person is genuinely uncomfortable (not just flustered), the teaser backs off, and noticing that line is a character beat.
- **The tease hides something.** The teaser is often lonely, testing, or more interested than they let on. Pay that off.

### 4.5 The retaliation

The smack, the thrown pillow, the bucket. It is a genre convention, and it:
- **Must be proportionate and cartoonish.** It leaves no injury and no fear.
- **Must not be the only reaction the heroine has.** She should also be embarrassed, annoyed, or secretly amused, with an inner life.
- **Should evolve over an arc.** By the midpoint the heroine sighs instead of hitting, and that change is intimacy. See [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md) on how tsundere violence should soften.

### 4.6 The object as stand-in

Let objects carry the suggestion so bodies don't have to: the towel that is *definitely* going to fall (it doesn't), the swimsuit top flying past in the wind (it belongs to a stranger), the shirt borrowed after the rain that is too big. For high-school casts, objects are the *whole* joke.

## 5. The classic episodes

Each has a standard shape. The age column says what is allowed.

| Episode | Standard shape | High-school cast | Adult cast |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beach / pool** | Sunscreen politics, swimsuit reveal anticipation, watermelon split, someone loses something to the waves | Logistics comedy only: sunburn, lost sandals, a shark-shaped float, the lead refusing to swim. **No swimsuit description.** | One flirt beat allowed (a sunscreen request played for the lead's panic), then comedy |
| **Hot spring (onsen)** | Gendered baths, a thin wall, overheard conversations about the lead, a wrong-door accident | **Overheard-conversation comedy** is the gold standard: the lead hears the girls talk about him through the wall. Wrong-door accidents are instant and cut. | Wrong-door accident may hold a line of panic before the cut. The overheard conversation can be franker. |
| **Lucky accident** | Trip and fall into a compromising position | Instant, interrupted, and played for panic and misunderstanding. No body detail. | One line of panic from inside the moment, then the fade-out |
| **Sleepover / cohabitation** | Shared space, the bathroom schedule, seeing someone in pajamas | Domestic comedy: bed-hair, the snoring reveal, the fight over the TV | Flirtatious domestic tension, with a cutaway at the bedroom door |
| **Rain-soaked** | Caught in rain, a borrowed shirt | A too-big shirt is comic (sleeves past the hands), towels thrown, sneezing | May carry mild suggestion through the lead's averted eyes |
| **Changing-room mix-up** | Walks in at the wrong time | Door opens, a scream, the door closes, the aftermath. The camera never enters. | Same, with at most one panicked line before the cut |
| **The teasing senior** | An older character flusters the lead | High-school senpai teasing stays verbal and non-sexual ("Kuze-kun, you're red~") | Onee-san teasing may be flirtatious, and the lead's panic is the joke |
| **Massage / care scene** | One tends to the other's injury | Played as misunderstanding from outside the room | Mild tension allowed, fade-out before anything more |

## 6. Tropes: done well vs. failing

| Trope | Done well | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Lucky-pervert lead** | His accidents are genuine, his panic is sincere, and he apologizes properly | He enjoys it, or it's deliberate. That makes him a villain, not a lead. |
| **The smack** | Cartoon timing, evolves over the arc | Every scene, escalating force, reads as abuse |
| **The shameless tease** | She has a hidden vulnerability that pays off | She's a vending machine for innuendo |
| **Towel drop** | Threatened, never delivered. The *threat* is the joke. | Delivered and described |
| **The overheard bath talk** | The girls say something sincere about the lead that he wasn't meant to hear | It becomes a body-comparison scene |
| **Nosebleed** | One classic nosebleed gag in a long series, as a parody | Recurring, which reads as dated |
| **"Pervert!"** | Said once, loudly, at the peak of a misunderstanding, and becomes a nickname callback | Screamed every chapter |
| **Clumsy heroine** | Her clumsiness is characterful (she trips over her own ambition) | Her clumsiness exists only to generate positions |

## 7. Scene recipes

### 7.1 The onsen wall (high-school safe, 1,200-1,800 words)

1. Class trip to a ryokan. Boys' and girls' baths share a bamboo partition.
2. The boys talk nonsense (Gon's legends, Enomoto's bad jokes). This is the comic warm-up.
3. Through the wall, the girls' voices. The boys go silent to listen, which is itself funny, and the narrator feels shame about listening and listens anyway.
4. The girls talk about a boy. The narrator's analysis runs wild as he works out who.
5. **Turn:** it's him. Someone says something sincere and unflattering, or sincere and kind.
6. Someone on the boys' side sneezes or drops a bucket. Silence on both sides.
7. **Punchline:** "...Who's there?" / Gon, in a falsetto: "Nobody, verily."
8. **Tilt:** at dinner, the girl who spoke won't meet his eyes.

No bodies described at any point. The humor is entirely in voices, silence, and the wall.

### 7.2 The wrong door (any age, 600-1,000 words)

1. Setup: a strange house (a friend's, a ryokan, a share-house), with doors that look alike.
2. The narrator notes the danger explicitly, with genre awareness.
3. He takes every precaution. He knocks. No answer (hair dryer running).
4. Door opens. **Freeze line.**
5. **Cut.**
6. Aftermath: the evidence (seiza in the corridor, a handprint, a sign now taped to the door in large letters).
7. Dialogue through the door: an apology, a retort, a grudging reconciliation.
8. Tilt: a callback line the next morning.

### 7.3 The onee-san tease (adults only, 1,000-1,500 words)

1. The lead (a working adult or university student) is new: a new job, a new apartment.
2. The older neighbor or coworker notices him, and she decides he's fun to fluster.
3. The tease escalates through dialogue: double meanings, a lean-in, a comment on his ears going red.
4. The lead's panic monologue runs in counterpoint.
5. **Turn:** he says something accidentally sincere, and *she* is flustered for once.
6. She retreats with a joke, and her ears are red too.
7. Tilt: a small kindness later that shows the tease was cover.

### 7.4 The misunderstanding from the doorway (high-school safe)

1. Innocent task requires closeness (an eyelash in the eye, a stuck zipper on a *bag*, reaching a high shelf).
2. The narrator's inner commentary is only on the task.
3. A third party appears at the door.
4. The frame shifts to their view, one line of how it looks.
5. Group chatter spreads the wrong version.
6. The truth is mundane, and nobody believes it.

## 8. The adult default cast (for suggestive-comedy examples)

When a scene needs flirtatious or suggestive beats, use adults. A ready-made set for this library:

- **Souta Kirishima** (24, POV): new hire at a small game-localization company, overanalyzes everything, terse, a black-coffee drinker. He is the same narrator engine as Kuze, grown up.
- **Chiaki Sawamura** (29): his team lead, the onee-san type. Lazy smile, long wavy chestnut hair usually clipped up with a pencil, and devastating at spreadsheets. She teases because she's bored, and because she's lonely in ways she hides well.
- **Mei Tsukishiro** (23): Souta's university kouhai-turned-coworker, a tsundere who insists she only joined the company for the dental plan.
- **Airi Kanzaki** (27): the upstairs neighbor in Souta's apartment building, a night-shift nurse, blunt and cheerful, who treats him like a stray cat.

Everyone is a working adult, and the text says so.

## 9. Writing restraint well

Restraint is the genre's craft, not a limitation on it.

- **The implication rule:** anything a reader can infer is funnier unwritten. A cut to the aftermath is a joke. A description is a pause in the joke.
- **One line, then cut.** With adults, allow at most one suggestive line from the narrator's panicked perspective per beat, and it should be about his *reaction* ("He had never been more aware of the concept of shoulders"), not an inventory.
- **Faces over bodies.** Describe expressions (a flush reaching the ears, eyes wide, a mouth opening and closing), not anatomy.
- **Never linger.** Fanservice beats are short. If a beat is longer than its punchline, it's in the wrong genre.
- **Avoid the catalogue.** No lists of body parts, no clothing inventories, no "the camera pans" writing.
- **Keep dignity.** Every character in an ecchi scene should come out of it with their dignity intact in the reader's eyes, even while mortified in-story.

### 10.2 Sample: the onee-san tease (adult cast)

> Chiaki Sawamura did not so much sit on the corner of Souta's desk as occupy it, the way a cat occupies a keyboard: completely, and as if it had always been hers.
> "Kirishima-kun." She had a pencil in her hair and another in her mouth, and she took the second one out to point it at him. "You stayed till eleven again."
> "The build was broken."
> "The build is always broken. That's what builds are for." She leaned in to look at his screen, and her sleeve brushed his shoulder, and Souta's brain, which had been handling a merge conflict with total competence, dropped every thread at once.
> Analysis: she was close.
> Further analysis: she knew she was close.
> Conclusion: he was going to fix nothing for the next four minutes.
> "Your ears," Chiaki said pleasantly, "are doing the thing."
> "They're not doing anything."
> "They're very red, for ears that aren't doing anything."
> "It's warm in here."
> "It's nineteen degrees. I set it myself." She rested her chin on her hand and smiled, slow, the smile of someone who had found the one fun toy in a boring office. "Relax. I'm only teasing. You're just so easy, Kirishima-kun. It's like pressing an elevator button and watching all the lights come on."
> "I'm glad I'm a source of entertainment."
> "You are. Truly. The only one on this floor." She said it lightly, and then, for half a second, it wasn't light at all. Her eyes went past him, to the dark window and the empty desks, and came back.
> Souta, who had spent his whole life reading the pauses in other people's sentences, read that one and didn't know what to do with it.
> "...You stayed till eleven too," he said. "Your light was on."
> Chiaki blinked.
> "I saw it from the station," he added, and then realized how that sounded, and wanted to die.
> "..."
> "Not that I was looking. The office is on the way. The light is very visible. From the platform. As a light."
> For the first time since he'd joined the company, Chiaki Sawamura had nothing to say. She stood up, straightened her blazer, and walked back to her desk without a single tease.
> Her ears, Souta noted with a scientist's detachment and a condemned man's joy, were doing the thing.

## 11. Ecchi and fanservice revision checklist

1. **Age check (mandatory):** list every character in each fanservice beat and their stated age. Any suggestive beat with anyone under 18, or anyone written as childlike, must be rewritten as non-sexual comedy or cut.
2. **Body check:** search the text for body-part and clothing words. For high-school characters, remove any sexualized description entirely. For adults, keep at most one suggestive line per beat, filtered through the narrator's panic.
3. **Explicitness check:** does every beat fade out before anything explicit? It must.
4. **Consent check:** is every compromising moment an *accident*, with a sincere apology or mutual embarrassment? No deliberate violation played for laughs.
5. **Is it funny?** Every fanservice beat must have a punchline. If you removed the suggestive element, would the scene still be a scene? It should be.
6. **Panic monologue present:** is the narrator's crisis reasoning the center of the beat?
7. **Faces over bodies:** are reactions carried by expressions and objects?
8. **Retaliation proportionate** and evolving over the arc?
9. **Dignity:** does every character leave the scene with the reader's respect intact?
10. **One sincere beat:** does the episode also move a relationship forward (an overheard kindness, a moment of vulnerability)?
11. Run [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md): no "couldn't help but stare", no "her curves", no "the camera lingered".
