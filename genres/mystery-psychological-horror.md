# Mystery, Psychological & Horror

> **Purpose:** Teach the writer how to bend the LN voice toward suspicion, manipulation, dread, and fear: school mysteries, mastermind plots, unreliable narration, and horror, with the house style intact.
> **Load when:** The story involves a puzzle to solve (who leaked the photos, who is sending the letters), a manipulator pulling strings, a narrator whose read on events may be wrong, or anything meant to unsettle or frighten.
> **Related:** [Genre Index](genre-index.md), [School Drama & Slice of Life](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md), [Action & Battle](action-and-battle.md), [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md), [Narration & POV](../core/narration-and-pov.md), [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md), [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md), [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md), [Social Media & Texts](../setting/social-media-and-texts.md), [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md), [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md), [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md), [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md)

## 1. Why this voice is already half a mystery

The base LN voice is built on **suspicion**. The narrator notices small asymmetries, quotes words back, builds chains of hypotheses, and distrusts his own reading (see [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md)). That is a detective's toolkit wearing a school uniform. The source material shows it plainly: an ordinary class-duty afternoon becomes an investigation into *why* a classmate offered drinks to one person and not another.

This genre file turns that instinct from **texture into plot**:
- In school drama, the suspicion colors ordinary events.
- In mystery, the suspicion has an *answer*, and the reader is racing the narrator to it.
- In psychological fiction, the suspicion is *weaponized*: someone knows the narrator overthinks and feeds him the wrong clues.
- In horror, the suspicion has no safe answer. The more he notices, the worse it gets.

## 2. What readers want, by subgenre

| Subgenre | Reader wants | Voice shift |
|---|---|---|
| **School mystery** (everyday or light) | A fair puzzle, a clever deduction, a human motive at the bottom | Analytical monologue at full power, comedy intact, and lower stakes |
| **Mastermind / manipulation** | To sense the strings before the narrator does, and a reveal that recolors every earlier scene | Short omniscient cut-aways to the manipulator. Dramatic irony |
| **Unreliable narration** | To realize the narrator's certainty was the trap | The same voice, with confidence that is subtly wrong |
| **Psychological drama** | To watch a mind crack under social pressure | Longer spirals, repetition, broken rhythm at peaks |
| **Horror** (supernatural or human) | Dread, then fear, then the image they can't unsee | Slower, quieter, fewer jokes; sensory detail goes up |

## 3. The fair-play school mystery

### 3.1 The mystery engine
A school mystery needs five things:
1. **A disturbance**: stolen photos posted to Chirp, a love letter in the wrong shoebox, a broken club trophy, a desk vandalized overnight.
2. **A closed community**: Class 2-3, the Literature and Broadcast Club, the student council. Everyone is a suspect because everyone *knows* everyone.
3. **Clues grounded in school systems.** Who had class duty that day, who has the key to the broadcast room, who was at cleaning duty in the east wing, whose attendance number puts them next to whom. See [School Life](../setting/school-life.md). This is where the LN setting pays off: the rituals of Japanese school life produce alibis and opportunities for free.
4. **Social motive**: jealousy, humiliation, love, hierarchy. The murders are rare; the *motives* are universal.
5. **A deduction scene** where the narrator lays it out, usually to one person, rarely to a crowd.

### 3.2 Clue planting in the house voice
The analytical monologue does the planting naturally. The trick is to **plant clues inside the social analysis**, so the reader files them as character observations, not as evidence.

> Shirasagi posted the photo of her parfait at 4:12. Kuze knew because he'd been looking at it at 4:13, in the family restaurant, wondering why anyone needed forty-seven filters on a strawberry.
>
> He didn't think about the timestamp again. Not for three days.

The first paragraph reads as a joke about a character. The timestamp is the clue. The second paragraph is a **tell-forward**, a hint that it matters, which is allowed once or twice per mystery for suspense (see [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md)).

### 3.3 Fair play rules
- Every clue the narrator uses in the deduction must have appeared on the page earlier.
- The narrator may hold back his *conclusion*, but not the *evidence*.
- One red herring per suspect, and each herring must also reveal something true about character.
- The solution must be something the reader could have reached, and probably did not because the voice pointed their attention elsewhere.

### 3.4 The deduction scene
Written as a conversation, not a lecture. The house voice's snap-back banter (see [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md)) keeps it alive. The suspect pushes back, and the narrator counters each objection with one piece of evidence.

> "It couldn't have been me. I was at cleaning duty. Ask anyone."
>
> "East-wing stairs. I know."
>
> "Then--"
>
> "The east-wing stairs go right past the broadcast room."
> Kuze held up his phone: the photo of the parfait, the timestamp.
> "And you posted this while you were supposedly scrubbing them. With both hands."
>
> "..."
>
> "Unless you've got a third one."

Note the rhythm: short objection, flat correction, the evidence shown, `"..."`, and a deadpan line to close. Keep deductions to 400-900 words.

## 4. The mastermind thread

The source material's most effective long-game device is a character who **appears helpful** and is quietly moving the pieces: a short cut-away of her alone, drawing names and arrows on paper, smiling. In the default cast this is **Sayo Kujou**.

### 4.1 Rules for a mastermind
1. **Seed with cut-aways.** Every 5-10 chapters, a 100-300 word omniscient section shows the mastermind alone doing something *slightly wrong*: a diagram, a deleted message, a smile at the wrong moment. Never explain it.
2. **Give her real kindness too.** The best manipulators help people. Kujou really does stop the bullying, really does call Kuze a friend. The reader should be unable to decide which of her acts were sincere.
3. **Her influence works through systems**: the class vote, the script she "helped" write, the rumor she let slip at the right lunch table. See [Social Media & Texts](../setting/social-media-and-texts.md) for posts as instruments.
4. **The narrator half-notices.** Kuze's analytical brain *almost* gets there, then talks himself out of it because he wants to trust her. That self-talk is dramatic irony gold:
   > Why had she scolded him for the same thing she'd done? It was odd. Too odd.
   > ...No. She'd called him a friend. Doubting her would be shameful.
5. **The reveal recolors, it does not replace.** When the truth comes out, earlier scenes should gain a second meaning, and her sincere moments should remain sincere.

### 4.2 Sample cut-away (mastermind seed)

> Kujou's room was very quiet. It usually was.
>
> On the desk, a sheet of drawing paper held nine names in neat, deliberate handwriting. Amamiya's sat in the center. Arrows ran toward it from four directions, each labeled in a small, careful hand: *obvious*, *obvious*, *hiding it*, *lying to herself*.
>
> One name sat in the lower corner, apart from the others. No arrows. Not yet.
>
> *Kuze Minato.*
>
> She tapped the pen against it twice, thoughtfully, the way someone might tap a chess piece they haven't decided to move.
>
> Then she drew a single, very thin line from it to Tachibana's name, and did not label it.
>
> "Hmm."
> She said it to nobody, and smiled.

That is 140 words. It raises four questions and answers none, and it ends on a smile. See [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md) for scheduling these seeds across arcs.

## 5. Unreliable narration

Close-third LN narration is **naturally unreliable** because it's filtered through a biased mind. The source narrator is certain the popular classmate is a hypocrite, and he is sometimes right and sometimes simply jealous. Use that deliberately.

### 5.1 Types of unreliability in this voice
| Type | How it shows | Payoff |
|---|---|---|
| **Biased reading** | The narrator reads malice into every Amamiya gesture | A later scene shows Amamiya's side and reveals innocent motives for some of it, but not all |
| **Motivated blindness** | He can't see that Tachibana likes *him*, because his theory says everyone likes Amamiya | The reader sees it chapters earlier. Dramatic irony plus romance |
| **Self-deception** | He calls his choices "rational" when they're fear | The confidant's speech tears the label off (see [Emotional Climaxes](../scenes/emotional-climaxes.md)) |
| **Missing information** | He was absent the day it happened | The truth arrives via someone else's POV |
| **Manipulated reading** | The mastermind feeds him clues | The reveal |

### 5.2 Technique
- **Let the narrator be confident in the prose.** Don't hedge with "perhaps he was wrong." The certainty is the trap.
- **Leave fingerprints of the truth** in other characters' dialogue that the narrator dismisses:
  > "Kuze, you really don't notice anything, do you."
  > Tachibana hit his shoulder much harder than necessary.
  > He decided she was talking about Amamiya.
- **Deploy a POV cut-away** that shows the same event from another angle. It's the cleanest way to reveal unreliability without breaking the anchor's voice. See [Narration & POV](../core/narration-and-pov.md).
- **Never cheat the narrator's senses.** He can misread what he sees, but the prose shouldn't lie about what he sees.

## 6. Psychological drama: the mind under pressure

When the narrator is cornered (by bullying, guilt, or a manipulator), the rhythm becomes the instrument.

### 6.1 The spiral
Chains of questions accelerate, repeat, and loop:
> What did she say to Hoshino? Why did she say it wasn't a problem? Wasn't it supposed to be fine? Didn't she say it was fine?
> It's fine. She said it's fine. So it's fine.
> It's not my problem.
> It's not my problem.

### 6.2 The mantra
A phrase the narrator repeats to hold himself together ("It's not my problem", "Rationally", "Endure") that gets **shorter and more frequent** as pressure rises, until it breaks. In the source material, a "rationally, rationally, rationally" run precedes the protagonist's snap. Build the mantra over several chapters so its collapse is felt.

### 6.3 Intercutting
At peak pressure, alternate one line of outside noise (a chant, a sneer) with one line of inner response. The outside gets louder while the inside gets smaller:
> """Say it, say it, say it~"""
> She has nothing but her looks.
> """Say it, say it, say it~"""
> Nobody would miss her.
> """Say it, say it, say it~"""
> "..."

### 6.4 The dissociation beat
After a psychological peak, write one or two paragraphs of blankness: flat sensory detail, no analysis. It signals shock more powerfully than description of emotion:
> He took the usual train. He sat in the usual seat. The ad above the door was for a dental clinic and showed a cartoon tooth giving a thumbs-up.
> He looked at it for eleven stations.

## 7. Horror in the LN voice

Horror LNs range from school ghost stories (the seven wonders, the cursed club room) to human horror (stalkers, yandere obsession) to cosmic dread. The house voice needs three adjustments.

### 7.1 Slow the rhythm down
Horror inverts action. Instead of short hits, use **longer, quieter sentences with a short sting at the end.** Let the reader settle into a paragraph, then pull the floor out.

> The fourth-floor hallway was the kind of empty that only exists in schools after dark, where every door is the same door and the fire-exit sign paints the floor green and the building makes small noises that you decide, one by one, are the heating.
>
> Kuze decided the footsteps were the heating.
>
> The heating stopped when he did.

### 7.2 Keep the analytical voice, and let it fail
The narrator tries to rationalize, and the rationalizations fail one by one. This is the specific pleasure of a cynic in a horror story.
> Pipes, then. Old buildings had pipes. Pipes expanded when the temperature changed.
> Pipes did not breathe.

### 7.3 Detail density goes up
Horror is the one genre where you slow down on scenery. Choose **wrong details**: a single shoe in the shoe locker, a desk that faces the back of the room, a class photo with thirty-one faces for a class of thirty. One wrong detail per scene is more effective than a paragraph of gothic atmosphere.

### 7.4 Comedy as a pressure valve
Keep one dry line per scene, *before* the scare. Laughter lowers the guard, and the scare lands harder. Gon's archaic speech is perfect for this:
> "Verily, there is nothing in the music room but pianos and regret."
> The piano played one note.
> "...And that. There is also that."

### 7.5 Human horror
Stalkers, obsessive admirers, the class that turns on someone: these are closer to the base genre and often scarier. Write them with **mundane detail**. A Chirp post showing a photo of the narrator's own house from across the street. A bento left on his desk with his name written on it in a handwriting he doesn't recognize. The yandere archetype lives here; see [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md) for writing obsession with psychological truth rather than as a gimmick.

## 8. Tropes: execution and failure

| Trope | Execute well | How it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **The seven school wonders** | Each wonder has a mundane explanation except one, and the one is never explained | All supernatural (no tension) or all explained (no dread) |
| **The anonymous account** | The account's word choices are a clue to identity | It is a hacker, who could be anyone |
| **The locked club room** | The school key system (who signs for keys at the staff room) is the solution | A secret passage |
| **The friend who's too helpful** | Real help, with one small lie | Obviously evil from chapter 1 |
| **Missing student** | Classmates' reactions reveal the social hierarchy | A plot device with no personality |
| **The video/photo that shouldn't exist** | The camera angle tells you where the photographer stood | Nobody asks who took it |
| **The confession letter mystery** | Handwriting, stationery, the shoebox system | Solved by asking |
| **The narrator was the culprit** | Seeded by gaps in his accounting of time | A twist with no seeds (cheating) |
| **Cursed object** | Rules that the characters discover by breaking them | Rules explained by a convenient book |
| **The teacher knows more** | Ogata-sensei's odd "Later." or a too-quick answer | A teacher who is just exposition |

## 9. Scene recipes

### 9.1 The morning the desks were written on
**Beats:** the narrator enters the classroom and senses the air is different; the defaced desk(s); classmates' reactions as stacked whispers; the narrator's hypothesis chain; one clue noticed and filed (the handwriting, the paper type); an ally or enemy acts; a small hook.
**Tone:** base-genre dread. No supernatural.

### 9.2 The night-school investigation
**Beats:** a reason to be at school after dark (a forgotten phone, an errand for the teacher); a companion (Gon for comedy, Tachibana for tension); the building described with wrong details; a false scare with a comic payoff; a real one; retreat.

### 9.3 The chart on the wall
**Beats:** the narrator lays out suspects (in his notebook, or in his head as a list); each suspect gets one line of motive and one line of alibi; a missing piece nags; a random daily-life detail supplies it (the parfait timestamp); the sediment shifts.

### 9.4 The confrontation in the empty classroom
**Beats:** evening light; the suspect agrees to meet; small talk as fencing; the evidence laid out in banter; the suspect's mask slips; a confession *with a human reason*; the narrator's verdict line, which is often more sympathetic than expected.

### 9.5 The mastermind's cut-away
**Beats:** alone, doing something small and wrong; one line of dialogue to nobody; a smile. 100-300 words.

## 10. Chapter and arc shapes

- **Mystery chapter:** open with the disturbance or its aftermath. Plant 1-2 clues per chapter inside character beats. End each chapter on a new question, not an answer.
- **Mystery arc (6-12 chapters):** disturbance, suspects introduced via daily life, the first theory (wrong), escalation (a second incident), the narrator's personal stake rises, a key clue from an unlikely source, the deduction, and the aftermath with a human cost. Leave one thread open for the series mastermind (see [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md)).
- **Horror arc:** the rumor, a skeptical first visit, the first wrong detail, a friend affected, rules discovered, the confrontation or escape, and an ambiguous ending. End with *one* thing unexplained.

## 11. Naming and setting conventions

- School legends: "the seven wonders of Seiryou", "the thirteenth step on the west stairs", "the music room at 4:44".
- Anonymous accounts: bland handles (`seiryou_truth`, `2-3_observer`) whose style gives them away.
- Places: the old building (kyuukousha), the unused fourth-floor classroom, the incinerator behind the gym, the club building after lock-up.
- Time: evenings after club activities, the hour when the broadcasting club plays the closing music, rainy days during tsuyu (rainy season).

## 12. Sample passage: The fair-play clue

> The broadcast room key hung on the third hook from the left in the staff room, under a laminated sign that said PLEASE SIGN OUT KEYS written in Ogata-sensei's very round handwriting.
>
> Nobody signed out keys. Everybody knew that. The sign-out sheet on the clipboard beneath the hook was mostly doodles, a crossword somebody had given up on, and one entry from last April in which a student had signed their name as *Batman*.
>
> Kuze wasn't here for the key, anyway. He was returning the class journal, which was a class-duty chore and therefore his, and he was only looking at the hooks because Ogata-sensei was on the phone and he didn't want to look like he was listening.
>
> He wasn't. Much.
>
> Third hook from the left: empty.
>
> Fourth hook: empty.
>
> That was strange, but only a little. The broadcast club had practice. The fourth hook was the equipment locker. They'd take both.
>
> Except the broadcast club didn't have practice today. Amamiya had spent all of lunch complaining about it, loudly, to four girls at once, because practice was cancelled and he had nothing to do and wasn't that *tragic*, and all four girls had agreed that it was.
>
> "Kuze-kun? The journal?"
>
> "Ah. Here."
>
> Ogata-sensei took it, gave it a glance, and wrote a big circle on the cover with a red pen. She did that to everyone's. He'd never figured out what the circle meant.
>
> "You look like you're thinking hard about something."
>
> "About the circle."
>
> "Teehee. It's a secret."
>
> He bowed and left. In the hallway, he took out his phone and opened Chirp and scrolled through the broadcast club members' posts from the past hour.
>
> Nothing. Nothing. Parfait. Nothing.
>
> Parfait.
>
> He looked at the timestamp for a long time.

**What it demonstrates:** clues embedded in school systems (key hooks, class duty, the journal); comic texture hiding the clue (the "Batman" line); a social memory used as evidence (Amamiya's lunchtime complaining); a teacher's tic that may be a later clue; and a repetition beat that turns a joke detail ("Parfait.") into a revelation.

## 13. Sample passage: Human horror (the gift)

> There was a bento on his desk.
>
> Kuze stopped in the doorway. It was seven-fifty; the classroom was empty except for the smell of floor wax and the sound of the baseball team running laps somewhere below. Nobody came this early. That was the whole reason he came this early.
>
> The bento was wrapped in a navy furoshiki, tied in a neat, careful knot. A folded note was tucked under the knot.
>
> He didn't touch it for a while. He just looked at it the way you look at a phone ringing with a number you don't know.
>
> Then he unfolded the note.
>
> *You skipped breakfast again. Tamagoyaki is sweet today, the way you like it.*
>
> He read it twice.
>
> He did like his tamagoyaki sweet. His mother made it that way. He had never told anyone that, because who tells anyone that? It wasn't a fact. It was barely even a preference. It was just the way things were at his house, at six-forty every morning, at a kitchen table that nobody at Seiryou had ever seen.
>
> And he had skipped breakfast today. He'd overslept by twelve minutes and run for the train.
>
> Kuze went to the window and looked down at the front gate, at the long straight road toward the station, at nothing in particular.
>
> Nobody was there.
>
> He folded the note, put it in his pocket, and put the bento in his locker without opening it.
>
> At lunch, when he went to get it, the knot on the furoshiki had been retied.
>
> Tighter.

**What it demonstrates:** horror through mundane detail; the analytical voice reasoning its way toward something worse; the wrong detail (the knot); a one-word sting as the final line.

## 14. Sample passage: The unreliable narrator's slip

> Amamiya was doing it again.
>
> Standing at Tachibana's desk after class with that easy smile, holding out a bottle of the sports drink she liked, the lemon one, the one they only sold in the vending machine by the gym. He must have walked all the way there for it. For her.
>
> Of course he had. It was the most efficient way to keep her on the hook: a small kindness, specific enough to feel personal, cheap enough to cost nothing. He'd probably bought four. One for each of them. Kuze would bet his lunch money there were three more bottles in that bag.
>
> Tachibana took the bottle, went red to her ears, and hit Amamiya in the arm.
>
> "I didn't ask for this, idiot!"
>
> "Whoa, whoa! You said you were thirsty!"
>
> "That was *second period*!"
>
> ...Textbook. Kuze went back to packing his bag.
>
> When he looked up again, Amamiya had gone, and Tachibana was standing at *his* desk. The lemon drink was in her hand, unopened.
>
> "Here."
>
> "What?"
>
> "I don't like lemon."
>
> She put it down in front of him. Then she left without another word, fast, the way she did when she'd lost a kendo point and didn't want anyone to see her face.
>
> Kuze looked at the bottle.
>
> She didn't like lemon? She'd been drinking the lemon one every day since April.
>
> ...Maybe she'd gotten sick of it.
>
> That made sense. That was the rational conclusion.
>
> He drank it on the train. It was very sweet.

**What it demonstrates:** confident misreading presented as fact ("Textbook."); the truth visible to the reader through Tachibana's behavior and her lie; the narrator's "rational conclusion" as the trap; a final sensory line that tells the reader what he won't admit.

## 15. Genre-specific revision checklist

- [ ] **Mystery:** is every clue used in the deduction on the page earlier, embedded in character or daily-life beats?
- [ ] Does each suspect have a motive rooted in social hierarchy, love, jealousy, or shame?
- [ ] Does each chapter end on a new question rather than an answer?
- [ ] Is the deduction scene a *conversation* with pushback, under 900 words?
- [ ] **Mastermind:** are cut-aways short (100-300 words), unexplained, and spaced 5-10 chapters apart?
- [ ] Does the mastermind do real good as well as harm?
- [ ] **Unreliable narration:** is the narrator confident, with no hedging, while the truth leaks through other characters' behavior?
- [ ] Do the narrator's senses report accurately even when his interpretations are wrong?
- [ ] **Psychological:** does the mantra build over several chapters before breaking?
- [ ] Does rhythm change at peaks (intercutting, fragments, then a flat dissociation beat)?
- [ ] **Horror:** are sentences longer and quieter, with a short sting at paragraph ends?
- [ ] Is there one *wrong detail* per scare scene instead of generic atmosphere?
- [ ] Is there a dry line before the scare, not after?
- [ ] Have you cut the genre clichés: "a chill ran down his spine" (once per book at most), "an eerie silence", "something was off", "little did he know", "his blood ran cold"? See [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md).
- [ ] Is at least one thing left unexplained at the arc's end, on purpose?
