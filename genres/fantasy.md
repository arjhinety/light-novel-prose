# Fantasy

> **Purpose:** Adapt the light-novel voice to high fantasy, magic academies, and adventurer-guild stories. It covers handling status screens lightly, building the world through dialogue, and explaining a magic system without info-dumps.
> **Load when:** The story is set in a secondary world (swords, spells, guilds, kingdoms, academies) and the protagonist was born there. For protagonists from Earth, load [Isekai & Reincarnation](isekai-and-reincarnation.md) as well.

## 1. What readers of LN fantasy want

LN fantasy is **not** Western epic fantasy with anime names pasted on. It is lighter, faster, and far more interested in people than in maps. Readers want:

1. **A familiar frame, instantly legible.** Adventurer guild, rank from F to S, a magic academy with a class hierarchy, a demon lord somewhere over the horizon. Nobody needs the guild explained, and the genre's shared vocabulary *is* worldbuilding shorthand. Use it without apology.
2. **One fresh twist on that frame.** The guild receptionist is the strongest person in the city. Magic is measured by debt, not mana. The academy's lowest class is where the real geniuses are dumped. The twist is the hook of the whole series.
3. **A protagonist whose mind is fun to be in.** The same analytical, wry, underreacting voice as the school genre, now applied to monsters and nobles. The joy of the genre is someone treating a dragon attack with the same deadpan he'd use for class duty.
4. **Growth you can see.** Skills learned, ranks gained, allies earned. Progress is a core pleasure, so make it concrete.
5. **Found family.** A party is a friend group with swords. The emotional core is the same as a school story: belonging.

Readers do **not** want pages of history, languages with apostrophes, or politics they need a chart for. Keep it moving.

## 2. How the base voice shifts

### 2.1 POV

- **Close third on one anchor** still works best. First person is also common in fantasy LNs (especially isekai) and is acceptable if the story bible sets it. Pick one and never mix them within a book.
- The **analytical monologue** now reads *power* as well as social cues. The protagonist notices that the knight's gauntlet has a repaired rivet on the left hand (injured, favors the right), or that the noble girl chose a fire spell when ice would have been cheaper (showing off, so for whom?). See [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md).
- **Cut-aways** are more common than in school drama. Use them for the villain's council, the princess in her tower, and the guild master reading a report. Keep them short and end each on a threat or tease.

### 2.2 Rhythm

- **Travel and daily life:** Base rhythm, about half dialogue.
- **Combat:** Tighter. Short beats, one-line paragraphs, and sound effects. See [Action & Battle](action-and-battle.md).
- **Awe moments** (first sight of the capital, a dragon's wing blotting out the sun) earn the **long exhale**: one sweeping, clause-rich sentence, followed immediately by a short deflating line from the POV. The contrast keeps awe from turning purple.

> The capital spread across the valley floor like a spilled jewelry box, white towers and blue roofs and a cathedral spire so tall that clouds snagged on it and trailed away like torn silk, while beneath it all the river flashed gold in the afternoon light.
> "Huh. Rent must be terrible."

### 2.3 Comedy ratio

30-50%. Fantasy LNs are funnier than their Western cousins. Comedy comes from:
- The POV's underreaction to the fantastical
- Party banter (a boke/tsukkomi pair is almost mandatory; see [Rom-Com & Comedy](romcom-and-comedy.md))
- Bureaucracy (guild paperwork, academy forms, the tax on monster corpses)
- Mismatched expectations (the "legendary sword" is a butter knife)

### 2.4 Description density

Higher than school drama, but still **people-first**. Describe a new city in two to four sentences, with one landmark and one sensory detail. Describe a new character with the full portrait formula (see [Description & Portraits](../core/description-and-portraits.md)) plus **one piece of gear** that tells us their story (a scabbard too ornate for the plain sword in it).

## 3. Worldbuilding without info-dumps

This is the most important skill in the genre and the most common failure of AI-written fantasy.

### 3.1 The three-sentence rule

Never explain more than **three consecutive sentences** of world information in narration. After three, a character must speak, act, or react. Exposition becomes story when someone has an opinion about it.

**Don't:**
> The Kingdom of Velstra was founded three hundred years ago by the Hero-King Aldric, who defeated the Demon Lord with the aid of the seven Elemental Spirits. Since then, magic has been divided into seven schools corresponding to each Spirit. Nobles are generally born with affinity for two or more schools, while commoners typically have one or none. The Royal Academy accepts only those with two or more affinities, except for one scholarship seat per year.

**Do:**
> The examiner didn't look up.
> "Two affinities minimum. Next."
> "I have one."
> Now he looked up. His eyes did the thing Noel had seen in every noble's eyes since he'd walked through the gate. A quick, pitying tally.
> "Then you're in the wrong line, boy."
> Noel held up the letter.
> "Scholarship line. It says one."
> The examiner took the letter as if it might bite. Behind Noel, somebody snickered. Somebody else muttered *scholarship* the way you'd say *head lice*.
> One seat a year. Three hundred years of Hero-King Aldric's glorious academy, and they'd saved exactly one chair for everyone else.
> Noel wondered who'd been sitting in it last year, and where they were now.

The world information is the same (affinities, class divide, scholarship seat, and the founding legend), but it arrives through **conflict, an opinion, and a question.**

### 3.2 The worldbuilding carriers

| Carrier | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Argument** | Two characters disagree about a world fact | "Water magic is for healers." / "Water magic drowns people just fine." |
| **Price** | Mention what something costs | "A healing potion is two silver. My rent is three." |
| **Rule broken** | Show a rule by breaking it | The commoner sits in the noble row; the room goes silent. |
| **Slang** | Coined words that imply history | "Don't be such a tower-born." |
| **Object** | An item with a story | A coin with the old king's face scratched out |
| **Joke** | Humor assumes shared knowledge | "Even a slime could pass that test." "A slime *did* pass. He's in Class D." |
| **Paperwork** | Forms, contracts, and guild boards | A quest notice with "NO WYVERNS, WE MEAN IT" underlined twice |

### 3.3 The "known to everyone" test

Before explaining anything, ask: **would the POV character think about this right now?** A person born in this world doesn't mentally explain the guild system while walking into a guild, any more than you explain elevators while riding one. Explain only:
- What is **new to the POV** (they learn it, and we learn it with them)
- What the POV **has an opinion about** (it's in the monologue anyway)
- What is **about to matter** (set up a Chekhov's rule a scene before it fires)

### 3.4 Names

- Keep a consistent **naming palette** per culture: Velstra uses Latinate or Germanic names (Seraphina Altmark, Noel Farrand), the eastern islands use Japanese-flavored names (Kaede Tsukishiro), and the desert uses Arabic-flavored ones. Readers use names to sort factions.
- Avoid apostrophes and unpronounceable clusters. LN fantasy names are **easy to say**.
- Give nobles long names and let the POV shorten them rudely ("Lady Seraphina Eleonore von Altmark, the Frost Rose of the North. Or, as Noel had been calling her in his head, the Problem.").
- Honorific systems can be localized (Lady, Sir, Master, Dame) or kept Japanese-flavored in a Japanese-coded culture. Pick one per culture and log it. See [Honorifics & Address](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md).

## 4. Magic systems without lectures

### 4.1 Show the magic by its limits

Readers understand a magic system by what it **can't** do. Introduce each rule at the moment it constrains someone.

> Seraphina's ice lance shattered against the golem's chest.
> She'd put everything into it. Noel could tell by the way her knees buckled, the way she didn't bother to hide it. Nobles didn't show the cost of a spell in front of commoners, ever. So either she'd forgotten he was there, or she was past caring.
> Mana debt. That was the part the textbooks put in small print. Borrow too much from your own body, and your body collects.

### 4.2 The one-page rule

Your story bible should describe the whole magic system in **one page or less**, as a list of rules and costs. If you can't, it's too complicated for this genre. The "one fresh twist" should be statable in one sentence: "Magic is paid for in memories." "Every spell needs a song." "Commoners can only cast by borrowing from nobles."

### 4.3 Chant and incantation

LN fantasy loves named spells and chants. Rules:
- **Spell names** go in the character's language. Use English (Frost Lance) or a Japanese-flavored rendering with an English gloss the first time only.
- **Chants** are short, 1-3 lines, and set in italics as their own paragraph. Never write more than one full chant per chapter. After that, just "she began the chant."
- The POV can find chants embarrassing. That's a great comic beat, especially for a cynical narrator.

> "*O frost that sleeps beneath the northern stars, answer the blood of Altmark--*"
> Noel, who cast his one spell by snapping his fingers, tried very hard to keep his face neutral.

### 4.4 Status screens (in born-in-world fantasy)

If the world has status screens (plates, cards, appraisal crystals), handle them **lightly**:
- Show a screen in full **once**, early, and keep it short (under 10 lines). The full formatting guide is in [Isekai & Reincarnation §5](isekai-and-reincarnation.md#5-status-windows-and-skill-notifications).
- After that, reference only **changes** ("His Swordsmanship ticked up to Lv. 4. That was all. Three weeks of blisters for one number.")
- Let characters have opinions about their stats. In-world, a status card is a social document, like a report card or a résumé. People hide theirs, lie about them, and are judged by them. That is far more interesting than the numbers.

## 5. Genre tropes: executing well vs. failing

### 5.1 The adventurer guild

**Well:** The guild is a workplace with its own social map (see the classroom map in [School Drama §3](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md#3-the-social-map-hierarchy-as-setting)). It has regulars, a receptionist who knows everyone's secrets, a veteran drunk who's secretly A-rank, and a notice board full of jobs with personality.
**Fails:** The guild is a vending machine for quests.

### 5.2 The rank system (F to S)

**Well:** Ranks are social class. A D-rank talking back to a B-rank is a scene. Ranking up is a public event with witnesses and jealousy.
**Fails:** Ranks inflate until S+++ means nothing.

### 5.3 The magic academy

**Well:** It is a Japanese high school with spells. Everything in [School Life](../setting/school-life.md) transfers: class lists, the festival, midterms, the student council, the rooftop (a tower balcony), and the go-home club (the kid who skips mandatory dueling club). The class hierarchy is literal (Class S to Class F).
**Fails:** It's a generic castle with no daily routine.

### 5.4 The underestimated protagonist

**Well:** The protagonist really is weak in the measured way and strong in an unmeasured one (analysis, a strange single skill, stubbornness). The pleasure is watching others' assessments be wrong, one by one. Pace the reveals. One "wait, what?" per arc is plenty.
**Fails:** The protagonist is secretly overpowered from chapter one and the "underestimation" is fake suspense.

### 5.5 The noble heroine

**Well:** She is proud because pride is the only armor her family gave her. Her tsundere or kuudere behavior (see [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md)) has a political cause. Being seen with a commoner has real costs.
**Fails:** She's rude until she suddenly isn't.

### 5.6 The party

**Well:** Each member has a combat role *and* an emotional role. The tank is the big sister, the healer is the moral center, the mage is the tsukkomi, the thief is the comic relief who has the saddest backstory. Party dinners are the equivalent of lunch on the rooftop.
**Fails:** Everyone is interchangeable outside fights.

### 5.7 The demon lord / dark lord

**Well:** Distant for most of the story, felt through consequences (refugees, prices going up, the knight who never came back). When finally met, they are a *person* with a reason.
**Fails:** Cackling in cut-aways.

### 5.8 The dungeon

**Well:** A dungeon is a pressure cooker, like a school trip. It forces a small group together, cuts them off, and tests trust. The best dungeon chapters are relationship chapters.
**Fails:** Floor-by-floor monster lists.

### 5.9 The tournament

**Well:** A tournament gives a sports-arc structure (see [Sports, Idol & Club](sports-idol-and-club.md)). Bracket reveals, rival introductions, and fights that are also conversations.
**Fails:** Eight fights in a row with identical beats.

### 5.10 Slow life / cozy fantasy

**Well:** Running a café, farming, or brewing potions in the countryside. This is slice of life with a fantasy skin. Everything in [School Drama & Slice of Life §5](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md#5-making-nothing-happens-chapters-compelling) applies directly.
**Fails:** No small wants, no friction, only recipes.

## 6. Scene recipes

### 6.1 Arrival at the guild
**Beats:** The door, the noise, the smell (ale, leather, wet dog). Heads turn and assess. The receptionist's smile. A veteran's challenge. The POV's underreaction.
**POV angle:** Reading the room like a new classroom, noting who's at the center, who's alone, and who's watching the door.

### 6.2 The aptitude test
**Beats:** The crystal, the line, and the nobles going first. The protagonist's result is disappointing or impossible. The room's reaction. The examiner's second look.
**Twist option:** The crystal cracks, and the POV's first thought is "Do I have to pay for that?"

### 6.3 The first real fight
**Beats:** Overconfidence, then a mistake, then fear, then an improvised solution. Someone gets hurt. After the fight, the shaking hands.
**Tip:** The shaking hands scene afterwards matters more than the fight. See [Action & Battle](action-and-battle.md).

### 6.4 The campfire
**Beats:** Night, the party, and a crackling fire. Someone asks a real question. Backstory comes out *sideways* (a scar, an old song, a refusal to talk about home).
**This is the rooftop-lunch scene of fantasy.**

### 6.5 The market day
**Beats:** Prices, haggling, and street food. A child pickpocket. The noble heroine who has never held a copper coin.
**Use for:** Worldbuilding via prices and the class divide, plus a date-like scene without calling it a date.

### 6.6 The ball / audience with the king
**Beats:** The commoner is out of place. Dress codes, a dance he can't do, a noble who insults him, and the heroine who defends or ignores him (and the reason for her choice).
**See also:** [Villainess & Otome](villainess-and-otome.md) for ballroom politics.

### 6.7 The monster that isn't
**Beats:** A feared creature turns out to be protecting young, starving, or controlled. The party disagrees about what to do.
**Use for:** A moral argument that reveals each party member's values.

## 7. Chapter and arc shapes

### 7.1 Quest arc (6-10 chapters)
1. **Board:** The job and its hidden problem
2. **Party:** Formation or friction
3. **Road:** Travel banter, a campfire, and one sign of wrongness
4. **Arrival:** The situation is worse than the notice said
5. **First clash:** It fails
6. **Regroup:** A campfire again, a confession or conflict, and a new plan
7. **Second clash:** It succeeds at a cost
8. **Return:** The guild's reaction, the reward, and a rank change
9. **Tail:** A cut-away hinting that the job was connected to something larger

### 7.2 Academy term arc (10-15 chapters)
Mirror the school calendar: entrance, class assignment, first practical, a rival, a mid-term tournament or exam, a festival, and a crisis (monster attack, political scandal). The 9-beat drama arc from [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md) runs underneath.

### 7.3 Chapter shape
Same as the base voice: numbered micro-sections, each ending on a tilt. Fantasy chapters may run longer (3,500-5,000 words) because of action. Keep the sections at 600-1,200 words each, so an action chapter simply has more sections.

## 8. Sample passages

Original cast for this file:
- **Noel Farrand**: a commoner scholarship student at the Royal Academy of Velstra, one affinity (wind), and cynical. His inner tally of nobles is his version of Kuze's ledger.
- **Seraphina von Altmark**: an ice-affinity duke's daughter, proud and precise, and secretly exhausted by her family's expectations. A kuudere.
- **Tessa Brandt**: a knight-family girl with a greatsword too big for her, loud and warm. The genki of the party.
- **Old Marla**: the guild receptionist, rumored ex-S-rank, who knits during negotiations.

### 8.1 Guild arrival (≈480 words)

> The Adventurers' Guild of Velstra smelled like a hundred people who had all decided, independently, that baths were for nobles.
> Noel stood in the doorway and let his eyes adjust.
> Long tables. A notice board the size of a barn door, papered three layers deep. A counter at the back with a single receptionist, an old woman with a gray bun, knitting something long and purple. And in the middle of the room, loud enough to be heard from the street, a table of B-ranks with their boots up, laughing at something that had probably been a person.
> The laughter stopped when they saw his uniform.
> Academy blue. Of course. He'd come straight from class.
> The largest of them had crumbs in his beard from several different meals.
> "Oi, oi. Lost, little scholar? The library's two streets over."
> "I'm here to register."
> "*Register?*"
> The man looked at his friends, who dutifully laughed.
> "You hear that? The academy's sending us their leftovers now."
> Leftovers. Noel filed the word away. It was more accurate than the man knew. One affinity, one scholarship seat, and one allowance that stopped the day his grades dipped. He needed a second income, and the guild was the only place in the capital that paid commoners by the job and not by their surname.
> He walked to the counter. The room watched him do it.
> The old woman didn't look up from her knitting.
> "Name."
> "Noel Farrand."
> "Affinity."
> "Wind."
> "Just wind?"
> "Just wind."
> *Click, click, click,* went the needles. "What can you do with it?"
> Noel considered lying. Then he considered the old woman's hands, the calluses on the pads of her fingers that no amount of knitting would make, and the thin white scar that ran from her wrist into her sleeve.
> "I can make a door slam. From across a room."
> Behind him, the B-rank table burst out laughing.
> The old woman's needles paused.
> For the first time, she looked up at him. Her eyes were a faded blue and very, very awake.
> "Can you, now. Show me."
> Noel snapped his fingers.
> Across the guild, the front door, which had been standing open to the afternoon, slammed shut with a sound like a thunderclap. Every head turned. A mug fell off a table. The bearded B-rank, who'd been leaning his chair back on two legs, discovered that he was no longer leaning on anything.
> *Crash.*
> "..."
> The old woman went back to her knitting.
> "F-rank. Welcome to the guild, Farrand. Mind the step on your way out. Some people fall over it."

**What to notice:** A smell-based entrance joke. The room map (center table, lone receptionist). Worldbuilding carried by an insult ("leftovers") that the POV analyzes. A price-and-class detail (allowance tied to grades). The underestimation reversal, paced with one reveal. The receptionist's power is shown through her hands, not stated. Deadpan closing line.

### 8.2 The noble at the market (≈430 words)

> Seraphina von Altmark stopped dead.
> "What is *that*."
> "A skewer."
> "Of what?"
> Noel squinted at the stall. The vendor, a round man with a burn scar across one forearm, grinned at them with three teeth.
> "Best not to ask, miss! Two coppers!"
> Seraphina looked at the skewer. She looked at the vendor. She looked at Noel, with the face of a duchess whose carriage had just been splashed by a peasant, which was, he supposed, exactly what she was.
> "I don't have coppers."
> "You have a coin purse."
> "I have *silver*."
> "Then you have about ninety coppers."
> "..."
> She held out a silver coin between two gloved fingers, as if it were a dead insect. The vendor's eyes went wide. The whole street seemed to lean in a little.
> Noel took the coin from her fingers before anyone else could.
> "Hey--"
> "You flash silver on Tanner's Row, somebody follows you home."
> He dropped two coppers of his own into the vendor's palm.
> "Two skewers."
> "Ohh, a gentleman!"
> "He's not."
> "I'm not."
> They walked on with a skewer each. Seraphina held hers the way she held her wand in practical class, at a precise forty-five-degree angle and well away from her body. She did not eat it. She examined it.
> Why had she come? That was what Noel couldn't work out. The Frost Rose of the North did not go to Tanner's Row. She had servants. She had servants whose servants had servants. And yet she'd appeared at the academy gate after class, alone and in a plain cloak that must have cost more than his whole wardrobe, and said, in a voice that brooked no argument: *You will show me where you buy your lunch.*
> Research? A dare? A bet with the other nobles, in which he was the punchline?
> Or was it something smaller and sadder than any of those? A girl who'd never once in her life been allowed to eat something without knowing exactly what it was.
> Seraphina bit the skewer.
> Chewed.
> Her face did not change at all, which on Seraphina meant a great deal.
> "...Two coppers?"
> "Two coppers."
> She took another bite. Then another. Grease shone on her glove, and she didn't seem to notice, which Noel thought might be the first time in recorded history that an Altmark hadn't noticed a stain.
> "Farrand."
> "What."
> She wasn't looking at him.
> "Tomorrow, I will have exchanged some silver for coppers."
> Noel looked up at the sky, which was blue and uninterested.
> Great, he thought. Now I have a regular.

**What to notice:** Worldbuilding through prices. A noble/commoner social map in a single exchange. The POV's hypothesis chain ends on the sad, human hypothesis. Kuudere reading ("did not change at all, which on Seraphina meant a great deal"). Snap-back banter ("He's not." / "I'm not."). A closing tilt.

### 8.3 Magic by its limits (≈360 words)

> The golem took Tessa's greatsword on its forearm and didn't even slow down.
> *Clang.*
> Tessa skidded backward across the flagstones.
> "That's cheating! Rocks aren't supposed to block!"
> "It's not a rock. It's granite with a core."
> Frost was already crawling up Seraphina's wand, white and crackling.
> "Stand aside."
> "*O frost that sleeps beneath the northern stars--*"
> Noel did not roll his eyes. He was proud of that.
> The ice lance formed above her head, long as a spear and sharp as a rumor. It flew. It struck the golem square in the chest.
> And shattered.
> Shards rained across the practice yard like broken glass. The golem took one step forward. Then another.
> Seraphina's knees buckled.
> It wasn't much. A dip, a half-second, and her hand shot out to steady herself on nothing. But Noel had spent two months watching nobles, and nobles didn't buckle. Not in front of commoners. Not ever. A noble who showed the cost of a spell was a noble admitting there *was* a cost.
> Mana debt. He'd read about it in the back of the first-year textbook, in the small print nobody was tested on. Draw more than your body can lend, and your body collects. Headaches first. Then tremors. Then worse.
> She'd put everything into that lance. For a practice golem. In front of the whole class.
> Why?
> He glanced at the observation balcony. At the tall man in Altmark silver standing there with his arms folded, watching his daughter the way you'd watch a horse you weren't sure you'd bought at a good price.
> Oh, Noel thought. Oh, that's why.
> The golem raised its fist.
> Noel snapped his fingers.
> Wind. Just wind. A single, precise gust, straight into the joint of the golem's knee, which, as the textbook also mentioned in small print, was where the core's binding runes were thinnest.
> The golem's leg folded like a bad argument.
> *Crash.*
> On the balcony, Duke Altmark's eyes moved from his daughter to the commoner beside her, and stayed there.
> Noel had the distinct feeling he'd just made a very expensive mistake.

**What to notice:** The magic system is revealed by failure and cost. The chant is used once, with the POV's embarrassment. The analytical POV reads the social pressure behind the spell (the father on the balcony). The underdog's single skill is used with precision. The closing tilt raises the stakes.

## 9. Common failure modes

1. **The encyclopedia opening.** The chapter starts with the history of the kingdom. *Fix:* Start with a person wanting something.
2. **Stat soup.** Pages of numbers. *Fix:* One screen early, then only changes, and always with a feeling attached.
3. **Everyone speaks the same.** Knights, nobles, and peasants all sound like modern teens. *Fix:* Give each class a register. Nobles use formal, full sentences. Adventurers use slang and clipped speech. The POV uses the base voice. See [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md).
4. **Purple awe.** Three paragraphs on the sunset over the spires. *Fix:* One long exhale, then a deflating line.
5. **Fights without faces.** Blow-by-blow with no feeling. *Fix:* See [Action & Battle](action-and-battle.md).
6. **Unmotivated nobles.** Arrogance with no cause. *Fix:* Every proud character is protecting something.
7. **Western-epic drift.** The prose grows archaic, "thee" and "thou" creep in, and the sentences get long. *Fix:* Re-read [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md). The LN voice stays contemporary even in a castle.

## 10. Genre revision checklist

- [ ] Did I explain more than three consecutive sentences of world information without a character reacting?
- [ ] Is every world fact carried by a conflict, a price, a joke, an object, or an opinion?
- [ ] Can the magic system's twist be stated in one sentence, and did it show up through a limit or cost?
- [ ] Is any status screen short, shown once, and followed by feeling?
- [ ] Do nobles, adventurers, and the POV each have a distinct speech register?
- [ ] Is the guild, academy, or party drawn as a social map with a center and edges?
- [ ] Did the POV underreact to at least one fantastical thing?
- [ ] Is every awe moment one long exhale plus a short deflation?
- [ ] Does the chapter change a relationship, a rank, or a theory, and end on a tilt?
- [ ] Are names easy to pronounce and consistent within each culture's palette?
