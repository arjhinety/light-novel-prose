# Isekai & Reincarnation

> **Purpose:** Write "transported to another world" and "reborn in another world" stories in the LN voice. It covers truck-kun openings and their alternatives, the modern-knowledge narrator, status and skill windows (with exact formatting), overpowered protagonists, slow life, reincarnated-as-X comedy, and the narrator's genre awareness.
> **Load when:** The protagonist comes from modern Earth (usually Japan) into a fantasy or game-like world, whether summoned, transported, or reborn.
> **Related:** [Genre Index](genre-index.md), [Fantasy](fantasy.md), [Villainess & Otome](villainess-and-otome.md), [Action & Battle](action-and-battle.md), [Rom-Com & Comedy](romcom-and-comedy.md), [Harem](harem.md), [Narration & POV](../core/narration-and-pov.md), [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md), [Tone & Comedy](../core/tone-and-comedy.md), [Punctuation & Typography](../core/punctuation-and-typography.md), [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md), [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md), [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md)

## 1. What isekai readers want

Isekai is the most crowded genre in web novels, which means readers are **experts**. They know every trope and have read the truck-kun opening a thousand times. What they want:

1. **A fast hook.** The world change happens in chapter one, often in the first thousand words. Web-novel readers decide in a few scrolls.
2. **A voice they'd follow anywhere.** The narrator, usually a modern Japanese person (often a tired office worker, NEET, or student), brings modern sensibility to a medieval world. The voice *is* the product. A sardonic, observant, self-deprecating narrator beats any power system.
3. **Competence fantasy with texture.** Watching someone solve problems with knowledge (modern hygiene, accounting, game mechanics) or with a strange power. The satisfaction is in the *specific* cleverness, not just in winning.
4. **A twist on the formula.** Reborn as a lighthouse. Summoned as the hero's accountant. A status screen that only displays other people's lies. The twist is the elevator pitch and the title.
5. **Emotional payoff about the old life.** The best isekai remember that the protagonist died or vanished. Somebody back home lost them. A single chapter where the narrator thinks about their mother's cooking can outweigh ten dragon fights.

Readers **tire of** unearned power, harems without personality (see [Harem](harem.md)), and narrators who explain gaming terms as if the reader has never played a game.

## 2. How the base voice shifts

### 2.1 POV: first person is common

Isekai is the one sub-genre where **first-person past tense** is as common as close third, because the narrator's modern commentary is the main attraction. Choose one:

| | First person | Close third |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | The commentary voice is direct and intimate. Jokes land faster. | Easier cut-aways. Easier to show others underestimating him. |
| Risk | Chatty, and can slip into "talking to the reader" too often | Loses a little of the snark |
| Tip | Keep asides to the reader at 1-3 per chapter | Free indirect thought carries the snark (see [Narration & POV](../core/narration-and-pov.md)) |

In first person, all the base-voice mechanics still apply: short beats, question chains, `"..."` silences, verdict lines. Only the pronoun changes.

### 2.2 The modern-knowledge narrator

The narrator filters everything through **Earth references**, and this is the genre's comedy and pathos engine at once.

- **Comparison humor:** "The Grand Cathedral was impressive in the way a Costco is impressive. Big, echoing, and full of people who wanted something."
- **Culture clash:** He is appalled by hygiene, baffled by the absence of forks, and bored by feudal etiquette.
- **Game literacy:** He recognizes the status screen. He guesses the quest-giver has a hidden agenda "because that's how it always goes."
- **Pathos through reference:** A vending-machine hum he'll never hear again. His phone's lock screen, a photo of his sister, that he can't remember clearly anymore.

**Rule:** Earth references should be **specific and a little dated**, the way a real person's references are. "Like a salaryman's last train on a Friday" beats "like something from modern times."

### 2.3 Rhythm and comedy

- Comedy ratio: **40-60%** in most isekai, and 20-30% in the "serious" isekai strain.
- The narrator's asides naturally lengthen paragraphs. **Counter this** with frequent one-line reaction beats and system notifications, which are rhythm devices in themselves.
- Big reveals (skill unlocked, OP ability demonstrated) land as **isolated one-line paragraphs** followed by a `"..."` from a witness.

### 2.4 Meta-awareness

The narrator knows he's in an isekai. Use it, but ration it:
- **Good:** A quick recognition, then something the genre didn't prepare him for. ("A goddess. Of course there was a goddess. What the light novels had not prepared me for was that she'd be doing her taxes.")
- **Bad:** Constant lampshading, where every trope is called out, which kills sincerity. One meta joke per chapter at most, and zero during emotional peaks.

## 3. Openings: truck-kun and the alternatives

### 3.1 Truck-kun (the classic)

A truck hits the protagonist, usually while he saves someone. Readers know it by heart. If you use it:
- **Get through it in under 300 words.**
- **Give it one specific human detail** that makes this death distinct (what was in his convenience-store bag, the last message on his phone).
- Or **subvert it** (the truck brakes in time, and he's summoned anyway, on the sidewalk, holding his onigiri).

### 3.2 Alternatives

| Opening | Flavor | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Overwork death** | Satirical and bleak | "Karoshi" office worker. A strong setup for a "slow life" story, since he finally rests. |
| **Class summoning** | Ensemble, school drama transplant | The whole Class 2-3 is summoned. The social map comes with them, and the loner is ranked lowest. School drama rules apply ([School Drama](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md)). |
| **Game login / VR trap** | Game-mechanical | Logs into a game and wakes up inside it. Stats are literal. |
| **Wakes up as someone else** | Mystery, identity | Mid-life, in another body, with memories bleeding in. Common in villainess stories ([Villainess & Otome](villainess-and-otome.md)). |
| **Born as a baby** | Slow burn | Reincarnation with memories intact. Early chapters are about infancy comedy, and the time skip must be handled cleanly. |
| **Summoned as the wrong person** | Comedy | The kingdom wanted the hero and got the hero's tax accountant. |
| **Portal / door** | Commuter isekai | A door in his apartment opens into another world, and he goes back and forth. Good for trade comedy. |
| **Reincarnated as an object or monster** | High-concept comedy | Slime, spider, sword, and so on. Invent your own; see §7. |

### 3.3 The first 1,000 words

1. **A specific modern moment** (100-300 words): who he was, one detail of his life, one regret
2. **The transition** (100-300 words): fast, sensory, a little absurd
3. **The new world's first image** (100-200 words): one striking detail, not a panorama
4. **The first reaction**: a deadpan or panicked line
5. **The first rule of the new world**, shown rather than told: a status screen, a goddess, a language he somehow understands, or a body that isn't his

End chapter one on a **hook**: a power revealed, a danger arriving, or a person whose first words change everything. See [Hooks & Cliffhangers](../structure/hooks-and-cliffhangers.md).

## 4. The OP protagonist problem

Overpowered protagonists are a core pleasure of the genre and its biggest trap. **Power without cost is boring.** The fixes:

1. **Power has a social cost.** He's strong, and that makes nobles scheme, churches suspicious, and friends afraid. The drama moves from "can he win?" to "what does winning cost him?"
2. **Power has a narrow shape.** He's unbeatable at one thing and useless at another (he can one-shot a dragon but can't read the local script and gets swindled at every market).
3. **Power is hidden.** He conceals his strength, which creates dramatic irony and underestimation scenes (see [Fantasy §5.4](fantasy.md#54-the-underestimated-protagonist)). Every reveal is an event.
4. **The problem isn't violence.** The real conflicts are social, emotional, political, or economic. The OP hero can't punch a famine or a grieving friend.
5. **The narrator's inner weakness.** Being unbeatable outside doesn't fix what's broken inside: the overwork trauma, the family he left, the loneliness. This is where the base voice's analytical monologue becomes the true story.

**Pacing the reveal:** In a hidden-power story, schedule reveals like cliffhangers. One major reveal per arc, with smaller leaks in between. Each reveal should have a **witness** whose reaction we see.

## 5. Status windows and skill notifications

### 5.1 Formatting

Use a consistent block format. Keep it **monospace-free and simple** so it renders anywhere. Recommended:

```
【Status】
Name: Sota Mikami
Race: Human (Otherworlder)
Level: 1
HP: 12 / 12
MP: 3 / 3

【Skills】
Appraisal Lv. 1
Bookkeeping Lv. 9

【Title】
Salaryman of the Month (Unclaimed)
```

For notifications, use one line, set apart as its own paragraph:

```
《Skill acquired: Haggling Lv. 1》
```

Pick bracket styles and **never change them**:
- `【 】` for full status headers
- `《 》` or `[ ]` for system notifications
- Log your choice in the story bible ([Story Bible Template](../templates/story-bible.md)).

### 5.2 Rules for status screens

1. **Show the full screen once**, early. Keep it under 12 lines.
2. **After that, show only changes**, as one-line notifications.
3. **Every screen needs a joke or a wound.** A title like "Salaryman of the Month (Unclaimed)" tells us about his old life in four words.
4. **The narrator reacts** in the beat right after the screen: disbelief, a deadpan comment, or a question chain.
5. **Never let a chapter's climax be a number.** The climax is what a person does with the number.
6. **Limit to 3-5 system notifications per chapter.** Beyond that, they're stat soup.

### 5.3 The system voice

If the "system" or skill has a voice (a talking skill, an AI assistant, a sarcastic appraisal function), treat it as a **character**. Give it a tic, a personality, and a relationship with the protagonist that develops. A cheerful, unhelpful system and a deadpan narrator make a classic boke/tsukkomi pair ([Rom-Com & Comedy](romcom-and-comedy.md)).

```
《Appraisal: This is a rock.》
...Thank you.
《You're welcome!》
```

## 6. Slow life

"Slow life" isekai (running a café, a farm, an inn, an apothecary) is **slice of life with a fantasy skin**, and it's hugely popular with readers exhausted by power fantasies. Everything in [School Drama & Slice of Life §5](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md#5-making-nothing-happens-chapters-compelling) applies: small wants, small obligations, specific textures, and rituals.

Special notes:
- **The narrator's burnout is the engine.** He wants rest. The world keeps offering adventure. Each chapter is a small war between his desire for peace and the people who need him. That war is funny and quietly sad.
- **Modern knowledge as cozy.** He brings mayonnaise, pudding, or proper bathhouses to the village. Readers love the specificity of the process: failed batches, local skepticism, the first villager's face on tasting it.
- **Found family by accretion.** One regular becomes three, then there's a live-in helper, then a stray beast-girl, then a retired knight who fixes the roof. The cast grows like a garden.
- **Time passes visibly.** Seasons matter more than in any other genre. Mark them.

## 7. Reincarnated-as-X comedy

The reincarnation-as-a-non-human premise is a comedy engine built on **constraint**. The narrator has a human mind in a body that can't do human things.

### 7.1 Designing your X

Ask four questions:
1. **What can it perceive?** (A lighthouse sees the whole coast but can't turn its head.)
2. **How can it act?** (It can dim or brighten its light, and that's it at first.)
3. **How does it grow?** (Levels unlock new actions: a foghorn, a second beam, a voice.)
4. **Who comes to it?** (Sailors, smugglers, a lonely keeper's daughter.)

The best Xs are **stationary or limited**, because the world has to come to them. That gives you a natural episodic structure where each visitor is a story.

### 7.2 Comedy mechanics
- **Misread by locals:** They think it's a god, a curse, or a haunted object.
- **Communication struggles:** Morse-like signals, blinking, and mistranslations.
- **The narrator's frustration** is deadpan and relatable ("I have been a lighthouse for eleven days and my back hurts. I do not have a back.")

### 7.3 Pathos mechanics
The same constraint produces sadness. He can see someone drowning and can only shine brighter. **Use this at least once per arc.** It's what makes the comedy matter.

## 8. Genre tropes: executing well vs. failing

### 8.1 The goddess meeting
**Well:** Short, specific, and the goddess has her own problems (overworked, bored, making a clerical error). One memorable gift or curse. Out in under 800 words.
**Fails:** A long cosmic lecture about the world's history.

### 8.2 The first village
**Well:** A small social map. The elder, the suspicious hunter, the curious kid, and the girl who finds him. First impressions set up the whole arc.
**Fails:** A generic tutorial zone.

### 8.3 Modern-knowledge uplift (inventions)
**Well:** Keep it grounded. He knows the idea but not the details, so the process is trial and error. "I knew mayonnaise was eggs, oil, and vinegar. I did not know it could split. I learned that forty eggs later."
**Fails:** He builds a steam engine from memory in an afternoon.

### 8.4 The adventurer registration
**Well:** An underestimation scene. See [Fantasy §8.1](fantasy.md#81-guild-arrival-480-words).
**Fails:** A repeated template.

### 8.5 The slave or companion purchase
**Choose the register deliberately.** It's a common trope with ugly implications, and the genre is full of bad versions. Played straight, it needs the narrator to notice the ugliness — horror, guilt, or a plan to end it — and the companion to have agency and a path out early. Played as dark comedy, as exploitation, or as open cruelty toward an adult companion, it is a register like any other (see [Ecchi §5.9](ecchi-and-fanservice.md#59-non-consent-and-dubious-consent)); what the narration never questions, the reader will read as endorsed. If you don't want to spend the chapter on it, use one of the alternatives: a rescued companion, a contracted employee, or a debt the narrator pays off for them.

### 8.6 The harem accumulation
**Well:** Each heroine joins for her own reason and has her own arc. See [Harem](harem.md).
**Fails:** Girls fall for him on sight.

### 8.7 The "wait, you're from Japan too?" reveal
**Well:** Another otherworlder appears with a different experience of the same premise. They might be a rival, a villain, or the only person who remembers ramen. That scene can be heartbreaking.
**Fails:** A cameo for a joke.

### 8.8 Returning home (or not)
**Well:** The question of going back haunts the story. When it's answered, it costs something either way.
**Fails:** Never mentioned after chapter one.

## 9. Chapter and arc shapes

### 9.1 Opening arc (5-10 chapters)
1. Death or transition, and arrival (ch. 1)
2. First rule and first power (ch. 1-2)
3. First person met: the anchor relationship (ch. 2)
4. First problem: small and local (ch. 3-4)
5. First use of power or knowledge, a reveal with a witness (ch. 4-5)
6. First cost: someone suspicious or someone hurt (ch. 5-6)
7. First home: a place to belong (ch. 6-8)
8. First glimpse of the larger threat, via a cut-away (end of arc)

### 9.2 Chapter shape
Base voice: numbered sections, each ending on a tilt. Isekai web novels are often **shorter per chapter** (1,500-3,000 words) with a high release cadence. Every chapter should contain at least one of: a new skill or rule, a new person, a reveal, or a joke that becomes a running gag. See [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md) for web-novel cadence.

### 9.3 Title conventions
Web-novel isekai titles are famously long and descriptive. That's genre-appropriate; embrace it for the series title:
- *I Was Reborn as a Lighthouse, So I'll Just Keep the Coast Safe (And Maybe Make a Friend)*
- *The Kingdom Summoned the Hero, but They Got His Tax Accountant Instead*

Chapter titles are short and punchy by contrast ("The Goddess Is Doing Her Taxes", "Forty Eggs").

## 10. Sample passages

Original cast for this file:
- **Sota Mikami**: a 28-year-old accountant from Saitama, summoned to the Kingdom of Aurelle by mistake (they wanted the hero, a college student who was standing next to him). First-person narrator.
- **Pim**: his skill "Ledger", which talks, cheerfully and unhelpfully.
- **Captain Ilse Varga**: a stoic knight captain assigned to "manage" him. A kuudere.
- **Lume**: the narrator of §10.3, reincarnated as a lighthouse.

### 10.1 First person, summoning by mistake (≈520 words)

> The last thing I saw in Japan was a vending machine.
> It was on the platform at Omiya, 11:47 p.m., and it was out of the black coffee I liked. I remember that. I remember standing there with a hundred-yen coin in my hand, deciding whether a sweetened café au lait counted as a defeat, and I remember the college kid next to me glowing.
> Not metaphorically. Glowing. Gold light pouring out of the ground under his sneakers in a circle with letters around the edge.
> The college kid blinked.
> "Eh?"
> "Eh?"
> Then the circle got bigger, and I was in it.
> 1
> "*Welcome, O Hero, to the Kingdom of Aurelle!*"
> Marble floor. Stained glass. About forty people in robes, all kneeling, and at the front of them a girl in a white dress with a crown so heavy it made her neck look tired. The air smelled like candle smoke and very old stone.
> The college kid was standing next to me, blinking. He was tall and had excellent hair. He looked like a hero. He looked like a hero in the way I looked like someone who does year-end adjustments for a mid-sized logistics company, which is to say, at a glance.
> The princess's eyes went to him and lit up.
> Then they came to me.
> Then they went back to him.
> Then they came back to me, slower, the way you'd recheck a receipt.
> "...Two?"
> Behind her, an old man in the tallest hat I'd ever seen started flipping through a very large book, very fast.
> "Your Highness, the ritual specifies *one* hero--"
> "I *know* what it specifies, Archbishop."
> "--so the second one is, ah, how do I put this--"
> "Collateral."
> Forty heads turned to me.
> "..."
> "Sorry. Professional habit."
> I raised a hand.
> "I'm not the hero. Pretty sure it's him. I was just standing too close to the circle. If there's a return policy, I'd like to use it."
> The Archbishop looked at the princess. The princess looked at the Archbishop.
> Nobody said the words *there's no return policy*. Nobody needed to.
> The college kid was staring at his hands, which had started to glow again, softly, the way a phone does when it gets a notification. I looked at my own hands. They did not glow. They were the hands of a man who'd eaten a convenience-store karaage bento at his desk for dinner every night that month.
> Then something went *ding* in my head.
> ```
> 【Status】
> Name: Sota Mikami
> Class: None (Accompanying Party)
> Level: 1
>
> 【Skills】
> Ledger Lv. 1
> ```
> 《Hello! I'm Ledger! I keep track of things!》
> "..."
> 《What would you like to track today?》
> The college kid, across from me, gasped. I assumed his status screen said something like *Holy Sword Mastery*. Mine said I was a spreadsheet.
> 《I'm very excited to work with you!》
> I closed my eyes.
> Somewhere in Saitama, I thought, the vending machine on the Omiya platform was still out of black coffee. And somewhere in my apartment, my phone was lighting up with a message from my sister asking if I'd be home for Obon.
> I opened my eyes.
> "Your Highness, before anything else. Who handles your kingdom's books?"

**What to notice:** A specific modern moment (vending machine, the café au lait "defeat"). A fast transition. The princess's re-check "like a receipt", a job-specific metaphor. A single status screen with a joke in it. A talking skill as the boke. Pathos placed right after comedy (the sister's message). The final line pivots toward the protagonist's real power.

### 10.2 The OP reveal with a witness, close third (≈400 words)

> Captain Ilse Varga had been assigned to babysit the accountant for eleven days, and in those eleven days she had formed a clear and professional opinion of him.
> Harmless. Tired. Owned one shirt he washed every night in the barracks basin and hung by the window like a flag of surrender.
> He didn't look up from the treasury ledgers stacked on the table.
> "Captain. Who approved the grain shipments to the eastern garrison?"
> "The Minister of Supply."
> "Mm."
> He turned a page. His finger moved down a column of numbers faster than she could read them.
> "The eastern garrison has three hundred soldiers."
> "Yes."
> "They've been sent grain for nine hundred. Every month. For four years."
> Ilse said nothing. Her hand, resting on her sword, didn't move. She had been trained not to move.
> 《Discrepancy detected!》 said the cheerful voice only he could hear, and he winced, as he always did, as if a small bird had sung directly into his ear.
> His voice stayed mild.
> "Where do you think the other six hundred soldiers' worth of grain is going, Captain?"
> "..."
> "Because it's not going east. The wagons come back empty too fast."
> He tapped a page.
> "Two days out, two days back. The eastern garrison is five days away."
> The room had gone very quiet. Outside, a bell rang for the evening prayer, long and low.
> Ilse looked at the ledgers. She looked at the tired man in the one shirt. For eleven days she'd thought the kingdom had summoned a mistake.
> The Hero could split a boulder with his holy sword. She'd seen it. It had been very impressive and completely useless, because the boulder hadn't been stealing from anyone.
> "Mikami-dono."
> He looked up. It was the first time she'd used his name.
> "Who else have you told?"
> "Nobody. You're the only one who's been in the room."
> Ilse crossed to the door and locked it.
> *Click.*
> Sota, to his credit, did not panic. He only sighed, the long, deep sigh of a man who had been here before, in a different world, in a different conference room.
> "Right. So it's that kind of kingdom."

**What to notice:** A cut-away-style POV for the witness, to show the reveal's impact. The kuudere's reaction is shown through stillness. The power is non-violent (accounting) and has a clear social cost (danger). Name escalation ("Mikami-dono") marks the shift. The final tilt is ambiguous: is she ally or threat?

### 10.3 Reincarnated as a lighthouse (≈360 words)

> On my forty-first day as a lighthouse, a girl climbed my stairs.
> I felt her the way you feel an itch: two hundred and twelve stone steps, one at a time, a small warm weight moving up my spine. I would have turned to look if I had a neck. I did not have a neck. I had a lamp room, a gallery, and a very good view of the Salt Coast, and that was the whole of my body.
> She came out onto the gallery, out of breath. Twelve, maybe thirteen. Freckles. A basket over one arm and a scarf that the wind kept trying to steal.
> She spoke to the lamp.
> "Hello."
> Nobody had spoken to me in forty-one days.
> I brightened. I didn't mean to. It just happened, the way a face goes red.
> The girl jumped back. Stared. The basket swung on her arm.
> Her voice dropped to a whisper.
> "...You're awake. Grandpa said you were. Nobody believed him."
> I dimmed, then brightened again. It was the only word I knew how to say.
> She laughed. It was the first laugh I'd heard since the truck.
> "Okay."
> She sat down cross-legged on the stone, right under the lamp, like we'd arranged to meet here.
> "Okay. Then. One flash for yes, two for no?"
> One flash.
> "Are you a ghost?"
> Two.
> "A god?"
> Two. Emphatically.
> "Are you... lonely?"
> I didn't answer right away.
> Forty-one days. Ships passing in the night and never once looking up. Gulls. Weather. The same fourteen stars in the same order. I had been an office worker in Nagoya with a studio apartment and one plant, and I had thought, back then, that I understood loneliness. I had not understood anything.
> One flash. Long and slow.
> The girl nodded seriously, as though I'd told her something important. Then she opened her basket.
> "I brought bread. I know you can't eat it. I'll eat it for you."
> She ate it for me.
> It was, I think, the best meal I'd ever had.

**What to notice:** Constraint comedy (no neck, one "word"). Pathos arrives through the same constraint. The yes/no protocol is a running gag in the making. The Earth-life reference (a Nagoya studio and one plant) is specific. The last line is a quiet, earnest verdict with no irony at the peak.

### 10.4 Note: the class-summoning variant

When a whole class is summoned, **import the classroom map intact** (see [School Drama §3](school-drama-and-slice-of-life.md#3-the-social-map-hierarchy-as-setting)). The popular boy gets the Hero class, the bully queen gets a charisma skill that makes her even more dangerous, and the loner gets something that looks useless on the status board. The first chapter in the new world is a **seat lottery with swords**: the king's appraiser reads each student's status aloud, and the POV's cynical monologue tracks how the old hierarchy survives the move and where it cracks.

> "Minato Kuze. Class: Observer. Skill: Insight Lv. 1."
> Somebody at the back laughed. Hoshino, probably. It was always Hoshino.
> Kuze looked at the words floating above his palm and felt, for the first time since the gold light swallowed Class 2-3, something close to relief.
> Observer. Well. At least the other world had read his file.

The drama follows naturally: the popular group becomes the kingdom's darlings, the outcast is expelled or sent to the worst post, and the question "who was really kind back in the classroom?" becomes a matter of life and death.

## 11. Common failure modes

1. **The long death.** Three pages of the old life before the transition. *Fix:* Under 300 words, with one detail.
2. **Stat-soup chapters.** Numbers as content. *Fix:* §5.2 rules.
3. **Explaining games to gamers.** "In video games, which are interactive entertainment..." *Fix:* Assume the reader is fluent.
4. **The narrator forgets he's from Earth.** After chapter three he's indistinguishable from a native. *Fix:* One Earth reference per chapter, with at least one per arc that hurts.
5. **Everyone loves him instantly.** *Fix:* Suspicion, jealousy, and indifference are the default. Affection is earned on-page.
6. **Lampshade overload.** *Fix:* One meta joke per chapter maximum.
7. **Power fixes feelings.** *Fix:* Inner wounds need inner solutions: people, time, choices.
8. **Unexamined ugly tropes** (slavery, coercion, "purchasing" companions). *Fix:* See §8.5. They are usable — as horror, as dark comedy, as an explicit adult register — but never as set dressing the narration fails to notice.

## 12. Genre revision checklist

- [ ] Does the world change happen within the first ~1,000 words of chapter one?
- [ ] Is the old life anchored by at least one specific, slightly dated detail?
- [ ] Are status screens short, consistently formatted, and followed by a human reaction?
- [ ] Are there 5 or fewer system notifications in this chapter?
- [ ] Does the protagonist's power carry a cost (social, narrow shape, hidden, or inner)?
- [ ] Did an Earth reference land as a joke, and has one landed as a wound this arc?
- [ ] Is meta-awareness limited to one moment, and absent from emotional peaks?
- [ ] Did each OP reveal have a witness whose reaction we see?
- [ ] Is the talking system (if any) a character with its own tic?
- [ ] Are all companions agents with their own wants?
- [ ] Does the chapter end on a tilt: a reveal, an arrival, or a question?
