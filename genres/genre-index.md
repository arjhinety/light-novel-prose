# Genre Index: Choosing, Tuning, and Blending Genres

> **Purpose:** Map every genre module in this library, explain how the base light-novel voice bends for each one, and show how to blend genres without losing the voice.
> **Load when:** Before planning any story that is not a pure school drama, or whenever a request names a genre ("harem rom-com", "isekai slice-of-life", "ecchi comedy", "villainess").
> **Related:** [Style Bible](../core/style-bible.md), [Tone & Comedy](../core/tone-and-comedy.md), [Archetypes](../cast/archetypes.md), [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md), [Character Tropes](../cast/character-tropes.md), [Arc Design](../structure/arc-design.md), [School Life](../setting/school-life.md)

---

## 1. The one rule that governs every genre

**The voice stays. The dials move.**

Every genre file in this folder assumes the same foundation, described in the [Style Bible](../core/style-bible.md):

- Close third person anchored to one point-of-view character, usually named by surname ([Narration & POV](../core/narration-and-pov.md)).
- An inner monologue that *reads people*: it notices small asymmetries, quotes words back, spins hypotheses, and suspects itself ([Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md)).
- A short-beat rhythm with deliberate long exhales ([Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md)).
- Dialogue carrying roughly half the word count, stacked as pure dialogue lines with no tags in the quote paragraph ([Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md)).
- Anime-visual portraits, honorifics, silent `"..."` beats, and sound-effect lines.

A genre does not replace any of that. It changes *how much* of each ingredient you use, *what* the POV character's analytical mind is pointed at, and *which* scenes the chapters are built from. A fantasy battle is still narrated by someone who overthinks. A harem comedy still has a narrator who notices who got the last croquette. A tearjerker still breathes in short beats. It just breathes slower.

If you ever find yourself writing a genre chapter that could have come from a generic English-language novel in that genre, you have turned off the voice. Go back and put the POV character's head back in the prose.

## 3. The voice dials

Think of each genre as a mixing-board preset. The eight dials:

1. **Introspection density.** What share of narration is the POV character's analysis versus reported action. The home setting is high: whole paragraphs of "Was it...? Or was it...?"
2. **Comedy ratio.** How often a scene resolves on a joke, an underreaction, or a tsukkomi.
3. **Beat length.** The average sentence length and the frequency of one-line paragraphs. Action drives it down. Romance and melodrama let it drift up, but only in *flow* (more connectives, longer exhales at quiet moments). Every genre still has to pass the measurable gates in [Revision Checklist](../revision/revision-checklist.md): narrative median at or below 10 words, and no more than 5% of narrative sentences at 25 words or more. A "4" on this dial means more exhales, not a different voice.
4. **Description density.** How much space scenery and bodies get. The home setting is portrait-first and scenery-light. Fantasy raises scenery. Ecchi raises bodies for adult casts and stays controlled when the cast is young.
5. **Cast breadth.** How many named characters share a scene. A harem scene often holds five or more. Melodrama often holds two.
6. **Meta awareness.** How often the narrator notices the genre he is standing in. This is highest in isekai and rom-com and nearly zero in horror.
7. **Stakes register.** Social (reputation, belonging), romantic (who likes whom), physical (injury, death), or existential (identity, memory).
8. **Sincerity ceiling.** How earnest the story is allowed to become at its peaks. Every genre in this library has *some* earnest peak. The dial controls how high it goes and how often.

## 4. The genre-versus-dial matrix

Values are relative to the home setting (school drama), which is the baseline of **3** on a 1-5 scale.

| Genre | Introspection | Comedy | Beat length | Description | Cast breadth | Meta | Stakes | Sincerity ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| School drama / SoL | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | Social | 4 |
| Rom-com | 3 | 5 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 4 | Romantic | 3 |
| Harem | 3 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 4 | Romantic + social | 3 |
| Ecchi comedy | 2 | 5 | 2 | 3-5 (register) | 4 | 4 | Embarrassment | 2 |
| Romance / melodrama | 4 | 2 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 1 | Romantic + existential | 5 |
| Fantasy | 3 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 2 | Physical + political | 4 |
| Isekai | 4 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 5 | Physical + comic | 3 |
| Villainess / otome | 5 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 5 | Social + mortal | 4 |
| Action / battle | 2 in fights, 4 between | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | Physical | 4 |
| Mystery / psych / horror | 5 | 1 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 1 | Existential + social | 4 |
| Sports / idol / club | 3 | 3 | 2 in matches, 3 otherwise | 2 | 4 | 2 | Competitive | 5 |

How to read it: in a harem chapter, spend less time on scenery than you would in fantasy, put five people in the room, cut sentences short, and let the narrator notice the rom-com he is in. In a melodrama chapter, slow down, put two people in the room, and let a paragraph run long. Keep the jokes rare enough that each one lands like relief.

## 5. What each genre points the analytical mind at

The inner monologue is the engine in every genre. Only its *target* changes.

| Genre | The POV character keeps asking... |
|---|---|
| School drama | "Why is *he* the one everyone likes? What does this seating arrangement mean?" |
| Rom-com | "Did she mean that the way it sounded? Is this a misunderstanding or am I the misunderstanding?" |
| Harem | "Who does he actually like? Why does she laugh at his jokes and not mine? Which girl is performing?" |
| Ecchi | "How do I survive the next thirty seconds without being called a pervert?" |
| Romance | "What did that pause mean? Am I allowed to want this?" |
| Fantasy | "What is the rule of this world, and who benefits from it?" |
| Isekai | "Which game/novel mechanic is this? What would a normal person from home do here?" |
| Villainess | "Which flag did that line trigger? Who in this ballroom wants me dead?" |
| Action | "What is the opponent's pattern? What does my body have left?" |
| Mystery / horror | "Who is lying? Is it me?" |
| Sports / idol | "What do they have that I don't? Is effort enough?" |

The self-suspicion move ("Or was that just his own jealousy talking?") survives in every genre. It is what keeps the narrator human instead of a detective, a tactician, or a comedian. See [Inner Monologue](../core/inner-monologue.md) for the full pattern.

## 6. Picking a genre from a request

Users rarely name one clean genre. Translate the request:

| User says | Primary | Secondary | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Anime-style school story" | School drama/SoL | Rom-com | Default home setting |
| "Harem comedy" | Harem | Rom-com | Keep the lead likable. See [Harem §4](harem.md#4-the-leads-likability-problem). |
| "Ecchi" | Ecchi comedy | Rom-com | Establish ages on the page. Adults are unrestricted; minors get no sexual rendering. See the [content line](ecchi-and-fanservice.md#1-the-content-line) and the [register table](ecchi-and-fanservice.md#3-choosing-the-register). |
| "Isekai" | Isekai | Fantasy | Decide: OP power fantasy or slow life? |
| "Villainess" | Villainess/otome | Romance, mystery | Doom flags are the plot engine |
| "Something sad" / "tearjerker" | Melodrama | School SoL | Plant the grief early |
| "Yandere story" | Psychological | Rom-com or horror | The dere-type file sets the character. The genre sets the tone. |
| "Magic academy" | Fantasy | School life + rom-com | Import the school calendar wholesale |
| "Battle shonen" | Action | Sports-style rivalry | Keep a talky, analytical lead between fights |
| "Band / idol" | Sports/idol/club | Rom-com | Performance scenes use match rhythm |
| "Mystery at school" | Mystery | School drama | The mastermind thread from [Series Planning](../structure/series-planning.md) |

If the request is ambiguous and the model is allowed one question, ask: **"Mostly laughs, mostly tension, or mostly feels?"** That single answer sets the comedy and sincerity dials, and everything else follows.

## 7. Blending genres

Most successful light novels are blends. A pure genre gets stale by volume two. The rules for blending:

### 7.1 One genre owns the chapter

Within any single chapter, one genre sets the dials. Blending happens *across* chapters and *across* sections, not inside every paragraph. A harem rom-com with a drama arc runs comedic chapters, then shifts into a drama arc for a stretch, then comes back. It does not try to be both at every line.

### 7.2 The pivot section

When a chapter moves between genres (comedy into drama is the most common), use a numbered section break as the hinge. The last comedic section ends on a gag. The next section opens with a flat, quiet beat:

> "Lame. Two points."
> Enomoto clutched his chest as if shot, and Hina laughed so hard she had to sit on the flowerbed ledge.
>
> 4
>
> The next morning, Kuze's desk was covered in paper.

The contrast does the tonal work. You do not need a transition sentence like "But things were about to take a darker turn." Never write that sentence.

### 7.3 Common blends and how they work

**Harem rom-com + school drama (the source's own blend).** The comedy is the surface and the drama is the undertow. The narrator's analysis of the harem lead is funny for ten chapters, then turns out to be right, or wrong, in a way that hurts someone. Plant the drama in the comedy: every gag about the lead's obliviousness is also evidence. See [Harem §7](harem.md#7-harem-plus-drama-when-the-comedy-has-teeth).

**Isekai + slice of life.** The power fantasy is resolved early (the lead is strong enough), so tension comes from *daily* problems: opening a café, raising a dragon hatchling, a neighbor's leaky roof. Comedy and warmth are high and stakes are low. Keep the narrator's modern-world analysis ("This is a supply-chain problem. I have seen this on a documentary.") as the engine. See [Isekai](isekai-and-reincarnation.md).

**Fantasy academy + rom-com.** Import the whole school calendar from [School Life](../setting/school-life.md) and rename things: the sports festival becomes the Tournament of Houses, and cleaning duty becomes stable duty. The rom-com runs on the same misunderstanding engine as a modern school. See [Fantasy](fantasy.md).

**Rom-com + mystery.** "Who sent the love letter?" and "Who leaked the photo?" A mystery spine gives rom-com chapters forward drive. The narrator's suspicion engine does double duty.

**Ecchi + harem.** Classic, and the most dangerous for voice. Without the analytical narrator it becomes a list of accidents. Keep the lead's *embarrassment and reasoning* at the center — the body is what the reasoning is about, not a replacement for it — and apply the content rule: adults unrestricted, minors never sexualized.

**Villainess + romance.** The doom flag is the external clock and the slow burn is the internal one. The heroine thinks she is dodging a death flag while the reader sees she is triggering a love flag. Dramatic irony is the comedy.

**Sports + melodrama.** The injury arc, the last tournament, or the senpai's final match. Match rhythm for play, melodrama rhythm for the locker room after.

### 7.4 Blends to handle carefully

- **Ecchi + melodrama.** Tonal whiplash rarely works. If a story needs both, separate them by whole arcs, and never play a character's trauma for fanservice.
- **Horror + harem comedy.** Works only if the horror is *comic* horror (a haunted clubroom, the girls screaming at a cockroach), or if the comedy is a deliberate mask that the horror tears off in a planned twist.
- **Yandere + rom-com.** The yandere's danger must be real *or* clearly comic, and the story must decide which. Half-and-half reads as endorsing the behavior. See [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md).

## 8. Genre and the cast

Genre changes which archetypes carry the story. Quick reference:

| Genre | Must-have roles | Common dere/tropes |
|---|---|---|
| School drama | Cynical POV, harem-lead foil, bully queen, confidant | Tsundere, kuudere, childhood friend |
| Rom-com | Straight man (tsukkomi) and a fool (boke) | Tsundere, genki, teasing senpai |
| Harem | Lead plus 3-6 differentiated heroines, the "friend who comments" | One of each: tsundere, kuudere, genki, onee-san, childhood friend, kouhai |
| Ecchi | The flustered lead, the shameless tease, the one who hits | Onee-san (adult), tsundere, oblivious airhead |
| Romance | Two leads, one obstacle (person, illness, distance) | Dandere, kuudere, childhood friend |
| Fantasy | Mentor, rival, party members | Kuudere mage, genki swordswoman, himedere princess |
| Isekai | The reincarnated lead, the first local who trusts them | Onee-san guild clerk, loyal beastkin, chuunibyou ally |
| Villainess | The villainess, the "heroine" of the original game, the princes | Ojou-sama, himedere, kuudere butler |
| Action | Rival, master, the one to protect | Kuudere rival, deredere partner |
| Mystery | Detective-ish POV, mastermind, red herring | Yandere, undere, the transfer student |
| Sports/idol | Captain, prodigy, underdog | Genki, kouhai, stoic senpai |

## 9. Genre-agnostic checks

After drafting in any genre, confirm:

1. **Is the POV character's head in every section?** At least one paragraph per section should be analysis, doubt, or wry commentary.
2. **Is dialogue still around half the text?** Action and fantasy tend to drift into narration-heavy blocks. Pull them back.
3. **Is there at least one silent `"..."` beat?** It fits every genre, including battle (a rival who won't answer) and ecchi (the frozen lead).
4. **Did the genre's clichés get used *knowingly*?** Every genre file lists tropes with notes on how they fail. Use a trope with awareness, since the narrator can even comment on it, or subvert it.
5. **Is there one earnest beat per chapter?** Even the silliest ecchi chapter has one line where someone means what they say.
6. **Run the full revision pass** in [Revision Checklist](../revision/revision-checklist.md) and [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md).

## 10. A single scene, five genres

The same premise (Kuze is asked to stay after class by a girl) tuned five ways shows how the dials move. The premise is fixed and only the genre changes.

### 10.1 School drama (baseline)

> Kujou stopped at his desk without looking at him.
> "Stay after class."
> Not *could you* or *would you mind*. Just an instruction, dropped on his desk like homework.
> Kuze watched her walk back to Amamiya's side, where her voice climbed half an octave.
> Why me? Why today? Why say it where Hoshino could hear?
> The sediment stirred.

### 10.2 Rom-com

> Kujou stopped at his desk.
> "Stay after class."
> Twelve heads turned. Somewhere near the window, a boy dropped his melon bread.
> Gon leaned over, whispering.
> "Is this a confession? Verily, is this a confession?"
> "It's cleaning duty. We're on the same rota."
> Gon nodded gravely.
> "That is exactly what a confession would say."

### 10.3 Harem

> Kujou stopped at his desk.
> "Stay after class."
> Tachibana's pencil snapped. Hina's smile froze at exactly the angle it had been at, which was somehow worse than a frown. Momo tilted her head, phone already halfway out of her pocket.
> Amamiya looked from Kujou to Kuze and back.
> "Whoa, whoa. Sayo, what's that about?"
> "Class business."
> Kujou patted Amamiya's hand in a way that was not class business at all.
> Ah, Kuze thought. I'm the chess piece today.

### 10.4 Mystery / psychological

> Kujou stopped at his desk.
> "Stay after class."
> She didn't lower her voice. That was the first thing he noticed. Someone who wanted a secret meeting would have whispered, so she wanted witnesses.
> The second thing was her left hand, tapping twice on his desk. It was the same rhythm she'd tapped on Hoshino's desk last Thursday, the day before Hoshino stopped coming to school.
> "..."
> Kuze said nothing. He counted the people who had heard.

### 10.5 Romance / melodrama

> Kujou stopped at his desk.
> "Stay after class."
> Her voice was smaller than usual. It still came out as an order, but there was a gap in the middle of it where a *please* might have gone.
> After the room emptied, she sat on the windowsill with the sunset behind her and did not say anything for a long time.
> Kuze didn't either.
> Outside, the baseball team was calling the last drill of the day, and the sound came up faint and far away, as if from another year.

Notice what stayed constant: the order-without-please, the POV reading it, and the short beats. What changed: the size of the audience, the reading's target, and the pace of the last line.

## 11. Genre drift: how stories go off course

Long serials drift. Watch for these signs and correct them early.

| Drift | Symptom | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Comedy creep** | Every drama beat now ends on a gag. The sincerity ceiling has collapsed to 1. | Write one full section with no jokes at all. Let silence and `"..."` carry it. |
| **Drama creep** | A rom-com has had three straight chapters of misery | Reinstate the running gags and bring the confidant back for a comic scene |
| **Exposition creep** (fantasy, isekai) | Paragraphs of world rules with no one reacting | Convert rules to dialogue. Someone asks, someone answers badly, and the narrator corrects in his head. |
| **Fanservice creep** | Beats per chapter keep rising, and the plot stops | Tie every beat to a relationship change or a plot turn. Raising the heat is fine; freezing the story to do it is not. Re-check the [content rule](ecchi-and-fanservice.md#1-the-content-line). |
| **Cast bloat** (harem, fantasy) | Eight named characters in every scene, most silent | Use the "two talk, one reacts, the rest are furniture" rule for any scene over five people |
| **Narrator erasure** (action) | Fight chapters read like choreography notes | Put one analytical or self-doubting paragraph between exchanges |
| **Tone lock** | Every chapter has the same shape and the same emotional temperature | Vary the chapter type: a quiet chapter, a comic chapter, a crisis chapter |

## 12. Genre-appropriate openings

The first line of a genre chapter signals the contract. Quick patterns:

| Genre | Strong opening shape | Example first line |
|---|---|---|
| School drama | A sound, or the POV alone and thinking | *Ding-dong-dang-dong.* |
| Rom-com | An absurd line of dialogue | "I have decided to become your rival in love, destiny, and the bread lottery." |
| Harem | Group chatter in medias res | "Haruto, you're late!" |
| Ecchi comedy | The narrator's genre-aware dread | He knew this corridor. He had read this corridor. |
| Romance | A small sensory detail and a time stamp | The last train, Tuesday. Her umbrella dripping on his shoe. |
| Fantasy | A rule of the world stated by a character who hates it | "Third-years get the east tower. First-years get the stairs." |
| Isekai | The narrator's deadpan assessment of a very strange situation | Kuze had been dead for eleven minutes and already disliked the paperwork. |
| Villainess | The heroine recognizing a scene from a game | Oh, she thought, watching the teacup fall. This is the tea party where I get exiled. |
| Action | A single short impact line | Steel. Then the floor. |
| Mystery / horror | A normal detail that is slightly wrong | There were thirty-one desks in Class 2-3. There were thirty-one students. There had always been thirty. |
| Sports / idol | The score, the clock, or the count-in | Two minutes. One point. The gym was so loud it had gone silent. |

None of these is mandatory. Vary them across chapters as [Chapter & Section](../structure/chapter-and-section.md) recommends. But when a new genre arc begins, an opening that matches its contract tells the reader the dials have moved.

## 14. Fan fiction and existing series

A request set in an existing series ("a chapter of [series] with [canon characters]") is **not a new genre**. It is an existing genre (usually harem, rom-com, or school drama) with a canon contract on top. Pick the genre file as normal, then load [Fan Fiction & Canon](../adaptation/fanfic-and-canon.md) and write a canon sheet for every canon character before drafting.

Three rules matter most, because test runs broke all three:

1. **The default cast is example material only.** Kuze, Rin, Sayo and the rest exist to illustrate techniques. Never give their traits (silver-ash hair, "lawful wife", the sediment) to canon characters.
2. **Follow the source's conventions over this library's defaults.** If the series narrates with given names, narrate with given names, even though the base voice anchors on surnames. If its lead is sincere, use the sincere temperament.
3. **When unsure, underspecify.** Leave out a hair color you aren't sure of rather than invent one, and never present an invented prop or character as canon.

The LN mechanics (pure dialogue lines, short beats, numbered sections, silences, the re-aimed analytical engine) still apply on top of canon.
