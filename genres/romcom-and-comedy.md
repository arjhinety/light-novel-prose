# Rom-Com & Comedy

> **Purpose:** Teach the mechanics of light-novel romantic comedy and gag comedy in prose: misunderstanding engines, tsukkomi/boke timing, escalation, callbacks, and how the cynical narrator makes jokes land on the page.
> **Load when:** Writing any chapter where laughs are the primary goal, or any rom-com scene inside a drama, fantasy, or harem story.
> **Related:** [Genre Index](genre-index.md), [Harem](harem.md), [Ecchi & Fanservice](ecchi-and-fanservice.md), [Tone & Comedy](../core/tone-and-comedy.md), [Dialogue Mechanics](../dialogue/dialogue-mechanics.md), [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md), [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md), [Rhythm & Flow](../core/rhythm-and-flow.md)

---

## 1. What rom-com readers want

1. **Chemistry they can hear.** Readers come for two (or more) people whose conversation crackles. Banter is the main course, not garnish.
2. **Delayed payoff.** Rom-com runs on *almost*. Almost confessing, almost holding hands, almost being understood. Each "almost" must feel like progress, not stalling.
3. **Embarrassment as intimacy.** The funniest scenes are the ones where a character is seen more clearly than they wanted. In a rom-com, being exposed is how people get closer.
4. **Reliable rhythm.** Readers want a laugh every few hundred words. A rom-com chapter with a 1,500-word dry patch feels broken.
5. **A narrator on their side.** The POV character's commentary is the reader's companion in the joke. When Kuze thinks "This is a rom-com, isn't it. I've been conscripted into a rom-com," the reader laughs *with* him.
6. **One sincere line per chapter.** The laugh-rich chapter earns one moment where someone means it. Without that, the romance never moves.

## 2. How the voice shifts for rom-com

Relative to the home setting (see [Genre Index §4](genre-index.md#4-the-genre-versus-dial-matrix)):

- **Comedy ratio up to 5.** Most sections resolve on a joke, a deadpan retort, or a physical gag.
- **Beats shorter.** Comedy lives on timing, and timing on the page means short paragraphs and line breaks placed where a comedian would pause.
- **Introspection stays but turns wry.** The narrator's analytical machinery is aimed at romantic signals, and its over-seriousness *is* the joke. A detective-level reading of a shared umbrella is funny because the stakes are so low.
- **Description is lean,** except for reaction faces, which are anime shorthand: the flush to the ears, the puffed cheeks, the eyes going blank like a switched-off TV.
- **Meta awareness up.** The narrator recognizes tropes as they happen. This is a strong LN signature and a safe source of laughs.

## 3. The comedy toolkit in prose

### 3.1 Tsukkomi and boke

The Japanese manzai pairing is the backbone of LN comedy:
- The **boke** (fool) says or does something absurd.
- The **tsukkomi** (straight man) corrects it, usually bluntly.

In this voice, **the cynical POV is almost always the tsukkomi**, and the correction often happens *in narration* rather than aloud. That creates two layers of joke: the spoken retort and the inner one.

> Gon announced it to the whole classroom.
> "I have decided to become a man of the sea."
> "You get carsick on escalators."
> "The sea is not an escalator."
> Correct, and irrelevant, Kuze thought.

**Rules:**
- The tsukkomi line is *shorter* than the boke line. Brevity is the punch.
- Let the boke defend the absurdity sincerely ("The sea is not an escalator"). Sincerity keeps the fool lovable.
- Rotate who is boke. When the cynical narrator becomes the fool for once, perhaps flustered by a girl, that role reversal is itself a joke. Let Enomoto or Tachibana be the tsukkomi then.

### 3.2 Underreaction

The flattest possible response to the biggest possible stimulus.

> Hina leapt onto his desk, struck a pose, and declared that from today onward the two of them were rivals in love, destiny, and the school's snack-bread lottery.
> "Okay."
> "Just 'okay'?!"
> "You're standing on my notebook."

The underreaction works when (a) the stimulus is described with energy, (b) the reply is one or two words, and (c) there is a follow-up that shows the narrator noticed something *completely different* from what mattered.

### 3.3 Overreaction and escalation

The reverse: a small stimulus provokes an outsized response, which then escalates. Tsundere and genki characters do this best.

The **escalation ladder** has three rungs and then a break:
1. Normal response.
2. Bigger response.
3. Absurdly big response.
4. **The break:** a flat line, or a physical interruption (a bell, a teacher, someone falling).

> "I-It's not like I made extra on purpose."
> "Okay."
> "I just miscounted the eggs!"
> "Okay."
> "Three times! I miscounted three times! Eggs are hard to count!"
> Ding-dong-dang-dong.
> She shoved the bento into his chest and fled before the bell finished.

### 3.4 The rule of three with a twist

List two expected items, then a third that breaks the pattern.

> Amamiya had three talents: remembering every girl's birthday, remembering every girl's favorite snack, and forgetting Kuze's name.

Keep it to *one* such list per scene. Stacked tricolons read as a machine (see [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md)).

### 3.5 The callback

A line or object from earlier returns with a new meaning. Callbacks are the most satisfying comedy in serialized fiction because they reward loyal readers.

- Plant the element in passing, without spotlighting it.
- Return to it at least one chapter later.
- Change its meaning on return. The black coffee Kuze drinks "to seem mature" in chapter 2 becomes the coffee Tachibana silently buys him in chapter 9, still black, which proves she was paying attention.

Track callbacks in the story bible ([Story Bible template](../templates/story-bible.md)).

### 3.6 The misread signal

The romantic engine of rom-com. One character sends a signal and another reads it wrongly. The narrator's analysis is detailed, logical, and completely off-target.

> She had bought two drinks and handed him one. Kuze turned the can over. Barley tea. The cheapest thing in the machine.
> A calculated message, then. Not the milk tea she liked, not anything that could be mistaken for thought. The message was clear: this is a transaction. We are even for the notes.
> Tachibana's face was red to the ears.
> "It was the only button that wasn't sold out."
> Also possible, Kuze conceded.

The joke is the contrast between the narrator's intelligence and the simple truth. Reveal the truth through dialogue or a reaction face, and never through the narrator's own sudden insight. He should stay a little wrong.

### 3.7 The deadpan scoreboard

The narrator or a character grades things numerically. It works as a running gag.

> "Lame. Two points."
> "Seven, but only because of the hand gesture."

Use at most once per chapter, and always in a friendship with established rhythm (Kuze and Enomoto).

### 3.8 The genre-aware aside

The narrator identifies the trope in real time.

> Bread in her mouth, running late, turning a corner at speed. Kuze stepped aside with the calm of a man who had read the manual.
> Momo collided with the vending machine instead.

This gag has diminishing returns. Use it about once per chapter and vary the phrasing: "rom-com logic", "the manual", "the script", "whoever writes these things".

### 3.9 Physical comedy on the page

Slapstick needs prose timing:
- **Setup sentence** (position, momentum).
- **The impact as its own line**, often a sound effect: *Thwack.*
- **Aftermath line**, a reaction or deadpan.

> Tachibana spun to face Amamiya, forgot she was holding a bamboo sword, and completed the rotation.
> *Thwack.*
> "Why me?!"
> "You were in the way of my feelings!"

Keep the violence cartoonish and consequence-free. The tsundere smack is a convention, but if it happens every chapter it reads as cruelty. See [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md) for how to evolve it.

## 4. Misunderstanding engines

A misunderstanding engine is a premise that *generates* comic situations for many chapters. Pick one per arc.

| Engine | Setup | How it runs | How it pays off |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The fake relationship** | Two characters pretend to date to solve a problem | Every public scene is a performance, and every private scene reveals the performance is becoming real | One of them forgets to pretend, and nobody is watching |
| **The wrong recipient** | A love letter or text reaches the wrong person | The recipient responds, and the sender can't correct it without confessing | The right recipient finds out at the worst moment |
| **The overheard half-sentence** | "I really like... [bell rings]" | Everyone fills in the blank differently | The completed sentence is mundane ("...the new curry bread") |
| **The secret identity** | The class's cold beauty is secretly a popular streamer / manga artist / Kuze's online gaming partner | Near-reveals every chapter | The reveal comes by the character's own choice |
| **The mistaken rivalry** | A girl thinks Kuze is her love rival for Amamiya's attention | She challenges him to absurd contests | She realizes she liked the contests more than Amamiya |
| **The misread reputation** | Everyone thinks the quiet guy is a delinquent (or a genius, or dating the class rep) | He can't correct it without making it worse | Someone learns the truth and keeps his secret |
| **The shared secret** | Two characters witness something embarrassing about each other | Mutual blackmail becomes mutual trust | One of them spends their leverage to protect the other |

**Rules for engines:**
- The misunderstanding must be **easy to correct in theory and costly to correct in practice**. If one sentence could fix it with no cost, readers get frustrated. Always give the character a reason not to say that sentence: pride, protecting someone, or fear of the confession it would require.
- **Escalate cost, not repetition.** Each chapter, correcting the misunderstanding becomes more expensive.
- **Let the narrator know it's absurd.** Kuze can think, "One sentence would end this. One sentence, and I would also have to die." The reader forgives the delay because the narrator sees it.

## 5. Timing on the page

Comedy is timing, and prose timing is paragraphing.

### 5.1 The beat before the punchline

Put the punchline in its own paragraph. The white space before it is the comedian's pause.

**Flat:**
> Enomoto said he'd prepared a special present for Kuze's birthday, which turned out to be a single boiled egg.

**Timed:**
> Enomoto set it on Kuze's desk with both hands.
> "I prepared something special."
> Kuze looked at it.
> It was an egg.
> "Boiled."
> Enomoto said it with pride.

### 5.2 The silent reaction

`"..."` is the strongest comic beat in the voice. It is the look to camera.

> "Kuze-kun, you have to call me Hinacchi from now on."
> "..."
> "Say it~"
> "..."
> "Just once~?"
> "Matsuri-san."
> "Why'd it get *more* polite?!"

### 5.3 Don't explain the joke

Never follow a punchline with a sentence that explains it or describes the room laughing, unless the laughter itself is the next beat.

**Explaining (bad):**
> "It was an egg."
> Everyone laughed at the absurdity of giving someone a single egg as a birthday present.

**Trusting the reader (good):**
> It was an egg.

### 5.4 Cut on the laugh

End sections on the punchline or the reaction to it, not on a summary. The [section-ending rules](../structure/chapter-and-section.md) apply doubly in comedy.

## 6. Rom-com tropes: executing them well, and how they fail

| Trope | Done well | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Indirect kiss** (sharing a drink or chopsticks) | One character notices, the other doesn't, and the narrator's analysis spirals | Both notice and blush for a page, which kills the asymmetry |
| **Shared umbrella** | Physical closeness in a small space, dialogue about anything but the closeness, one shoulder getting wet | The wet shoulder is explained as a gesture of love. Let the reader notice. |
| **Homemade bento** | The lunch reveals knowledge (his favorite side dish), and the maker denies effort | It becomes a cooking-description scene |
| **Study session at her house** | A parent or sibling interrupts at the worst moment, and the room reveals a secret hobby | Nothing is interrupted, nothing is revealed |
| **Accidental fall into a compromising position** | Held long enough to be legible, with the panic real and the reaction doing the work; adult casts can let it continue | It has no reaction and no consequence, so the beat is furniture moving. See [Ecchi](ecchi-and-fanservice.md). |
| **Jealousy misread** | Character A is jealous, character B reads it as anger at something else | The narrator correctly reads it in narration, which kills the comedy |
| **The confession interrupted** | By a bell, a sneeze, a passing Gon, a gust of wind; the interrupted person is secretly relieved | It's interrupted five chapters in a row, so vary the method and raise the cost |
| **Name slip** | Someone accidentally uses a first name, then denies it | Nobody reacts. Name changes are plot. See [Honorifics](../dialogue/honorifics-and-address.md). |
| **Festival date that isn't a date** | Both insist it's not a date while doing every date activity | The narration calls it a date. Let the characters' denials do it. |
| **The rival's challenge** | Absurd contests (who can eat more curry bread) with genuine feeling underneath | The contest becomes serious sports writing |
| **Beach/pool episode** | Comedy of logistics (sunscreen politics, a lost sandal, a watermelon-splitting disaster); adult casts also get the swimsuits, the sunscreen hands, and the strap that comes loose | The scene becomes a body inventory with no joke and no relationship beat under it. See [Ecchi §6](ecchi-and-fanservice.md#6-the-classic-episodes). |

## 7. Scene recipes

### 7.1 The misread text message (800-1,200 words)

1. The POV receives a short, ambiguous message: "Are you free Saturday."
2. **Analysis spiral:** no question mark, so is it a statement? A demand? He cross-references her past texts.
3. He drafts replies in narration. Each is rejected with a reason.
4. He sends the flattest one: "Why."
5. Her reply comes: something deflating ("Need someone to carry groceries") or something unexpectedly sincere.
6. **Tilt:** a second message arrives three minutes later, which reframes the first.

### 7.2 The public misunderstanding (1,000-1,500 words)

1. A private exchange is overheard or photographed out of context.
2. **Group chatter** spreads it: 4-6 untagged lines, each more wrong.
3. The POV tries to correct it. Every correction makes it worse.
4. The other person involved *doesn't* correct it, which confuses him.
5. **Tilt:** she tells him privately why she didn't.

### 7.3 The contest (1,200-2,000 words)

1. The challenge is issued with maximum drama (a finger pointed, a declaration).
2. The rules are negotiated absurdly.
3. The contest runs in short-beat sports rhythm, parodied.
4. The narrator discovers he is trying, and resents it.
5. The result is a tie, a disqualification, or an interruption.
6. **Earnest beat:** after, one line about why the challenger really wanted to spend time with him.

### 7.4 The escalating lie (1,500 words)

1. A small lie is told to avoid embarrassment ("I have a girlfriend").
2. Someone asks for proof.
3. A friend improvises, badly.
4. The lie requires a performance: a phone call, a photo, a meeting.
5. Collapse, with the truth coming out in the most public way possible.
6. **Tilt:** the person who "played the girlfriend" asks, quietly, whether the performance was *that* bad.

## 8. Chapter and arc shapes

**Rom-com chapter (3,000-4,000 words):**
1. **Cold open gag** (a sound effect, an absurd line, a callback).
2. **Setup:** the engine turns and a new complication arrives.
3. **Escalation sections,** two or three of them, each ending on a laugh.
4. **The quiet section:** smaller, slower, and one sincere line.
5. **Hook:** a comedic cliffhanger ("Also, my mother is coming to the festival.") or a romantic one (a name said for the first time).

**Rom-com arc (8-15 chapters):**
1. The engine starts (fake dating, a wrong letter).
2. Comic chapters, with the engine running.
3. The midpoint: the performance becomes partly real.
4. A third party threatens it (a rival, a parent, the truth).
5. The crisis comes, usually a *misread* of a sincere moment.
6. The resolution is a confession, or a deliberate *choice* to continue the misunderstanding because both now want it.
7. Coda: a callback gag proving the relationship changed.

## 9. Naming and setting conventions

- **Chapter titles** are playful, question-form, or deadpan: "Do You Like Boiled Eggs? 1", "The Umbrella Only Covered One and a Half People", "On the Correct Number of Eggs".
- **Nicknames are comedy fuel:** "Hinacchi", "Gon-dono", "Mina". The refusal to use one is a running gag.
- **Parody brands:** convenience stores (FamilyMarket, Lawsoon), apps (Chirp), idols (a boy band called "SHINE☆BOYZ" that the girls argue about).
- **Settings:** clubroom, rooftop, the vending machine by the gym, the family restaurant with the drink bar, the station's third platform. Each should recur so gags can grow roots.

## 10. Sample passages

### 10.1 Sample: the barley tea (tsukkomi in narration, misread signal)

> 1
>
> Tachibana dropped the can on his desk as if it had insulted her.
> "Here."
> Kuze looked at it. Barley tea. Room temperature, which meant it came from the vending machine by the gym, the one whose refrigeration had died sometime last autumn and which nobody had reported because nobody wanted to be the person who reported a vending machine.
> "What's this for?"
> "For the notes. Yesterday. Obviously."
> She crossed her arms.
> "I don't like owing people."
> Kuze turned the can. Barley tea was the cheapest button, eighty yen. The milk tea she drank every day was a hundred and thirty.
> Fifty yen. She had valued yesterday's notes at exactly fifty yen less than her own daily comfort.
> A precise message. Almost admirable.
> "Understood. We're even."
> "Huh? W-What's with that face? Why do you look like you just did math?"
> "I did math."
> "Don't do math on a drink!"
> "It's a drink with a price tag."
> Tachibana slammed both hands on his desk, and the can wobbled.
> "It's not about the price! The milk tea was sold out! That's all! That was the only button that was lit!"
> "..."
> Kuze considered this. The machine by the gym had six buttons, and only one lit meant five sold out. The machine was restocked on Mondays. It was Thursday.
> Also possible, then.
> "Thanks."
> Tachibana made a sound like a kettle and walked away very fast.
> From three rows over, Enomoto gave him a slow thumbs-up. Kuze did not know what it meant, and suspected he did not want to.

### 10.2 Sample: the fake-date performance (misunderstanding engine, escalation)

> Outside the station, Hina bounced on her heels.
> "So we're agreed. Until my cousin goes back to Osaka, you're my boyfriend. Totally fake. Super fake. Fake to the max, yup?"
> "Why me?"
> "Because you're the only boy I know who wouldn't make it weird!"
> Kuze accepted this as the insult it was.
> The cousin arrived at eleven. She was a first-year at a university in Osaka, had Hina's big eyes and none of her restraint, and looked Kuze up and down the way a customs officer looks at an unlabeled suitcase.
> "So you're the boyfriend."
> "Apparently."
> Hina's elbow found his ribs.
> "I mean, yes. I am. The boyfriend."
> He paused.
> "Hello."
> "What do you like about Hina?"
> The question landed like a pop quiz on a subject he had not studied. Kuze opened his mouth. Closed it. Beside him, Hina had gone perfectly still, the smile on her face fixed like a photograph of a smile.
> He could say *her cheerfulness*. Everyone said that about Hina. The school said it, the teachers said it, and it was printed practically on her forehead.
> But the cousin was waiting, and Hina was not breathing, and the first thing that came to mind wasn't cheerfulness at all.
> "She remembers what everyone drinks. At the family restaurant. She goes to the drink bar and comes back with everyone's refill without asking. Melon soda for Enomoto, oolong for Gon. She got mine wrong the first time and never again."
> "..."
> "..."
> The cousin looked at Hina. Hina looked at the pavement.
> "Huh. That's oddly specific for a fake boyfriend."
> Hina said it much too loudly.
> "It's not fake!"
> "I mean, obviously not fake, why would it be fake, what a weird thing to say, ahaha~"
> Kuze looked at the sky and wondered at which point in the last ten seconds he had lost control of the plan.

### 10.3 Sample: the group-chatter spiral (public misunderstanding)

> The rumor reached Class 2-3 before Kuze did.
> "Did you hear? Kuze and Kujou-san were alone in the library."
> "For an hour!"
> "I heard two hours."
> "I heard she was crying."
> "I heard *he* was crying."
> Gon arrived from nowhere.
> "Verily, I heard they were reshelving the Dewey decimal three-hundreds."
> The class turned to look at him with open disappointment.
> Kuze sat down. On his desk, someone had left a sticky note that said *congrats (lol)*. He peeled it off, folded it into eighths, and put it in his pocket, because throwing it away in front of everyone would be read as a statement, and keeping it would be read as a different statement, and a pocket was the only neutral territory left.
> Across the room, Kujou was sitting at Amamiya's side as usual, cool and upright. When their eyes met, she raised one eyebrow by a millimeter, which Kuze was fairly sure meant *you're welcome*.
> For what, he had no idea.

## 11. Running gags: lifecycle management

A running gag is a living thing. It is born, it grows, it peaks, and it should die or transform on purpose.

| Stage | What happens | Example (Hina's nickname demand) |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth** | Introduced casually, once | "Call me Hinacchi!" / "Matsuri-san." |
| **Repetition** | Returns 2-3 times with small variations | She tries a new angle each time: bribery, trickery, a formal written request |
| **Escalation** | The stakes of the gag rise | She makes it a condition of lending him her notes before exams |
| **Subversion** | The expected beat doesn't come | One day she doesn't ask. He notices the absence. |
| **Payoff** | The gag carries real emotion | At her lowest moment, he says "Hinacchi" once, without being asked |
| **Retirement** | The gag ends, or becomes a new normal | Afterward he goes back to "Matsuri", and she lets him, because she knows he can say it |

**Rules:**
- Track every running gag in the story bible with its current stage.
- **No gag more than once per chapter** in the repetition stage.
- **The payoff is almost always sincere.** A running gag's highest use is to carry an emotion that would be too embarrassing to state directly. That is the rom-com's secret weapon.
- Retire gags that have stopped growing. A gag at the same stage for twenty chapters is a tic, not a joke.

## 12. Comic voices by archetype

Different archetypes generate comedy differently. Assign each character a comic *function*, not just a personality.

| Archetype | Comic function | Typical move |
|---|---|---|
| Cynical POV | Tsukkomi, underreaction, analysis of trivia | "Correct, and irrelevant." |
| Tsundere | Overreaction, denial escalation | "I-I miscounted the eggs!" |
| Genki | Energy as absurdity, boundary-blind enthusiasm | Standing on desks, inventing nicknames |
| Ojou / kuudere | Deadpan cruelty, precise vocabulary | "My. They're charcoal." |
| Airhead | Literal interpretation, non sequiturs | Answering a rhetorical question sincerely |
| Otaku friend | Grandiosity, archaic speech about trivial things | "Verily, the bread lottery is a war." |
| Childhood friend | Knowing mockery, bad jokes, the scoreboard | "Lame. Two points." |
| Cool teacher | Adult irresponsibility, surprising competence | "Teehee." Then solving everything in one line. |
| Harem lead | Oblivious straight answers to loaded questions | "Huh? Why would Sayo be mad? She said she was fine!" |

See [Character Voices](../dialogue/character-voices.md) for full line banks and [Dere Types](../cast/dere-types.md) for how comedy evolves as characters warm up.

**Pair functions for scenes.** Tsukkomi plus boke is the base. A deadpan kuudere plus a genki girl is a double act. Two tsukkomi in a row is a drought, so add a boke. Two boke with no tsukkomi is chaos, which is good for exactly one scene per arc.

## 13. Common rom-com failure modes

- **The sitcom loop.** Every chapter resets to the status quo. The fix: end each chapter with one irreversible change, however small (a name used, a secret learned, a number exchanged).
- **The joke machine.** Gags arrive on schedule, but nobody wants anything. The fix: give each scene a want. Hina wants the nickname, Tachibana wants to repay a debt without admitting she cares, and Kuze wants to be left alone and secretly doesn't.
- **The mean streak.** The narrator's tsukkomi turns contemptuous, and readers stop liking him. The fix: aim the harshest jokes at himself at least as often as at others, and let him lose exchanges.
- **The explained blush.** "She blushed because she liked him." Delete it every time. The reader already knows, and the narrator is funnier not knowing.
- **Too many engines.** A fake relationship *and* a wrong letter *and* a secret identity in one arc. Pick one and let the others wait for later arcs.

## 14. Rom-com revision checklist

1. **Laugh density:** is there a comic beat at least every 300-400 words? Mark them. Fill any dry stretch.
2. **Punchlines isolated:** does every punchline sit at the end of a paragraph or on its own line?
3. **No explained jokes:** delete any sentence after a punchline that explains why it was funny.
4. **Tsukkomi brevity:** is every straight-man reply shorter than the line it answers?
5. **Misunderstanding cost:** is there a clear reason the confused character can't just say the correcting sentence?
6. **Narrator stays a little wrong:** in misread-signal scenes, does the truth arrive through dialogue or a reaction, not through the narrator's sudden insight?
7. **One sincere line:** does the chapter have exactly one moment where someone means it without a joke? (Two is fine at an arc midpoint.)
8. **Physical comedy consequence-free** and not repetitive: is the same smack used no more than once per chapter?
9. **Trope awareness:** is each trope either commented on by the narrator or subverted?
10. **Callback ledger updated:** any new plant recorded in the story bible?
11. **Content rule:** adults are unrestricted; characters who are minors get no sexual rendering at all. See [Ecchi §1](ecchi-and-fanservice.md#1-the-content-line).
12. Run [Anti-Robotic](../revision/anti-robotic.md): no tricolon stacking, no "a comedic misunderstanding ensued", no "couldn't help but laugh".
